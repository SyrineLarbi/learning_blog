A data analyst bootcamp · concepts, not code

Nobody hands you clean data. Making it trustworthy is the job.

Most analyst training teaches you to query data that has already been prepared. Then you arrive at a real company and find four tables called orders, a date column containing 1 January 1970, and a number in a board deck that nobody can reproduce. This course is about that gap — how to tell whether data can be trusted, how to make it so, and how to reason with it once it is.

How to use it

  1. If you have limited time, read Part II first. Modules 4 and 5 — detecting and cleaning dirty data — are the highest-value hours in the course and they depend on very little. Skim Module 1's section on grain first, then go straight there.
  2. Otherwise, read in order. The modules genuinely depend on each other: grain before dimensional modelling, dimensional modelling before pipelines, and the bias catalogue makes much more sense after you have seen how data gets damaged.
  3. Do the exercises before opening the solution. Write your answer down. The gap between yours and the solution is the actual lesson, and it disappears if you read the answer first — you will believe you knew it.
  4. Come back to the cheat sheets. Each part ends with one. They are written to be used while you are actually working, not while you are studying.
Why there is almost no code in here

Because the code is the easy part and it is already written down in a hundred places. What is not written down is which query to write, whether the answer can be trusted, and what the number means. A handful of three-line snippets appear where an example is genuinely clearer than a paragraph — but this is a course about judgement, and judgement is what survives when the tools change.

The one idea underneath everything

Data does not describe reality. It describes what somebody recorded about reality, using a system built for a different purpose, at a moment that has passed. Every technique in this course is a way of narrowing the gap between those two things, or of being honest about how wide it still is. Analysts who forget this produce confident numbers about a world that does not exist.

What data actually is

Module 1

Data literacy

Four ideas that decide whether your arithmetic is legitimate: what kind of value you have, what one row means, how rows relate, and which numbers may be added up.

Measurement scales — and the arithmetic each one permits

Not pedantry. The scale of a value determines which operations are meaningful, and the commonest breach of this rule is committed daily in every company on earth.

ScaleWhat it isExamplesYou may · you may not
Nominal Labels with no order. Country, channel, product category, status Count, mode, proportion. Not order, mean, or anything arithmetic — even if it is stored as a number. A "region code" of 3 is not more than a 2.
Ordinal Ordered, but the gaps between values are not equal or known. Satisfaction 1–5, T-shirt sizes, seniority level, risk band low/medium/high Count, mode, median, percentiles, order. Not the mean — see below, because this is the one everybody breaks.
Interval Equal gaps, but no meaningful zero. Temperature in °C, calendar dates, IQ Add, subtract, mean, standard deviation. Not ratios — 20°C is not "twice as warm" as 10°C, and 2026 is not twice 1013.
Ratio Equal gaps and a true zero. Revenue, weight, duration, count, age Everything, including ratios and percentage change. Most business measures are ratio scale, which is why the rule feels invisible until you hit an ordinal column.
Trap · the average satisfaction score

"Average NPS category", "mean satisfaction 3.7 out of 5", "average risk level 2.4". These are everywhere and they are all the same error: averaging an ordinal scale assumes the gap between 1 and 2 equals the gap between 4 and 5, and it does not. The distance from "very dissatisfied" to "dissatisfied" is not the distance from "satisfied" to "very satisfied" — and respondents do not treat them as equal either.

Why it actually matters: a mean of 3.7 can describe a population where everyone is mildly content, or one that is violently split between 1s and 5s. Those are opposite business situations with the same average, and the average is what gets reported.

The fix: report the distribution — the percentage in each category, or the share at the top two boxes ("62% satisfied or very satisfied"), which is what NPS-style metrics do and is the reason they do it. Use the median if you need one number. If you must average because a stakeholder insists, put the distribution next to it.

Grain — the most important word in this course

The grain of a table is what one row means. Not what the table is called, not what columns it has — what a single row is. Almost every serious analytical error traces back to a wrong belief about grain.

Grain decides what is answerable — not what is fast
  ONE ROW PER…            can answer                cannot answer
  ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
  employee                headcount today           headcount last March
  (current state only)    current org chart         who moved teams
                          average tenure NOW        joiner/leaver rates

  employee × month        both of the above         the exact date someone
  (snapshot)              headcount at any month    changed team
                          cohort retention          two changes in one month

  employment event        all of the above,         — (nothing, but every
  (hire/move/leave)       to the day                query is now harder
                                                    and needs care)

  THE POINT: the top table is not SLOW at answering "headcount last
  March". It is INCAPABLE. No index helps, no query is clever enough,
  no amount of skill recovers it. The information was never written down.

  ⭐ SO: GRAIN IS A PRODUCT DECISION WEARING A SCHEMA. It is chosen
  before anything else and it determines the questions the business
  will ever be able to ask. Choosing it is design work, not plumbing.

  AND THE COROLLARY — store the fact, derive the rest:
    hire_date        is a FACT.        store it.
    tenure_months    is a VIEW of it.  compute it.

Best practice · the grain test, in the first five minutes

Never trust a table name. Group by the columns you believe are the key, count, and keep anything appearing more than once. It must return nothing. If it returns rows, your understanding of the table is wrong and everything you build on it inherits the error.

Then write the grain down in one sentence — "one row per order line per shipment" — because that sentence is what you check every join against.

Keys, cardinality and the join that multiplies money

TermWhat it means, and why you care
Primary keyThe column(s) that uniquely identify a row. If it is not actually unique, it is not a key, whatever the schema says.
Natural keyA real-world identifier — an order number, an email, a product code. Meaningful, and it can change, which is the problem with using it as a join key.
Surrogate keyA meaningless generated id, stable forever. Analytics uses these because natural keys change and because a type-2 dimension needs one key per version, not per entity.
Composite keySeveral columns together. Common in snapshots: (employee_id, month). Your grain statement usually is the composite key.
Foreign keyA pointer to another table's key. When the target is missing you have an orphan — Module 4.
CardinalityHow many rows on each side of a relationship: one-to-one, one-to-many, many-to-many. This is what decides whether a join is safe.
The error that produces plausible wrong numbers

Fan-out. Join a one-row-per-order table to a one-row-per-shipment table and sum revenue. Every order with two shipments now contributes its revenue twice. The result does not error, does not look strange, and is simply 30% too high.

Worse: the inflation is not uniform. It concentrates wherever there are more child rows — which is often exactly the segment you are investigating. So the relative picture is corrupted, not just the total, and that is far harder to notice than a uniform error.

The habit that prevents it, forever: join, then count, then sum. After every join, check the row count and a money total against their pre-join values. If either changed and you did not intend it, stop. And aggregate the many-side to the grain of the one-side before joining, not after.

Facts, dimensions and measures

Facts — what happened

Events and their measurements. Long, thin, always growing: orders, page views, payments, shipments. The numeric columns are the measures.

Dimensions — the context

The nouns you slice by: customer, product, date, region, employee. Wide, comparatively short, descriptive. These are what turn a number into an answer.

Measures — the numbers

What you aggregate. Revenue, quantity, duration, count. The critical question about any measure is how it may be aggregated — see below.

The mental test

"Revenue by region by month." Whatever comes before "by" is a measure; whatever follows is a dimension. Almost every business question has this shape, which is why dimensional modelling works.

Additive, semi-additive, non-additive — which numbers may be summed

An under-taught distinction that causes confident, wrong totals. Not every number can be added up, and BI tools will happily add all of them.

TypeMeaningExamples · and what happens if you get it wrong
Additive Summable across every dimension, including time. Revenue, quantity, cost, order count. The easy case, and the one people assume for everything.
Semi-additive Summable across some dimensions but not across time. Any balance or level: stock on hand, headcount, account balance, open tickets, subscribers. You can sum stock across warehouses; summing January's, February's and March's closing stock gives a number that means nothing — it is not "Q1 stock". For time you take the last value in the period, or an average, and you must decide which. This is the one BI tools get wrong by default, because their default aggregation is SUM.
Non-additive Cannot be summed across any dimension. Every ratio, rate, percentage and average. Margin %, conversion rate, average order value. Adding three months' margin percentages is meaningless; so is averaging them.
Best practice · store the components, compute the ratio at read time

For any non-additive measure: store the numerator and the denominator separately, and divide only at the point of display. SUM(profit) / SUM(revenue) — never AVG(margin_pct).

The reason is that averaging a percentage weights a tiny category equally with an enormous one. A group whose true margin is 16.8% can show an average-of-category-margins of 24.1% — a seven-point error that looks entirely plausible on a slide and that nobody catches by eye. The same bug hides inside pivot tables, inside BI measures written as an aggregation over a calculated column, and inside AVG(rate) in SQL.

Module 2

Dimensional modelling

Why analytical schemas look deliberately "wrong" to anyone trained on application databases — and the pattern for keeping history, which is the thing most models get wrong.

Star versus snowflake

The same data, two shapes
STAR — one fact, dimensions one join away. Denormalised on purpose.

            dim_date        dim_customer
                 \             /
                  \           /
   dim_product ── fct_order_line ── dim_region
                  /           \
                 /             \
         dim_channel        dim_employee

  every dimension is a single flat table. dim_product holds category,
  brand, supplier and manufacturer as columns — repeated, redundant,
  and exactly what you want here.

SNOWFLAKE — dimensions normalised into their own hierarchies.

   dim_product ── dim_category ── dim_department
        │
        └──────── dim_supplier ── dim_supplier_country

  less redundancy, more joins, more places to get a join wrong.

WHY ANALYTICS PREFERS THE STAR — and it is not laziness:
  · fewer joins → simpler queries → fewer chances to be wrong
  · a dimension is small; storing "Analgesics" a million times costs
    almost nothing on columnar storage, which compresses repetition
  · the query optimiser handles the star pattern extremely well
  · and the real reason: A HUMAN CAN UNDERSTAND IT. An analyst who
    can see the whole model makes fewer mistakes than one navigating
    a normalised hierarchy.

  Normalisation exists to prevent UPDATE ANOMALIES. Analytical tables
  aren't updated by users — they're rebuilt by a pipeline. So the
  problem normalisation solves does not exist here, and you are free
  to optimise for comprehension instead.

Slowly changing dimensions

The question a dimension has to answer: when an attribute changes, do we need to know what it used to be? Everything follows from that.

TypeWhat it doesUse when · and the cost
Type 0 Never changes. Write once. Date of birth, original signup date. Anything genuinely immutable.
Type 1 Overwrite. The old value is gone. When history has no analytical value — a corrected typo, an email address. Cost: every historical report changes retroactively. Last quarter's report by department no longer reproduces, because people's departments have been rewritten. That is the trap: type 1 is not "simple", it is silently rewriting the past.
Type 2 New row per version, with valid_from, valid_to, is_current and a surrogate key per version. The default when history matters — department, manager, grade, price, customer segment. Facts join to the version that was current at the time of the event, so a historical report reproduces forever. Cost: the table grows, and every query must decide "as at the time" versus "as classified now". Module 8 has the nightly procedure.
Type 3 A previous_value column alongside the current one. Rare and limited — it holds exactly one step of history. Occasionally right for a one-off reorganisation where people want to compare old and new structure side by side. Do not reach for it as a lightweight type 2; it is not one.
The question that decides the join, and it has two right answers

With a type 2 dimension, every query must choose:

"As it was at the time" — join on the event date falling between valid_from and valid_to. Use this for anything historical: last year's revenue by department means the department people were actually in.

"As classified now" — join on is_current. Use this to compare today's organisation against history on a consistent basis: "how has my current team performed over three years", even for periods when the team was structured differently.

Both are correct and they give different numbers. The error is not choosing — most commonly by joining on is_current because it is simpler, without realising a choice was made. State which one a report uses. This is a frequent cause of two dashboards disagreeing while both queries are correct.

The dimension types with unhelpful names

Conformed dimension

One shared dimension used by several fact tables — the same dim_date and dim_product for orders, shipments and returns. This is what makes facts comparable: "orders and returns by product category" only works if both use the same category definition. Conforming dimensions is most of the work of building a coherent warehouse.

Degenerate dimension

A dimension attribute with no dimension table — an order number sitting on the fact row. It is a legitimate pattern, not an oversight: you want it for grouping and drill-through, and there are no other attributes to put in a table.

Junk dimension

A single table collecting several low-cardinality flags — is_gift, is_expedited, payment_type — that would otherwise be five columns on the fact or five tiny tables. Keeps the fact table narrow.

Role-playing dimension

One dimension used several times in different roles. dim_date joined as order date, ship date and delivery date. Expose it as three named views so a query reads ship_date.month, not dim_date_2.month.

Bridge table

For genuine many-to-many: a patient with several diagnoses, a product in several categories. A bridge table between fact and dimension. Beware: it re-introduces fan-out, so summing a measure across a bridge double-counts unless you carry an allocation weight.

Date dimension — build one

Not optional. A table with one row per day carrying fiscal period, week number, holiday flag, weekday, and "days from period start". It is what makes fiscal calendars, "same week last year" and business-day counts possible at all. Every warehouse needs one and it takes an hour.

The semantic layer

The last mile, and the answer to a problem you will certainly meet: three tools each computing "revenue" slightly differently, so three dashboards disagree and all three queries are correct.

A semantic layer is one definition of each metric and dimension, stored once, consumed by every tool. Instead of each report writing its own aggregation, they request revenue by month and the layer emits the query. It can be a dedicated product, your BI tool's model, or — perfectly respectably at small scale — a documented set of certified views that everybody is required to use.

What it buys you

One place where a definition changes, so a fix propagates everywhere. Metrics that are governed rather than reinvented per report. And an answer to "which number is right" that is not an argument.

What it costs

A layer to maintain, and a genuine constraint on analysts who want to write arbitrary SQL. The honest split: certified metrics go through the layer; exploration bypasses it. Forcing exploration through a governed layer kills the exploration.

Module 3

Where data lives

Why there is a separate copy of the data for analysis at all, and why the database that runs the business is the wrong place to query it.

OLTP versus OLAP

OLTP — the application databaseOLAP — the analytical store
Optimised forMany tiny operations. "Insert this order." "Fetch customer 4127."Few enormous ones. "Sum revenue by category across three years."
Typical queryTouches a handful of rows, all columns of those rows.Touches millions of rows, three columns of them.
StorageRow-oriented — a whole row stored together, so fetching one row is one read.Column-oriented — each column stored together, so reading three columns of a hundred never touches the other 97.
SchemaNormalised, to prevent update anomalies.Denormalised, for comprehension and fewer joins.
PriorityWrite throughput and low latency. The business stops if it is slow.Scan throughput. A query taking eight seconds is fine.
Why this matters to you practically

Never run analytical queries against the production application database. A full-table scan that is routine in a warehouse will hold locks, exhaust the connection pool, evict the working set from cache, and slow or stop the application. Analysts have taken down production this way, and it is a memorable way to lose trust.

If a read replica is the only option, use it and keep the queries bounded. But the reason the analytical copy exists is exactly this: the two workloads are hostile to each other.

Why columnar storage changes the arithmetic
The query:  SELECT category, SUM(revenue) FROM orders GROUP BY category
The table:  100 columns, 50 million rows

ROW STORE — rows are contiguous on disk
  ┌────────────────────────────────────────────┐
  │ id │ date │ cust │ … 95 more … │ cat │ rev │  ← row 1
  │ id │ date │ cust │ … 95 more … │ cat │ rev │  ← row 2
  └────────────────────────────────────────────┘
  to get `cat` and `rev` it must read EVERY ROW ENTIRELY.
  → reads ~100 columns × 50M rows. 98% of the I/O is wasted.

COLUMN STORE — columns are contiguous on disk
  ┌──────────────┐ ┌──────────────┐     ┌──────────────┐
  │ id id id id… │ │ date date …  │  …  │ cat cat cat… │
  └──────────────┘ └──────────────┘     └──────────────┘
  reads ONLY the `cat` and `rev` blocks.
  → reads 2 columns × 50M rows. ~50× less I/O.

  AND compression works far better, because a column holds values of
  one type with heavy repetition — "Analgesics" a million times
  compresses to almost nothing. Less data on disk means less to read.

  THE FLIP SIDE: fetching one complete row means touching 100
  separate places. Which is why OLTP is not columnar, and why the two
  storage models are not interchangeable.

Warehouse, lake, lakehouse

What it isGood for · weak at
Warehouse Structured, schema-on-write, SQL-native. Snowflake, BigQuery, Redshift. Reliable, governed, fast SQL; the right default for analytics. Weak at unstructured data and very large raw volumes.
Lake Files in object storage, schema-on-read. Parquet on S3. Cheap at any volume, any format, no schema needed up front. Weak at governance and consistency — and it degenerates into a "data swamp" without a catalog, because nobody knows what any file is.
Lakehouse Lake storage plus a table format giving transactions, schema enforcement and time travel. Delta Lake, Iceberg. Lake economics with warehouse guarantees — and time travel, which means you can query the table as it was last Tuesday. That is genuinely useful for debugging a changed number.
Partitioning and clustering, in one paragraph

Partitioning physically splits a table by a column — usually date — so a query filtered to one month reads one month's files instead of all of them. This is why WHERE order_date >= … can be a thousand times faster than a filter on an unpartitioned column, and why the idempotent "delete-insert by partition" pattern from Module 6 is cheap. Clustering (or sorting) orders data within partitions so that filters on a second column can also skip blocks.

Two practical consequences. Filter on the partition column whenever you can, because it is the difference between reading a gigabyte and reading a terabyte — and on metered warehouses that is literally your bill. And avoid wrapping the partition column in a function (WHERE DATE(order_ts) = …), which usually defeats partition pruning entirely and turns a fast query into a full scan.

Exercise 3.1 · 20 minutes

You are designing a table to support these four questions. State the grain, and say what happens if you get it wrong.

  1. "How many people work here today?"
  2. "How many worked here in March 2024?"
  3. "Do people who join in Q1 stay longer than those joining in Q3?"
  4. "How many people changed department last year?"
Show the solution

The four questions need different things, and the mistake is to design for question 1 — because it is the one that gets asked first and it is satisfied by the simplest possible table.

What each question needs

QuestionNeeds
1 · headcount todayCurrent state only. One row per employee is enough.
2 · headcount in March 2024Point-in-time state. Needs either monthly snapshots or hire/leave dates.
3 · Q1 vs Q3 cohort tenureHire date and leave date as facts, plus the ability to handle people who have not left yet.
4 · department changes last yearAttribute history — a type 2 dimension or an event log. Cannot be derived from snapshots alone if someone changed twice within a period.

The design

Two tables, and neither alone is sufficient:

  • dim_employee, grain: one row per employee per version (type 2, with valid_from/valid_to/is_current). Holds hire_date and leave_date as immutable facts, and tracks department, manager and grade as versioned attributes. This answers 1 (filter is_current), 3 (from the date facts) and 4 (count version transitions where department differs).
  • fct_employee_month, grain: one row per employee per month — a snapshot. Strictly derivable from the type 2 dimension, but built anyway because it makes question 2 and any monthly trend a simple filter instead of a range join. This is a legitimate, deliberate denormalisation.

What goes wrong if you get it wrong

  1. Only current state: questions 2, 3 and 4 are impossible, not slow. And you will not find out until someone asks — probably at a board meeting.
  2. Storing tenure_months instead of hire_date: question 3 dies. Tenure is a derived value that is wrong tomorrow and destroys the underlying fact.
  3. Type 1 instead of type 2 on department: question 4 dies, and worse — every historical report by department silently rewrites itself as people move. Last year's report no longer reproduces, and nobody knows why.
  4. Only monthly snapshots, no event history: question 4 is almost answerable, but you will undercount anyone who changed department twice inside one month, and you cannot state the date of a change. Whether that matters is a business question — ask it rather than assuming.
  5. Missing the point-in-time subtlety: "headcount in March" needs a definition before it needs a schema. Does someone who joined on 15 March and left on 28 March count? A snapshot at month-end says no; an event log lets you answer either way. The schema decides which definitions are available to you.

The lesson: write the questions down before the tables — and include the questions nobody has asked yet, because grain is the one decision that is expensive to change later and impossible to fake.

Part II · the centre of the course

Dirty data

Two modules. The first is how to find out that data cannot be trusted — a skill, with a procedure, that most analysts never learn and instead acquire by being embarrassed. The second is every technique for repairing it.

Module 4 · read this first if you read nothing else

How to know the data is dirty

Dirty data almost never announces itself. It produces numbers that are plausible, specific and wrong — which is the most dangerous kind, because nobody questions them.

Here is the thing that makes this hard. Data that is obviously broken is harmless: a query that errors, a column that is entirely null, a date in the year 4521. You notice, you fix it, you move on. The data that costs you your credibility is data that looks fine. A revenue figure that is 8% too low because one order status was excluded. An average that is dragged by 400 test orders worth one cent each. A retention curve that looks like a collapse because the newest cohort has only existed for three weeks.

So "is this data dirty?" cannot be answered by looking at it. It has to be interrogated, with a procedure, before you build anything on top of it. This module is that procedure.

The mindset shift

Treat every dataset as guilty until proven innocent, and treat the proving as billable work rather than an obstacle before the real work. On a two-week analysis, one to three days of profiling and reconciliation is not overhead — it is the part that determines whether the other eight days produce a finding or a fiction.

The seven dimensions of data quality

Ask these seven questions of any dataset and you will have found most of what is wrong with it. They are worth memorising because they are exhaustive in a useful way — nearly every data defect is a failure of one of them.

DimensionThe questionHow it fails in practice
Completeness Is everything here that should be here? Missing rows (a failed load, a source that only sends changes), missing values within rows, and the hardest one — entities that never got recorded at all, which you cannot see by looking at the data.
Validity Does every value conform to its rules? A negative quantity, an email with no @, a country code that is not a country, a date before the company existed. Checkable without leaving the database.
Accuracy Does the value match the real world? An address that is valid and wrong. A weight of 2kg on a product that is 20kg. Not checkable from inside the data — it needs an external reference, a physical count, or a person who knows. This is the dimension people assume they have checked when they have only checked validity.
Consistency Do the copies agree? The CRM says the customer is in Germany, billing says France. Order total does not equal the sum of its lines. Two reports on the same metric differ.
Uniqueness Is each real thing represented once? Exact duplicate rows, and the worse case — the same customer as four records with different spellings, which no primary key prevents.
Timeliness Is it current enough for the decision? A dashboard built on a table that stopped refreshing eleven days ago, and nothing said so. Timeliness failures are invisible: the data looks normal, it is just old.
Integrity Do the relationships hold? Orders pointing at products that no longer exist. A child row with no parent. Orphans, which an inner join silently deletes along with their money.
The distinction that matters most

Valid is not accurate. You can prove validity from inside the database; you can never prove accuracy from inside it. Every check in this module tests validity, consistency, completeness, uniqueness or integrity. Accuracy requires leaving the data — reconciling to a general ledger, comparing to a physical count, asking the person who entered it. That is why the last section of this module is about reconciliation, and why it is the only check that ever settles an argument.

The profiling battery

Run this on every table before you use it. It is mechanical, it takes about twenty minutes per table, and it finds something every single time. The order matters: it is arranged by cost-to-run against likelihood-of-finding, so you stop early when you find something disqualifying.

The battery · run top to bottom, stop when something disqualifies the table
─ LEVEL 1 · TABLE SHAPE ─────────────────────── seconds, always first
  1  row count, and row count OVER TIME (by month, by day)
     → look for: step changes, gaps, a cliff at the end, a duplicated
       period, a slow ramp (a system being adopted)
  2  the date range: min and max of every date column
     → look for: dates before the company existed, dates in the future,
       and the sentinel dates in the catalogue below

     keep anything with a count above one

       one row means is wrong, and everything downstream is wrong with it.

─ LEVEL 2 · COLUMN BY COLUMN ────────────────── minutes, high yield
  4  null rate per column — BY ROW COUNT AND BY VALUE (see below)

     → THIS IS THE HIGHEST-YIELD SINGLE CHECK IN THE BATTERY. It exposes

  7  min / max / percentiles of every numeric column

     status consistent with timestamps present

Best practice · always quantify twice

Every finding gets measured two ways: by row count and by value. "3% of rows have no cost" and "18% of revenue has no cost" are the same defect described twice, and they lead to opposite decisions. The reason they diverge is not coincidence — the broken rows are systematically the unusual, large, interesting ones, because unusual records are the ones that take unusual paths through a system.

"97% of rows are fine" has ended more careers than "we are missing 18% of revenue", because the first sounds like an all-clear.

The disguised-null catalogue

This is the single most useful list in the module. A null is easy to find. What is hard to find is a value that means "unknown" but is stored as something else — because it passes every completeness check, participates in every average, and silently corrupts every calculation it touches. These are called sentinel values, and they exist because at some point a system needed to write something into a non-nullable column.

0 in a numeric column
What it means
Usually "not recorded", not "zero". A price of 0, a weight of 0, a salary of 0, a duration of 0 — almost always a missing value that had to be written as a number.
How to confirm
Count the zeros and compare against the null count. If a column has zero nulls and 4% zeros, the zeros are the nulls. Then check whether a genuine zero is even possible in the business — a free product might be; a free shipment weight is not.
What to do
Treat as null explicitly and state the impact. This one matters enormously for averages: 400 zero-price orders drag an average order value down without changing the row count, so the number moves and nothing looks wrong.
1970-01-01 — and its neighbours 1969-12-31 and 1970-01-02
What it means
The Unix epoch is timestamp zero. So this date means somebody cast a null or a zero into a date — almost certainly in a pipeline, not in the source system. It is the single most diagnostic value in this catalogue, because it tells you not only that data is missing but roughly where it was damaged.
How to confirm
Look for a cluster of rows on exactly that date. And check the neighbours: a timezone conversion applied to the epoch produces 1969-12-31 in the Americas and 1970-01-01 in Europe, which is a useful tell that a conversion happened.
What to do
Null it, and then go and find the transformation that created it — because whatever produced it is still running tonight. This is a defect in the pipeline, not in the data.
1900-01-01, 1899-12-30, 9999-12-31
What it means
1900-01-01 and 1899-12-30 are the zero points of Excel and older SQL Server date types — so these mean the same thing as the epoch, arriving through a different door (usually a spreadsheet). 9999-12-31 is deliberate and different: it is the conventional "no end date yet" marker, particularly in slowly changing dimensions, and treating it as a real date puts contracts a thousand years in the future into your averages.
How to confirm
Check the max of every date column. If the max is 9999-12-31, you have found a convention, not an error.
What to do
For the epoch-family values: null them. For 9999-12-31: leave it alone but never include it in a duration calculation — "is this record still current?" is a boolean question, and computing a length from an open-ended date is how you get an average contract length of 4,000 years.
'NULL', 'null', 'None', 'nan' — as text
What it means
A null that passed through a text export and came back as a four-character string. Extremely common with CSV round-trips, spreadsheet exports, and anything that has been through pandas.
How to confirm
The top-25 values check finds these instantly. Also test the null rate: a column with zero nulls in data you would expect to be patchy is itself the signal.
What to do
Convert to real nulls at the earliest layer — this belongs in staging, not in your analysis query, so it is fixed once for everyone rather than by whoever remembers.
'' empty string vs ' ' whitespace vs NULL
What it means
Three different things that all display as nothing, and they behave differently: an empty string is not null, so it survives a null check, counts as a distinct value, and joins to other empty strings. A single space is worse, because it is invisible in every interface and unequal to the empty string.
How to confirm
Count each separately. Then check whether any text column has values that differ only by leading or trailing whitespace — "Berlin" and "Berlin " are two categories in every GROUP BY you will ever write.
What to do
Trim, then convert empty to null, in that order. Doing it the other way round leaves the whitespace-only values behind.
'N/A', '-', '?', 'unknown', 'TBD', '.'
What it means
Human-entered nulls, and the reason they are dangerous is that there are usually several variants of each in the same column — 'N/A', 'n/a', 'NA', 'na', 'not applicable' — so they appear as five separate categories and none of them is large enough to notice.
How to confirm
Top-25 values, and specifically look at the tail as well as the head — sort ascending by frequency too, because the rare junk values are where the entry-freedom shows.
What to do
Build an explicit sentinel list per column, and keep it in version control as a named mapping. Any string cleaning that lives only in one query will be forgotten by the next person, including you in six weeks.
-1, 999, 9999, 99999
What it means
Legacy sentinel codes, from an era when a fixed-width numeric field could not be null. -1 is often "unknown" or "not applicable" in dimension keys; 999 and friends appear in survey and administrative data as "refused" or "not asked". They are numerically valid and catastrophic in any aggregate.
How to confirm
Look at the distribution, not the min and max. A histogram of an age column with a spike at 999 is unmistakable; the mean alone just looks slightly high.
What to do
Ask whoever owns the source what the codes mean — they are almost always documented somewhere, and guessing at whether 999 means "refused" or "over 998" changes your interpretation.
'test', 'asdf', 'xxx', 'Test Test', test@test.com
What it means
Real rows that are not real business events. Every production database contains them: QA orders at €0.01, staff purchases at cost, a developer's account, and the residue of a data migration. They are invisible in totals and destructive to distributions, extremes and margins.
How to confirm
Search text columns for the obvious tokens, look for internal email domains, look for customer ids 1 through 10, and look for orders with implausibly small or round values. Then ask the source owner directly: "are there rows in here that aren't real business events?" — the highest-yield question you can ask them.
What to do
Exclude them in exactly one place in the pipeline, and write down the count. If that filter lives in five queries it will differ in five queries, and then two reports disagree for a reason nobody can find.

The smell tests

Heuristics that find dirt fast. None of them proves anything; all of them tell you where to look, which is more valuable than it sounds when you have forty columns and an afternoon.

Too-round numbers

Values clustering on multiples of 5, 10, 100 or 1000 in data that should be continuous. It means humans typed estimates rather than systems recording measurements. Common in weights, durations, prices and anything entered on a form.

Digit preference

Ages ending in 0 or 5 far more often than 1 in 5 of the time. Classic sign of self-reported or estimated data. The same pattern in a "years of experience" or "team size" field tells you the field is a guess.

Suspiciously uniform

A distribution that is too flat. Real-world quantities are lumpy; near-perfect uniformity usually means generated test data, a default being applied, or a value being round-robined by a system.

Missing weekends and holidays

Group any transactional table by day of week. A B2B dataset with equal Saturday volume is suspicious; a consumer dataset with zero Saturday volume is broken. This one check validates that your date column means what you think it means.

Gaps in a sequence

If invoice numbers or order ids are supposed to be contiguous, missing ones mean deleted rows, a failed load, or rows you were not given. Counting the gaps tells you how much is missing without needing the source.

Impossible combinations

A shipped date before an order date. A cancelled order with a delivery confirmation. An employee whose manager is themselves. Cheap to check, and each one found means the validation you assumed exists does not.

Benford's law — a real tool, with real limits

In many naturally occurring numeric datasets, the leading digit is not uniformly distributed: 1 appears about 30% of the time, 2 about 18%, falling to about 4.6% for 9. Data that has been fabricated or heavily hand-entered often does not follow this, so a leading-digit distribution that is close to uniform is a signal worth investigating.

But the conditions matter, and getting them wrong is a common error. Benford only applies to data that spans several orders of magnitude and arises from multiplicative or growth-like processes — transaction amounts, populations, invoice values, file sizes. It does not apply to: assigned numbers (IDs, phone numbers, postcodes), bounded ranges (ages, percentages, shoe sizes, test scores), or anything with a narrow spread. Applying it to a bounded column and declaring fraud is a genuine mistake people make. Treat it as one weak signal among many, never as evidence on its own.

What it means
Is that 3 April or 4 March? The failure mode is silent and partial: dates where the day exceeds 12 get parsed correctly, and dates where both parts are ≤ 12 get silently swapped. So roughly a third of your dates are wrong and two-thirds are right.
How to confirm
Histogram the day-of-month and the month. If months 1–12 are evenly spread but days 1–12 are twice as frequent as days 13–31, you have swapped dates. This is a beautiful check because the corruption creates a signature you can see.
What to do
Demand ISO 8601 (2026-04-03) from the source, and reject ambiguous input rather than guessing. If you must parse existing data, parse with an explicit format and fail loudly on non-conforming rows — never let a parser fall back to a second guess.

Any dataset that has been through a spreadsheet carries a specific set of injuries. Postcodes and product codes lose leading zeros (01234 → 1234). Codes that look like dates are converted (MAR1 → 1 March — this is why several human genes were officially renamed). Long identifiers lose precision when stored as floating point (1234567890123456 → 1234567890123450). Numbers become text with thousands separators.
How to confirm
Check the length distribution of identifier columns — a code that should always be 5 characters appearing as 4 means leading zeros went. Check for scientific notation (1.23E+15) in text columns.
What to do
Get the data from the system rather than from a person's export, whenever that is possible. Where it is not, insist on CSV with everything quoted, and treat identifiers as text from the moment they enter your pipeline — a code is not a number just because it is made of digits.

Referential smells: orphans, and why joins hide them

An orphan is a row pointing at something that does not exist — an order line whose product was deleted, an employee whose department was merged away. The reason this is a detection problem rather than a data problem is the join:

The same orphans, two joins, two very different stories
  fct_order_line has 41,203,884 rows.
  412 of them reference a product_id that isn't in dim_product.

  INNER JOIN to dim_product   →  41,203,472 rows
    · no error. no warning. €18,200 of revenue silently GONE.
    · your total is now 0.004% low, which nobody will ever notice, and
      the discrepancy will be blamed on rounding for years.
    · if the orphans were concentrated — a whole discontinued category —
      it could be 4% instead of 0.004%, and STILL silent.

  LEFT JOIN to dim_product    →  41,203,884 rows
    · 412 rows with NULL product attributes.
    · now you can SEE them, count them, price them, and decide.

  THE RULE: use a LEFT join to find out, then decide deliberately.
  An inner join is a decision to discard data, made silently, by
  default. It should never be how you discover a problem.

  WHAT TO DO WITH THEM (all three are legitimate):
    reject     → the row is invalid, keep it out, log the count
    quarantine → hold in a side table for someone to investigate
    unknown    → attach to a deliberate "Unknown" dimension row, so the
      member    measure survives and shows up as 'Unclassified' on the
                report instead of vanishing (see Module 5)

The check that settles arguments

Everything above tests the data against itself. Only one check tests it against the world: reconcile a total to a source that nobody in the argument controls.

The general ledger. A regulatory filing. A bank statement. A physical stock count. A published industry figure. The property that matters is not accuracy — it is independence. A number you and your stakeholder both derive from the same warehouse can be consistently wrong; a number that matches the audited accounts cannot be dismissed.

Best practice · build the reconciliation ladder

When your number does not match, do not adjust until it does. Write down each step:

naive query                              €512.4M   ✗ +6.7%
+ excluded cancelled orders              €491.1M   ✗
+ excluded internal & test channels       €489.6M   ✗
+ moved returns to net revenue            €481.0M   ✗
+ excluded the joint venture             €480.4M   ~
+ excluded VAT on the DE rows            €480.2M   ✓ matches ledger

That ladder is worth more than the final number. Each rung is a definitional decision you can now defend, and any one of them, left undiscovered, would have shown up later as a fake finding. The naive query was 6.7% high — enough to invent a trend that does not exist.

The mistake that ends the project

Building the analysis before reconciling. You spend eight days decomposing a movement, and the movement was 6.7% larger than reality because of cancelled orders. Every driver you found is partly fictional, and the fiction is concentrated in whichever segment had the most cancellations — so your specific, confident, actionable recommendation points at the wrong place.

Reconcile first. Always. If the numbers cannot be reconciled, that is your finding, and it is a more valuable one than whatever you were asked for.

Exercise 4.1 · 25 minutes

You are handed a customer table. Someone tells you it is clean, because "the primary key is unique and there are no nulls". List everything that could still be seriously wrong with it, and name the check for each.

Show the solution

"Unique key, no nulls" tests exactly two of the seven dimensions, and neither of the two that usually cost money. Ten things can still be wrong:

  1. Duplicate customers with different keys. Uniqueness of the key says nothing about uniqueness of the entity. "Maria Schmidt", "M. Schmidt" and "maria schmidt " are three rows and one person. Check: count distinct on normalised name plus email domain plus postcode, and compare to the row count.
  2. Zero nulls is itself suspicious. Real customer data is patchy. Zero nulls in a phone or second-address column means the nulls are disguised — as '', 'N/A', or 'unknown'. Check: the top-25 values of every column.
  3. Test and internal rows. Check: search for test tokens and internal email domains; ask the source owner.
  4. Whitespace and casing variants splitting categories. Check: compare distinct counts before and after trimming and lower-casing — if the number drops, you had variants.
  5. Sentinel dates. A created_at of 1970-01-01 for 3% of rows. Check: min and max of every date column, and count rows on the epoch.
  6. Truncation. Many names exactly 50 or 255 characters long. Check: string length distribution.
  7. Invalid-but-present values. Emails without an @, country codes that are not countries, postcodes of the wrong shape. Check: pattern conformity per column.
  8. Timeliness. The table may have stopped refreshing. Check: max updated_at against now, and row count by month — a cliff at the end is the tell.
  9. Completeness of the entity set. Are customers who churned still present, or were they deleted? If deleted, every historical analysis is subject to survivorship bias and no check inside this table can reveal it. Check: ask, and reconcile the count to another system.
  10. Accuracy. Every value could be valid and wrong. Check: impossible from inside the data — reconcile the count and a key aggregate against an independent source.

The general lesson: "unique key, no nulls" is a technical quality statement, made by someone thinking about database integrity. Analytical quality is a different question, and the two most expensive failures — duplicate entities and survivorship — are both invisible to it. When someone tells you data is clean, ask them what they checked.

Checklist · before you trust a dataset
  • You have run the grain test and it returned zero rows.
  • You have looked at the top 25 values of every categorical column.
  • You have checked for every sentinel in the disguised-null catalogue, especially 0 and 1970-01-01.
  • Null and defect rates are quantified twice — by row count and by value.
  • You have plotted row count by month and classified every anomaly as artefact, system change or real.
  • The incomplete final period is excluded or visibly flagged, and the cut-off is on the chart.
  • You used LEFT joins to find orphans, counted them, and made a deliberate decision.
  • You have asked the source owner: "are there rows in here that aren't real business events?"
  • You have reconciled a total to a source nobody in the argument controls.
  • If you could not reconcile, you have said so rather than adjusting until it matched.

Cleaning techniques

The cardinal rule, before any technique

Never clean in place. Cleaning is a layer; the raw copy survives untouched, forever.

Three reasons, and each one will eventually matter to you. A cleaning rule you cannot reverse is a decision you cannot revisit — and you will want to, because in three months someone will ask what those excluded rows were. Cleaning is lossy, and the thing you deleted is sometimes the finding. And if the raw data is gone, you cannot prove your number came from anywhere: every reconciliation, every audit and every "why is this different from last quarter" needs the untransformed original to compare against.

Missing values

Before choosing a technique, you must know why the value is missing, because the mechanism determines what is legitimate. This taxonomy is often quoted and frequently stated wrongly, so here it is precisely:

MechanismWhat it meansExample, and what you may do
MCAR
missing completely at random		 Whether a value is missing is unrelated to any variable — observed or unobserved. Pure chance. A sensor dropped packets at random intervals. Deletion is unbiased — you lose statistical power but not correctness. Imputation is also safe. MCAR is rare and people assume it far too readily.
MAR
missing at random		 Missingness depends on other observed variables, but not on the missing value itself once you account for those. Younger customers skip the income field more often — missingness depends on age, which you have. Imputation conditioned on the observed variables can be unbiased. Plain deletion is biased, because you drop young customers disproportionately.
MNAR
missing not at random		 Missingness depends on the missing value itself. High earners refuse to state their income. Neither deletion nor standard imputation fixes this. Imputing the mean pulls the top of the distribution down and makes the bias worse while making it invisible. You need external data, an explicit model of the missingness, or the honesty to say the variable is unusable for that question.
How to actually tell which one you have

You cannot prove MCAR versus MAR from the data alone, and you can never prove or disprove MNAR from the data alone — that is the definition of the problem. What you can do is: create a binary "is missing" flag and check whether it correlates with the variables you do have. If missingness correlates with age, region, channel or account size, it is not MCAR and deletion is biased. If it correlates with nothing observable, it might be MCAR — or it might be MNAR, which looks identical from inside the data. Then use domain reasoning: is there a reason a person or system would withhold this particular value? Refused salaries, unreported side effects and unlogged failures are all MNAR by their nature.

Listwise deletionDrop the whole row.Only under MCAR, or when the row is unusable anyway. Avoid when missingness correlates with anything — you are silently selecting a subpopulation.
Mean / median imputationFill with a central value.Median over mean if skewed. Understates variance and destroys correlations — every imputed row sits exactly on the average, which fabricates a spike in the distribution. Acceptable for a rough aggregate; never for a model input or a distribution chart.
Mode imputationFill categoricals with the most common value.Rarely good. Inflates the dominant category, which is often exactly the comparison you were making.
Forward fill / last observation carried forwardRepeat the previous known value.Correct for genuinely stepwise series — a price that holds until changed, a status that persists. Wrong for measurements, where it invents a flat line and hides the gap.
InterpolationEstimate between two known points.Reasonable for smooth continuous series with short gaps. Never across a long gap, and never for a series that can step — you will smooth over the event you were looking for.
Model-based / multiple imputationPredict the missing value from other columns; or generate several imputed datasets and pool the results.The statistically sound option under MAR. Costly, and it demands that you carry the extra uncertainty through to the answer, which most reporting cannot express.
Explicit "Unknown" categoryMake missingness a visible value of the dimension.Strongly preferred for categoricals. "Unknown: 12%" on a report is information; silently dropping those rows is not.
Best practice · the flag column, without exception

Every imputed value gets a companion boolean that says it was imputed. salary and salary_is_imputed. No exceptions, no judgement calls.

Because the alternative is a number that looks measured, which is strictly worse than a null. A null announces its own absence; an imputed value lies quietly. With the flag you can always answer the question that eventually arrives — "how much of this is real?" — and you can re-run any analysis excluding imputed rows to see whether the conclusion survives. Without it, that question is unanswerable and the honest answer becomes "I don't know."

Duplicates and entity resolution

Two different problems that look the same. Exact duplicates are a pipeline defect — the same row twice, usually from a load that ran twice or an append where an upsert was needed. They are easy to find and easy to fix. Entity duplicates are a data problem: one real customer represented as four records, none of them identical, no shared key. That is entity resolution, and it is genuinely hard.

Deduplication · the four-step procedure
STEP 1 · NORMALISE FIRST, COMPARE SECOND
  Most "fuzzy" duplicates are exact duplicates wearing a disguise:
    trim whitespace · collapse internal spaces · lower-case
    strip punctuation and accents · standardise abbreviations
    (Ltd/Limited, St/Street, GmbH) · normalise phone and postcode format
  → Do this and a surprising share of the problem disappears without any
    similarity scoring at all. ALWAYS try it before reaching for step 3.

STEP 2 · BLOCK, or you will never finish
  Comparing every record to every other is O(n²): 100,000 customers is
  5 billion comparisons. Instead, partition into BLOCKS on a cheap,
  reliable key and only compare within each block:
    first 3 letters of surname · postcode · email domain · birth year
    (or several blocking passes, each on a different key, then union)
  → the key must be something a duplicate pair would AGREE on. Blocking
    on a field that is itself dirty just hides the duplicates you want.

STEP 3 · SCORE SIMILARITY — and pick the right measure
  Levenshtein        edit distance (insert/delete/substitute).
                     Good for typos. Transposition costs 2 edits, so
                     "Smtih" vs "Smith" scores worse than you'd expect.
  Damerau-Levenshtein  as above, but a transposition costs 1. Better for
                     keyboard errors.
  Jaro-Winkler       built for SHORT strings, especially person names.
                     Boosts pairs sharing a prefix. The usual default
                     for name matching.
  Trigram / q-gram   overlap of 3-character substrings. Handles longer
                     strings, tolerates some reordering, and — crucially —
                     it is INDEXABLE, so it scales to large tables.
  Soundex/Metaphone  phonetic. "Shaw"/"Schaw". Use as one blocking pass,
                     not as a score.
  Token-based        compare sets of words, order-independent.
  (Jaccard/cosine)   "Acme Ltd" vs "Ltd Acme". Right for company names.

  → combine fields into one score with weights. An email match should
    outweigh a name match; a postcode match on a common surname means
    much more than a surname match alone.

STEP 4 · CHOOSE THE SURVIVOR — and write the rule down
  This is a business decision, not a technical one. Common rules,
  applied in a stated priority order:
    most recently updated · most complete (fewest nulls) · from the
    most trusted source system · the manually curated record if one
    exists · longest value per field ("Robert" over "Rob")
  → BEST PRACTICE: don't pick a winning ROW, build a GOLDEN RECORD by
    picking the best value PER FIELD, and keep a link table mapping
    every source record to the resolved entity id. Then the merge is
    reversible and you can always answer "which records became this?"

Two warnings. Set a review threshold, not just a match threshold — above 0.95 auto-merge, below 0.80 auto-reject, and the band between goes to a human, because that band is where the expensive mistakes live. And merging is much harder to undo than to do: two customers wrongly merged means one person can see another's orders, which in a regulated context is an incident rather than a data-quality issue.

Inconsistency and standardisation

The dullest section in the module and the one that recovers the most silently-lost data, because every inconsistency splits a category and every split category understates the thing you were measuring.

ProblemThe rule
Casing and whitespaceTrim, collapse internal runs of whitespace, then case-fold for comparison. Keep the original for display — you standardise the key, not the label a human reads.
UnitsConvert to one canonical unit at the staging layer and put the unit in the column name — weight_g, duration_ms, amount_eur_cents. A column called weight is an outage waiting for the day a source switches from kg to g.
CurrencyStore the original amount and its currency and the converted amount and the rate used and the rate date. Five columns, and you will need all five the first time someone asks why last year's figure changed.
TimezonesStore in UTC, convert for display, and state the reporting timezone once in the metric definition. Up to two hours of every day lands in the wrong day otherwise — which quietly breaks every daily number and every month boundary.

Validating first rejects rows that a trim would have saved: "DE " fails a country-code check that "DE" passes. Standardising first, validating second, means you only reject what is genuinely invalid — and your rejection log then contains real problems rather than whitespace.

Invalid values: correct, reject, or quarantine

Three options, and the mistake is treating correction as the default.

Correct — when the intent is unambiguous

"Gerrmany" → DE. A negative quantity on a line that is clearly a return. A postcode missing its leading zero in a country where all postcodes have five digits. The test: could two reasonable people disagree about the correct value? If yes, do not correct it.

Reject — when the row cannot be interpreted

An order with no customer and no amount. Log the rejection with a reason and a count; a silent reject is data loss. Then report the reject rate as a metric — a rising reject rate is an early warning that a source has changed.

Quarantine — when someone should look

The value is suspicious rather than impossible. A €480,000 order line. Hold it in a side table, let the pipeline continue, and have a person decide. This is what stops a single strange row from either corrupting a total or halting the whole load.

Never: silently coerce

Casting an unparseable date to null, or a non-numeric string to zero, without recording it. That is where 1970-01-01 comes from, and it converts a visible problem into an invisible one.

Outliers

The most over-cleaned category in data. The default reflex is to remove them, and the default reflex is usually wrong — because an outlier is either an error, or it is the most interesting row in your table.

Detection, then the four responses
DETECTING — three methods, and none of them is authoritative
  IQR rule        outside Q1 − 1.5×IQR or Q3 + 1.5×IQR.
                  Distribution-free, robust, the sensible default.
  z-score         more than ~3 standard deviations from the mean.
                  ⚠ ASSUMES ROUGHLY NORMAL, and the outliers themselves
                    inflate the SD, so extreme values hide each other.
                    Use a modified z-score (median and MAD) instead.
  domain rule     "a parcel cannot weigh 400kg", "a human cannot be 180
                  years old". ⭐ ALWAYS THE BEST ONE WHEN IT EXISTS.
                  Statistical detection is what you fall back to when
                  nobody can tell you the plausible range.

RESPONDING — pick deliberately, and write down which
  1 KEEP AND FLAG        ⭐ the right default.
      Add is_outlier. Report the number with and without. If the
      conclusion changes, that IS the finding and the reader needs it.
  2 CAP / WINSORISE
      Replace values above p99 with the p99 value. Preserves the row
      count and the direction, limits the leverage. Good for a mean you
      must report. ⚠ it CHANGES the number — say so on the chart.
  3 TRANSFORM
      Analyse on a log scale where the data is multiplicative (revenue,
      session duration, city populations). Often the outliers were never
      outliers — the scale was wrong.
  4 REMOVE
      Only when you can state WHY the row is invalid, not merely
      extreme. "It's a test order" is a reason. "It's big" is not.

⚠ AND THE ONE THAT MATTERS MOST:
  In business data, the extreme row is frequently your largest customer.
  Removing outliers from a revenue distribution can delete a third of
  revenue and produce a tidy chart about a company that doesn't exist.
  Always check WHAT the outliers are before deciding. Read the rows.

Two closing principles

Cleaning is a stage, not a task

The dirt arrives again tomorrow. A cleaning script run once in a notebook is a one-off fix to a recurring problem, and next month's numbers will be wrong in exactly the same way. Every rule in this module belongs in the pipeline, in the staging layer, versioned, tested and running on a schedule. If you cleaned data in a notebook, you have not fixed anything — you have produced one correct answer.

Sometimes the dirt is the finding

Before cleaning, ask what the dirt means. Missing delivery timestamps concentrated in one warehouse is not a data problem — it is an operational problem, and it is more valuable than the analysis you were doing. A spike in null customer ids after a release is a bug someone needs to know about today. Clean the data, and separately report the dirt, because the pattern of missingness is often information about the process that produced it.

Exercise 5.1 · 30 minutes

A delivery_time_minutes column: 12% null, 4% zero, values from 0 to 41,760 (29 days), a spike at exactly 999, and a cluster at 1,440 (24 hours). You need the average delivery time by region for a board report. Walk through what you do, in order.

Show the solution

Do not compute anything yet. There are five distinct defects in one column and each needs a separate decision — and one of them will probably change the conclusion.

1 · The 999 spike is a sentinel, not a delivery

999 minutes is 16.65 hours — an oddly specific value to spike on. Almost certainly a legacy "unknown" code. Confirm: check whether the spike is on exactly 999 with nothing at 998 or 1000; a natural distribution does not have a single-value spike. Then ask the source owner. Action: null it, and count how many.

2 · The zeros are missing values, not instant deliveries

A delivery cannot take zero minutes. This is the classic disguised null from Module 4, and it matters more than the nulls do: 4% zeros drag an average down without changing the row count, so the number moves and nothing looks wrong. Action: null them. Then check whether the zeros are concentrated in one region — if they are, that region's average was being understated and your regional comparison was wrong in a specific direction.

3 · The 1,440 cluster needs a domain answer before a statistical one

Exactly 24 hours, clustered. Three plausible causes and they need opposite treatment: a default applied when the actual time was unknown (a sentinel — null it); a genuine SLA where next-day delivery legitimately lands near 1,440 (real — keep it); or a truncation at one day in the source system (a ceiling — the true values are higher and unknowable, so the column understates and you must say so). You cannot resolve this from the data. Ask. This is the step that separates cleaning from guessing.

4 · The 29-day maximum: read the rows

Do not apply an IQR rule yet. Pull ten of the extreme rows and look at them. If they are international shipments or backorders, they are real and the distribution is genuinely long-tailed. If they are rows where the delivery timestamp was backfilled months later, the duration is an artefact of a data repair.

5 · Now the nulls — and here is the trap in the whole exercise

You now have 12% original nulls plus the zeros plus the 999s plus possibly the 1,440s: call it 18% missing. The question is not how to impute them. It is whether missingness correlates with region — because if one region has 40% missing and another has 3%, then an average computed on non-null rows is comparing a nearly-complete region against a heavily self-selected sample of the other. And the missingness is almost certainly not random: deliveries that go wrong are the ones that fail to get a timestamp. That is MNAR, and it biases every region differently.

What you actually deliver

  1. Not the mean — the median and a percentile. The distribution is right-skewed with a long tail, so the mean is dragged by the tail and describes nobody. Report median and p90; p90 is also the number the business cares about, because it is the bad experience.
  2. Coverage per region, on the same chart as the metric. "Region A: median 340 min, 97% coverage / Region B: median 290 min, 61% coverage." That second number is not a footnote — it is the reason not to conclude that B is faster.
  3. No imputation. Under MNAR, imputing pulls the missing (likely slow) deliveries toward the middle and makes the bias invisible. Leaving them out is also biased, but at least the coverage figure discloses it.
  4. One sentence of limitation in the board note: "delivery time is recorded for 82% of orders; coverage varies by region from 61% to 97%, and missing timestamps are more common on delayed orders, so these figures understate delivery times — most in Region B."

The lesson: the request was "average delivery time by region". The honest answer is a median, a percentile, a coverage figure and a caveat — and a flag raised about why Region B is not recording timestamps, which is the actual finding.

Exercise 5.2 · 20 minutes

You are deduplicating a 200,000-row customer table with no reliable id. You have name, email, phone, postcode and created date. Design the approach — and say what could go badly wrong.

Show the solution

Normalise first

Before any scoring: trim and case-fold, strip punctuation and accents, normalise phone to E.164, normalise postcode format, and lower-case the email. Then look for exact duplicates on normalised email alone — that will resolve a large share of the problem for almost no effort, and you must do it before investing in similarity scoring.

One refinement worth knowing: for Gmail-style addresses, a.b+tag@gmail.com and ab@gmail.com are the same mailbox. Whether to canonicalise that is a business decision — it is right for deduplication and wrong if you are counting sign-ups.

Then block

200,000 records is 20 billion pairwise comparisons — impossible. Several blocking passes, each cheap, then union the candidate pairs:

  • same normalised email domain + first 3 letters of surname
  • same postcode + first letter of surname
  • same phone number (last 9 digits, to survive country-code inconsistency)
  • same Soundex of surname + birth year, if available

Multiple passes matter because any single blocking key fails when that field is the one that differs between the duplicates.

Then score, with the right measure per field

Jaro-Winkler for names — it is designed for short strings and rewards a shared prefix. Trigram for addresses, because they are longer and word order varies. Exact match for phone and postcode. Then a weighted combination in which an email match dominates, since it is the strongest single signal, and a name match on a common surname counts for very little on its own.

Then decide, with a review band

Above 0.95 auto-merge, below 0.80 auto-reject, and the 0.80–0.95 band goes to a human. Build a golden record per field rather than picking a winning row — most recent non-null value for mutable fields like address, longest value for names, earliest created date for tenure — and keep a link table from every source record to the resolved entity id, so the merge is reversible.

What goes badly wrong

  1. Over-merging, and it is not symmetric with under-merging. Two different people merged means one can see the other's orders and history. In a regulated context that is a data-protection incident, not a quality issue. Under-merging just means a slightly inflated customer count. So bias the thresholds toward under-merging — that asymmetry should drive the whole design.
  2. Households. Two real people share a surname, address and phone. Every similarity measure says they are the same person; they are not. If the domain has households — retail, insurance, healthcare — you need a first-name check that can veto a match, not merely lower its score.
  3. Shared and placeholder contact details. info@company.com on four hundred B2B records, or noreply@, or a call-centre phone number. A high-frequency contact value is not an identifier — put a frequency cap on any field before using it as evidence.
  4. Transitive merging. A matches B, B matches C, but A does not match C. Naive clustering chains them together and you can collapse hundreds of distinct people into one entity. Decide the clustering rule explicitly — connected components is the permissive option and it is how runaway merges happen.
  5. It is not idempotent unless you make it so. Run it again next week with 1,000 new records and the resolved ids must be stable, or every downstream report's history changes. The link table is what gives you that.

The lesson: the technical part — normalise, block, score — is the easy half. The hard half is that merge errors are asymmetric, that households and shared contact details break every naive method, and that the operation has to be repeatable and reversible.

Checklist · a defensible cleaning process
  • The raw layer is untouched and append-only; every rule lives in staging.
  • Every cleaning rule is named, counted, and its counts are trended so a jump becomes an alert.
  • Sentinel values are nulled at the earliest layer, from a versioned list per column.
  • You know the likely missingness mechanism, and you have checked whether missingness correlates with your grouping variable.
  • Every imputed value has a companion flag column. No exceptions.
  • Deduplication normalises before scoring, blocks before comparing, and biases toward under-merging.
  • Entity merges are reversible via a link table, and stable across runs.
  • Standardisation happens before validation, not after.
  • Rejections are logged with a reason and a count; the reject rate is monitored.
  • Outliers are flagged rather than removed, unless you can say why the row is invalid.
  • Identifiers are text; money is integer minor units or decimal; units are in the column names.
  • Orphans point at an explicit Unknown member, and the Unknown share has a threshold.
  • Every rule runs on a schedule, not in a notebook.
  • You have asked what the dirt means, and reported it separately where it is a finding.

ETL / ELT

The machinery that moves data from where it is created to where it can be analysed, and keeps doing it every night while nobody watches. Four modules: the architecture, getting data in, transforming it, and operating the whole thing when it breaks.

Analysts are often told this is somebody else's job. It is not, for two reasons. Module 6

Pipeline architecture

ETL, ELT, what actually changed, and the layered model that makes a pipeline maintainable instead of a pile of scripts nobody dares touch.

ETL versus ELT — and what really moved

Both acronyms describe the same three operations in a different order. The difference is where the transformation happens, and the reason the industry moved is not fashion — it is that the economics changed.

ETL — extract, transform, loadELT — extract, load, transform
Order Pull from source → transform on the way → load only the finished result into the warehouse. Pull from source → load raw into the warehouse immediately → transform inside the warehouse with SQL.
Why it existed Warehouse storage and compute were expensive and fixed. You could not afford to store raw data you were not going to use, so you transformed first and loaded only what was needed. Cloud storage became almost free and compute became elastic and separable. So you can afford to keep everything raw and transform repeatedly.
The big advantage Less data stored; sensitive fields can be dropped or masked before they ever land. Reprocessing. The raw data is still there, so when a definition changes or a transform had a bug, you rebuild from raw. Under ETL, the raw data was never kept — so a bad transform is permanent data loss and you must re-extract from a source that may have moved on.
The cost Transform logic lives in a separate tool, often visual, hard to version, hard to test, and usually understood by one person. You store a lot of data you never use, warehouse compute bills are metered per query, and raw sensitive data now lives in the warehouse — which is a governance obligation.
Still right when Regulation forbids raw data landing (health, payment card details); the volume is genuinely too large to store; or you are streaming and must act on data in flight. Almost everything else, for an analytics workload.
The one sentence to remember

ELT's real product is not speed — it is the ability to change your mind. Keeping raw data means a definition change is a rebuild rather than a re-extraction, and a discovered bug is fixable retroactively. Almost every argument for ELT reduces to this, and it is the reason "land it raw first" is now the default advice regardless of the tool.

The layered model

This is the single most useful structural idea in the module. Every maintainable pipeline has these layers, whatever the tool calls them, and each layer has exactly one job. The discipline is not the names; it is the refusal to do two jobs in one place.

The four layers · and the rule for each
Raw · bronze

As it arrived

An exact copy of the source. Append-only, never modified, schema as-received. Includes the dirt, the duplicates and the sentinels.

Rule: nothing is cleaned here, not even whitespace. This layer is your evidence and your rebuild path.

Staging · silver

One model per source table

Rename to your conventions, cast types, trim, null the sentinels, deduplicate, standardise units. Every rule from Module 5 lives here.

Rule: no joins. A staging model that joins two sources cannot be reused by anyone who needs only one of them.

Intermediate

The awkward middle

Where grain changes and multi-step logic live — allocating a shipment cost across lines, resolving a hierarchy, building a slowly changing history.

Rule: anything that fans out or aggregates happens here, once, so no downstream model can get the grain wrong.

Marts · gold

What people query

Facts and dimensions, business-named, documented. This is the contract with your users.

Rule: assumes its inputs are clean. If you are still cleaning here, the same dirt is being handled in several places and they will diverge.

Why "no joins in staging" matters more than it sounds

It is the rule people break first and regret longest. Suppose you join customers to orders in staging because your first report needs both. Now the customer model is unusable for anyone analysing customers who have not ordered, the order model does not exist independently, and the join's grain assumption is baked into the foundation of everything built after it. Six months later a wrong number traces back to that join and the fix touches forty models.

One staging model per source table, doing only cleaning, is boring and it is what makes the layers above it composable.

Idempotency — the property that makes a pipeline survivable

A pipeline step is idempotent if running it twice produces the same result as running it once. This sounds academic and it is the single most practical property in the module, because pipelines fail halfway and get retried constantly — by a scheduler, by a person, by a deploy.

The same step, non-idempotent and idempotent
NOT IDEMPOTENT — the default if you don't think about it
  INSERT INTO fct_orders
  SELECT * FROM staging_orders WHERE order_date = '2026-09-01';

  run once   → 12,400 rows.
  run twice  → 24,800 rows. Revenue for that day is now double.
  → and nothing errored. The pipeline "succeeded" both times.

IDEMPOTENT — three ways to get there

  1 · DELETE-INSERT BY PARTITION   ⭐ simplest, and usually right
      DELETE FROM fct_orders WHERE order_date = '2026-09-01';
      INSERT INTO fct_orders SELECT ... WHERE order_date = '2026-09-01';
      → run it a hundred times, the day always ends up with exactly
        one copy. Needs the whole partition to be reproducible.

  2 · UPSERT / MERGE ON A KEY
      MERGE ... ON target.order_id = source.order_id
        WHEN MATCHED THEN UPDATE ... WHEN NOT MATCHED THEN INSERT
      → needs a genuine unique business key. If the "key" isn't
        unique, MERGE either errors or silently picks a row.

  3 · CONTENT HASH DEDUPE
      hash every column, ignore rows whose hash already exists
      → good for append-only event data with no natural key.
        Costs storage and doesn't handle legitimate re-sends.

THE TEST YOU SHOULD ACTUALLY RUN:
  run the pipeline twice in a row on the same day and compare row
  counts and a money total. If they change, it is not idempotent, and
  it will eventually double-count in production on a night when
  someone retried a failed run.

Module 7

Extract & load

Getting data in, and the ways it silently loses rows. This module contains the failure modes that cause most "the number is wrong" incidents.

Full load or incremental?

Full load — replace everything, every runIncremental — only what changed
How Truncate the target and reload the entire source, or write to a new table and swap. Identify what is new or changed since last time, and apply only that.
For Naturally idempotent, self-healing, and simple. Deletes and retroactive edits in the source are handled for free, because you never carry old state forward. A bug is fixed by rerunning. Fast, cheap, and the only option once the table is large or the source has rate limits.
Against Cost and time grow with total volume, and there is a window during which the target is incomplete unless you swap atomically. Every failure mode in this module is an incremental failure mode. It carries state, and state drifts. It will not self-heal.
Best practice · stay full for as long as you can bear it

The instinct is to build incremental immediately because it feels professional. Resist it. A full reload of a 50-million-row table on a modern warehouse takes minutes and costs pennies, and in exchange you get self-healing correctness — the single most valuable property a pipeline can have.

Go incremental when the full load stops fitting in its window or the bill becomes noticeable, not before. And when you do, keep a periodic full refresh — weekly or monthly — as a reconciliation safety net. That one habit catches every kind of drift described below.

Watermarks, and the four ways they lose data

The commonest incremental strategy: remember the highest updated_at you have seen, and next run fetch everything greater than it. It is simple, it needs nothing from the source, and it is quietly lossy in four distinct ways. These cause real incidents and almost nobody is taught them.

1 · The transaction that commits out of order
What happens
A long transaction starts at 10:00 and commits at 10:07. Its rows carry updated_at = 10:00. Your pipeline runs at 10:05, sees nothing above its watermark from that transaction (it is not committed yet), and moves the watermark to 10:05. At 10:07 the rows become visible — timestamped 10:00, below the watermark. They are never picked up. Ever.
Why it is nasty
It is invisible, it is silent, and the lost rows are disproportionately the large, complex transactions — the ones that took long enough to straddle a run. So you lose your biggest orders.
Fix
Use a lag window: fetch everything above watermark − N minutes where N safely exceeds your longest transaction, and rely on an idempotent upsert to absorb the re-read rows. You trade a little redundant work for correctness. The periodic full refresh is your backstop.
2 · Ties on the boundary
What happens
Fifty rows share the exact same updated_at, and it happens to be your watermark value. With > watermark you skip all fifty. With >= watermark you re-read them every single run, forever, which is harmless only if your load is idempotent.
Why it happens
Bulk updates write identical timestamps to thousands of rows. Low-resolution timestamps (second, not microsecond) make collisions routine.
Fix
Use >= and make the load idempotent — that is the whole answer. Or use a composite watermark, (updated_at, primary_key), so ties have a deterministic tiebreaker.
3 · Rows updated without the timestamp changing
What happens
A row is genuinely modified but updated_at does not move — because the update was a bulk operation that bypassed the application, or a direct edit by an engineer, or a trigger that was never applied to that table, or a column the application does not consider "an update". Your pipeline can never see this change.
How to detect
Only by comparison: periodically pull the full source and diff against your copy, or compare row-level hashes. There is no clever query that finds it, because the evidence does not exist in the column you are watching.
Fix
The periodic full refresh, or log-based CDC, which observes the actual write rather than trusting an application-maintained column. This failure mode is the strongest argument for CDC.
4 · Deletes are completely invisible
What happens
A row is deleted in the source. There is no updated_at on a row that no longer exists, so a watermark query returns nothing about it. Your warehouse keeps the deleted row forever, and it keeps counting toward every total.
Why it is the worst of the four
It compounds. Every deletion since the pipeline was built is still in your numbers, so the error grows monotonically and there is no point at which it becomes obvious. Cancelled orders, removed products, deleted test accounts — all still there.
Fix
Three options, in order of preference. (a) Soft deletes in the source — an is_deleted flag, so a delete becomes an update and the watermark sees it. Ask for this; it is the cleanest answer and costs the source team little. (b) Log-based CDC, which emits delete events natively. (c) Key reconciliation — pull just the list of primary keys from the source (cheap, no row data), diff against your keys, and mark the missing ones deleted. This is a genuinely good compromise and it is underused.

Change data capture

CDC means capturing changes as events rather than inferring them from a timestamp. Three mechanisms, with real trade-offs — and being able to name them is a strong signal in any data conversation.

MechanismHow it worksGets you · costs you
Log-based Reads the database's own transaction log — Postgres WAL, MySQL binlog — which the database already writes for replication. Captures every change including deletes, in commit order, with almost no load on the source (it is reading a file the DB writes anyway). Costs: privileged access and a replication slot; a slot that stops being consumed can fill the source's disk and take production down; schema changes can break the reader; operationally the most complex option.
Trigger-based Database triggers write every insert/update/delete into an audit table, which you then extract from. Captures deletes, works on almost any database, no special privileges. Costs: write amplification — every production write now does extra work, so it slows the application you are observing; and triggers are code hidden in the database that nobody maintains or tests.
Query-based
(polling a timestamp)		 The watermark approach above. Not really CDC, but it occupies the same slot in a design. Simplest by far, needs nothing from the source team. Costs: all four failure modes above — misses deletes, misses timestamp-less updates, vulnerable to commit ordering — plus query load on the source.
The honest recommendation

For most analytics work: query-based with a lag window, an idempotent upsert, key reconciliation for deletes, and a periodic full refresh. That combination is simple to operate and closes all four failure modes without asking anything of the source team.

Move to log-based CDC when you genuinely need low latency, when the table is too large to refresh, or when the source has no reliable timestamp at all. Do not adopt it because it is the sophisticated answer — a replication slot is a production dependency, and a full disk on the source database is a much worse day than a stale dashboard.

Schema drift

Sources change without telling you. A new column appears, a column is renamed, a type widens, an enum gains a value. Your pipeline's response to each is a design decision, and the default is usually wrong.

ChangeWhat should happen
New column addedIngest it into raw automatically, ignore it downstream until someone asks. Harmless, and auto-adding to raw means the history exists when it is eventually wanted.
Column removed or renamedFail loudly. This is the one to never auto-handle: a silently vanished column becomes a column of nulls, and a column of nulls becomes a metric quietly reading zero.
Type changedFail, or land as text in raw and cast in staging with explicit failure on non-conforming values. A widened type that you silently truncate is data loss.
New enum / category valueAlert, do not fail. A new order status you do not know about is usually excluded by your WHERE clauses, so revenue quietly drops. An accepted_values test is exactly what catches this.
Meaning changed, structure identicalThe worst case, and undetectable by any automated test — the column still validates, the values still look right, and they now mean something else. Only a conversation or a distribution alert catches it. This is what data contracts exist to prevent.

Extracting from APIs and files

Pagination that shifts under you

Offset pagination over data that is being written breaks: a row inserted between page 2 and page 3 shifts everything down, so you miss a row and duplicate another. Prefer cursor or keyset pagination. If only offsets exist, sort by an immutable key and accept the redundancy of an idempotent load.

Rate limits and retries

Honour Retry-After. Exponential backoff with jitter — without jitter, every client retries in lockstep and you build a synchronised stampede. Cap the attempts and dead-letter the rest.

Partial failure is the normal case

You fetched 40 of 60 pages and the API failed. The rule: never publish a partial extract as complete. Land pages into a staging area and only promote the run when every page has arrived, or record explicitly how far it got so the next run resumes.

Files: never trust the name

sales_final_v2.csv is not a contract. Validate row counts and a checksum against a manifest if there is one. Watch for the same file arriving twice under different names, and for a file that arrives truncated because you read it while it was still being written — land files with a completion marker.

Exercise 7.1 · 25 minutes

Your nightly incremental pipeline has run without error for eight months. Finance says the customer count in your warehouse is 4% higher than in the source system. The pipeline has never failed. What are the likely causes, and how would you confirm each?

Show the solution

"Never failed" is the clue, not the reassurance. A pipeline that has run cleanly for eight months while drifting 4% high is doing exactly what it was built to do — the defect is in the design, not in an incident. And higher in the warehouse than in the source narrows it a great deal: you are keeping things the source no longer has.

1 · Deletes — by far the most likely, ~90% of my prior

Watermark-based incremental loads cannot see deletions. Eight months of deleted customers — test accounts, GDPR erasures, merged duplicates, spam registrations — are all still in your copy, and the gap grows monotonically. That signature matches: no errors, slowly increasing discrepancy, warehouse always higher.

Confirm in ten minutes: pull the list of primary keys from the source, diff against yours. If the extra 4% are keys that simply do not exist upstream, you are done. Then check whether the source uses soft deletes and you are ignoring the flag — that is the same bug with an easier fix.

2 · Duplicates from a non-idempotent load

If the load appends rather than upserts, any retried run duplicated its rows. Confirm: the grain test — group by the business key and count. Also check whether the extras cluster on particular dates, which would point at specific reruns.

3 · A definitional difference, not a data difference

Before assuming a bug: is finance counting the same thing? Their number may exclude soft-deleted customers, or B2B accounts, or a subsidiary, or count only customers with an order. Confirm: ask what their query is. A material share of "the warehouse is wrong" reports are definitional, and checking costs one message — do this first, actually, because it is the cheapest.

4 · Hard-deleted rows re-inserted by a late-arriving update

Subtler: a row deleted in the source, but an update event for it arrived afterwards from another path and your upsert re-created it. Confirm: look at the extras' timestamps for any pattern of resurrection after deletion.

What I would do about it

  1. Add key reconciliation to the nightly run. Pull the source's key list, diff, mark absentees deleted. Cheap, and it closes the delete hole permanently.
  2. Add a periodic full refresh — weekly if the table allows. This is the habit that would have prevented eight months of silent drift.
  3. Add a reconciliation test to the pipeline: warehouse count versus source count, with a tolerance, failing the run when it breaches. The real lesson here is that the pipeline had no test that could have detected this — it only checked that it ran, not that it was right.
  4. Tell finance the number was wrong, with the size and the cause, before they find the next one. And check what else was built on that count.

The general lesson: "the pipeline never failed" and "the data is correct" are unrelated statements. Monitoring that only watches for errors cannot see drift, and drift is the characteristic failure of incremental loads.

Keeping history — SCD type 2 as a pipeline job

Module 2 covers what a type 2 dimension is. This is what the pipeline actually has to do every night, because it is the most error-prone transformation in common use.

The nightly type-2 procedure
GIVEN  a target dimension with:
         natural key        (employee_id — the real-world identifier)
         surrogate key      (one per VERSION, not per entity)
         tracked attributes (department, manager, grade)
         valid_from, valid_to, is_current

STEP 1 · DETECT CHANGE
  Compare each incoming row to the CURRENT version, on a hash of the
  TRACKED attributes only.
    hash matches      → nothing to do
    hash differs      → a change: close and open
    no current row    → a new entity: open only
    in target, not in source → the entity is gone (see deletes, M7)

  ⚠ hash only the attributes you TRACK. Hashing every column means an
    irrelevant field (a "last_login" timestamp) creates a new version
    every night, and your dimension grows without carrying information.

STEP 2 · CLOSE THE OLD VERSION
  set valid_to   = 
      is_current = false

STEP 3 · OPEN THE NEW VERSION
  insert with a NEW surrogate key
      valid_from = 
      valid_to   = 9999-12-31
      is_current = true

THE THREE DECISIONS THAT DECIDE WHETHER THIS IS CORRECT

  1 WHICH TIMESTAMP?  the source's effective date, NOT your load time.
    If someone's promotion was effective 1 September and your pipeline
    saw it on 4 September, using load time puts three days of work
    under the wrong grade — and every "as at 2 September" report is
    wrong. Use load time only when the source genuinely offers nothing
    better, and say so in the documentation.

  2 WHICH ATTRIBUTES ARE TYPE 2, AND WHICH ARE TYPE 1?
    Almost every real dimension is a hybrid. Department: type 2 — you
    need to know who was where. Email address: type 1 — just overwrite
    it, nobody analyses by historical email. Getting this wrong in the
    type-2 direction bloats the table; in the type-1 direction it
    destroys history you cannot recover.

  3 WHAT ABOUT A CHANGE THAT ARRIVES LATE?
    A promotion backdated to 1 August, arriving in September, when
    you already have a version starting 15 August. You must INSERT A
    VERSION IN THE MIDDLE and re-close its neighbours. Most naive
    implementations simply cannot do this — and they fail silently,
    producing overlapping or gapped validity ranges.
    → Test for it: no two versions of one natural key may overlap,
      and there must be no gap between valid_to and the next
      valid_from. Assert both, every run.

The two tests that make a type-2 dimension trustworthy: exactly one is_current row per natural key, and no overlaps or gaps in the validity ranges. Without them, a subtle bug corrupts history for months and only surfaces when somebody asks a point-in-time question — at which point the answer is quietly wrong.

Incremental models and the correctness risk

Once a transformation gets slow, the temptation is to make it incremental — process only new rows and append. This is where correctness quietly dies, because an incremental model is a cache and caches go stale in ways that do not announce themselves.

The trapWhat happens, and what to do
Retroactive source changes You processed 1 September last week. Yesterday somebody edited a 1 September record. Your incremental model only looks at new rows, so it never revisits September — the old value is frozen in your mart forever. Fix: reprocess a trailing window (the last 7 or 30 days) every run, not just new rows. Cheap, and it absorbs almost all retroactive edits.
Window functions over a boundary A running total, a "previous order date", a rank — these need rows outside the increment to be correct. Computing them on today's slice alone produces a value that is right within the slice and wrong overall. Fix: either compute these in a full-refresh model, or explicitly read the necessary history back in. This is the most common incremental bug and the hardest to spot, because the number looks plausible.
Logic changed, history did not You fix a definition today. New rows use the new logic; every row already in the table uses the old one. Now your table contains two definitions and a discontinuity on the deploy date. Fix: a logic change requires a full rebuild. Make that a rule, not a judgement call.
Late-arriving dimension rows A fact arrives before its dimension member exists, so it maps to Unknown — and stays Unknown forever, even after the dimension catches up an hour later. Fix: reprocess the trailing window, or re-resolve Unknown-keyed facts on each run.
Best practice · the rules that keep incremental honest

Reprocess a trailing window, not just new rows. Full-refresh anything with a window function. A logic change forces a rebuild. And keep the model runnable as a full refresh — if rebuilding from scratch is impossible, you have no recovery path from any bug, ever.

Trap · pre-aggregating too early

The mistake: building a daily summary table because the detail query feels slow, before measuring it.

Why it costs you: an aggregate answers only the questions its grain and dimensions allow. Someone will ask "can you split that by channel?" and the answer is "not without rebuilding the aggregate" — so you build another one, and now two summary tables can disagree with each other and with the detail. Aggregates multiply, and each one is a definition to maintain.

The fix: query the detail until it is measurably too slow for a real user, then aggregate deliberately, document what the aggregate cannot answer, and make sure it is derived from the detail rather than computed independently — so it can never disagree.

Module 9

Pipeline operations

Everything that happens after the pipeline works once. This is where the job actually is — and where "why did last month's number change?" gets answered.

Orchestration: dependencies, not schedules

The commonest operational mistake in data is scheduling by clock time. Extract at 02:00, transform at 03:00, refresh the dashboard at 04:00 — on the assumption that each finishes within its hour.

Why time-based scheduling fails, and what replaces it
TIME-BASED — the trap
  02:00  extract      (usually 25 min… tonight, 80 min)
  03:00  transform    ← RUNS ANYWAY, on yesterday's data
  04:00  dashboards   ← refresh from a half-built mart

  Nothing errors. Every job "succeeded". The dashboard is silently
  a day stale, or worse, mid-load and internally inconsistent.
  The failure is invisible precisely BECAUSE nothing failed.

DEPENDENCY-BASED — a DAG
  extract_orders ─┐
  extract_products ┼─→ stg_* ─→ int_* ─→ fct_orders ─→ dashboard refresh
  extract_shipments ┘                 └─→ dim_product ─┘

  each task starts only when its upstream tasks have SUCCEEDED.
  a failure upstream means downstream doesn't run — which is the
  correct behaviour, because a partial mart is worse than a stale one.

THE THREE THINGS A DAG BUYS YOU
  1 correctness — nothing reads half-built data
  2 a place to retry — one failed task, not the whole night
  3 lineage — the graph IS the documentation of what breaks what

⚠ SCHEDULE ONLY THE ROOTS. Everything downstream is triggered by
  dependency. If you find yourself adding a "wait 20 minutes" step,
  you have a dependency you have not declared.

Freshness, and the failure that looks like nothing

The most under-monitored failure in analytics: a pipeline that stops running. There is no error because there is no run. The dashboard loads, the numbers are plausible, and they are eleven days old. Somebody makes a decision on them.

Best practice · freshness is a metric, and it belongs on the dashboard

Put "data as at 02:14 today" on every dashboard, visibly, always. Not in a tooltip. It costs one text box and it converts a silent failure into something any user notices immediately — which turns every consumer into a monitor.

Then set an explicit freshness SLA per table ("orders is never more than 26 hours stale") and alert on the SLA rather than on job failure. Those are different alarms: a job can fail while the data is fine, and the data can be stale while every job reports success.

Backfills — the hardest routine operation in data

A backfill is re-processing history: because you fixed a bug, added a column, changed a definition, or recovered from missed data. It is routine, it is dangerous, and there is no undo.

The backfill checklist · in order, and do not skip 1 or 3
 1 STATE WHY, IN ONE SENTENCE, AND WHAT WILL CHANGE
     "Fixing the timezone bug in stg_orders will move ~2% of orders
      between days for all of 2025, changing daily revenue but not
      monthly totals."
     → if you can't predict the direction and rough size of the
       change, you don't understand the fix well enough to run it.

 2 TELL THE CONSUMERS FIRST — before, not after
     Numbers people have already reported are about to change. A
     backfill discovered by a stakeholder is an incident; a backfill
     announced in advance is maintenance. Same operation, completely
     different day.

 3 VERIFY THE STEP IS IDEMPOTENT
     Re-run ONE partition. Does it produce one copy or two? If it
     produces two, stop — you are about to duplicate history.
     This is the check that prevents the worst backfill outcome.

 4 SNAPSHOT THE OLD VALUES
     Copy the current aggregate (revenue by month, say) to a side
     table before you start. It is your before-and-after diff, your
     evidence, and your rollback reference.

 5 DO ONE PARTITION AND DIFF IT
     Pick one month. Run it. Compare old versus new. Is the change
     the size and direction you predicted in step 1? If not, stop
     and find out why — the fix may be wrong.

 6 CHUNK THE REST, AND WATCH THE COST
     Month by month, not three years in one statement. A single
     enormous job can run for hours, hold locks, blow your compute
     budget, and — worst — starve the nightly production run.
     Backfill outside the normal window, with a concurrency limit.

 7 REBUILD DOWNSTREAM TOO
     Marts, aggregates, extracts, BI caches. A fixed staging model
     with stale marts on top of it is the worst of both worlds:
     inconsistent, and nobody knows which layer to trust.

 8 RECONCILE AFTERWARDS
     Re-run the reconciliation from Module 4. A backfill that leaves
     you further from the ledger has made things worse.

 9 WRITE DOWN WHAT YOU DID
     Date, reason, range, expected effect, actual effect. In six
     months someone will find a discontinuity on that date and this
     note is the only thing that explains it.

Late-arriving data and restatement

This is the answer to the question every analyst eventually gets asked: "why is last month's number different from what you told me last month?"

Data arrives late for entirely legitimate reasons. Invoices from suppliers land days after the sale. Returns are processed weeks after the order and are booked back to the original period. Insurance claims settle months later. Corrections are made. None of this is a bug — but it means a period's number is not final when the period ends.

The habitWhy
Every figure carries an "as at" date"September revenue was €4.1M, as at 3 October." Then a different number later is an update, not a contradiction. This single convention prevents most credibility damage.
Define a close date, and freeze after itAgree with finance that a period is final after N days. Before that, figures are provisional and labelled; after it, they do not move. Without a close date, every number is permanently provisional and nobody can plan.
Reprocess a trailing windowThe pipeline revisits the last 30 or 60 days every run, so late arrivals actually land instead of being frozen out by an incremental filter.
Measure the restatementTrack how much a period typically moves after close. If September usually gains 1.8% in the following three weeks, you can tell someone their provisional number will probably rise — which is enormously more useful than a bare figure.
Snapshot what you publishedKeep the numbers as you reported them. When someone quotes an old deck, you can explain the difference in one sentence instead of an afternoon.

Alert on stale data, not on job failed — because a job can fail while the data is still fine (a retry succeeded), and the data can be stale while every job reports success (the schedule was disabled). The thing you care about is whether a consumer would be misled, so monitor that.

And route alerts somewhere a human reads. An alert channel nobody watches is a slower way of not knowing.

Data quality tests in the pipeline — the four kinds

Detection in Module 4 was something you do once. This is the same thinking, automated, so the pipeline tells you it has broken rather than a stakeholder telling you.

KindExamplesNotes
1 · Structural not_null, unique, accepted_values, relationships (no orphans) Cheap, fast, and they encode your grain assumption as an executable assertion. Put unique on every primary key of every model — that one test catches fan-outs, duplicated loads and grain misunderstandings all at once.
2 · Volume & freshness Row count within an expected band; newest timestamp within the SLA Catches partial and duplicated loads, and the pipeline that stopped. Use a band from recent history rather than a fixed number, or you will be updating the threshold forever.
3 · Distribution Null rate below a threshold; mean or p95 within a range; a category's share stable Catches the meaning changes nothing else can see. Higher maintenance — these need thresholds that tolerate genuine business change, so start with a handful on the columns that matter most.
4 · Reconciliation Warehouse revenue by month versus the general ledger, within tolerance The most valuable test you can write, and the one almost nobody has. The other three check that the pipeline behaved; only this one checks that the answer is right. It is what would have caught the eight-month drift in exercise 7.1.
Best practice · decide what a failing test does

A test that fails and lets the pipeline continue is a warning. A test that fails and stops the pipeline is a gate. You need both, and you must choose per test.

Gate on things that make the data wrong: a duplicated primary key, a failed reconciliation, a volume at twice normal. Warn on things that make it suspicious: a null rate creeping up, a new category value. Gate everything and one odd row halts the business; warn everything and nobody reads the warnings. The default in most tools is "warn", which is why broken data reaches dashboards.

Contracts, ownership and cost

Data contracts

An explicit agreement with the source: these fields, these types, these guarantees, this notice period before a breaking change. It converts "they changed the schema and broke us" from a recurring surprise into a violated commitment. Even a one-page document naming an owner and a notice period is a large improvement on the usual arrangement, which is nothing.

Ownership

Every table needs a name attached — who fixes it at 3am, who decides its definition. Unowned tables rot: nobody updates them, nobody deletes them, and people keep querying them. Ownership is the cheapest governance there is.

Lineage and catalogs

Lineage answers "what breaks if I change this?" and "which dashboards are wrong right now?" A catalog answers "does a table for this already exist?" — which is the question that prevents the fourth table called orders.

Cost, because compute is metered

On modern warehouses you pay per query. A dashboard set to refresh every five minutes on a full-table scan is a real monthly bill. Watch: unnecessary refresh frequency, full scans where a partition filter would do, and full-refresh models that could be incremental once they are large. Cost is now an analyst concern, not just an infrastructure one.

Exercise 9.1 · 30 minutes

Monday, 09:10. The head of sales says the revenue dashboard shows €0 for the whole of last week. Walk through your first thirty minutes — what you check, in what order, and what you communicate.

Show the solution

Two principles for the whole exercise. Communicate before you diagnose — someone is about to make a decision on a broken dashboard, and stopping that is more urgent than knowing why. And work down the lineage from the consumer to the source, because that is the order that localises the failure fastest.

Minute 0–2 · Stop the bleeding

Reply immediately: "Looking at it now — treat that dashboard as unreliable until I confirm. I'll update you within the hour." One message. It costs nothing and it prevents the worst outcome, which is somebody acting on the zero.

Minute 2–5 · Is it the data or the presentation?

Query the mart directly for last week. This single check splits the problem in half:

  • Mart has data, dashboard shows zero → the failure is in the BI layer: a broken filter, a changed default date range, a stale extract, a permissions change so this user sees nothing, or a renamed field. Note that "shows €0" rather than "shows an error" often means a filter — a status value that stopped matching, for instance.
  • Mart is also empty → continue upstream.

Minute 5–10 · Freshness and the run history

Check the newest timestamp in the mart, and the orchestrator's run history for the week. Three distinct pictures, three different causes:

  • No runs at all → the schedule is disabled, credentials expired, or the orchestrator itself is down. The classic silent failure — nothing failed because nothing ran. Check whether anything was deployed or rotated last week.
  • Runs succeeded, loaded zero rows → the pipeline is healthy and the source gave it nothing. Most likely: a schema change so the extract matched no rows, a source-side filter, or an upstream system that stopped writing. This is the case where volume monitoring would have alerted on Monday last week.
  • Runs failed → read the error, and then ask the more important question: why did five days of failures not alert anyone?

Minute 10–20 · Walk up the layers

Mart → intermediate → staging → raw. The first layer that has the data tells you exactly where it was lost. This takes four queries and it is why the layered architecture in Module 6 has operational value, not just aesthetic value.

Two specific things to check as you go: does raw have the rows (if yes, it is a transform bug, not an ingestion bug), and did a WHERE clause start excluding everything — a new status value, a changed enum, a timezone shift moving rows out of the window.

Minute 20–30 · Communicate properly, then fix

Second message, with four things and no jargon:

What's wrong:  the revenue dashboard has shown €0 since Tuesday.
Why:           our sales feed changed a field name on Tuesday's
               release; our pipeline has been loading zero rows
               since, and nothing alerted us.
Scope:         the dashboard and the weekly revenue email. Monthly
               finance reporting is unaffected — that comes from
               the ledger.
Fix + when:    fix is about two hours, then I'll backfill the six
               missing days. Correct figures by end of day, and
               I'll confirm.

Note the scope line. The instinct is to describe the fault; what the business needs is to know what else it cannot trust. That is what lineage is for.

Afterwards — the part that matters more than the fix

The real failure here is not the renamed field. It is that a pipeline loaded zero rows for five days and nothing noticed. So the follow-up work is:

  1. A volume test that fails when row count falls outside its recent band. This alone would have caught it on day one.
  2. A freshness SLA and alert on the mart, routed to a channel someone reads.
  3. Freshness on the dashboard itself — "data as at…" in a text box, so a consumer sees staleness without needing to be told.
  4. A schema test so a renamed column fails the run loudly rather than producing zero rows quietly.
  5. A conversation with whoever owns the sales system about notice for breaking changes — the beginning of a data contract.

The lesson: incident response is 10% diagnosis and 90% communication and prevention. And "nothing failed" is not evidence that nothing is wrong — it is often the signature of the worst class of failure.

Checklist · a pipeline you can leave running
  • Every step is idempotent, and you have verified it by running twice and comparing.
  • Raw is append-only and never modified; every model is rebuildable from it.
  • Only the roots are scheduled; everything else runs on declared dependencies.
  • Incremental models reprocess a trailing window, and window functions are full-refresh.
  • A logic change triggers a rebuild, as a rule rather than a judgement call.
  • Deletes are handled — soft deletes, CDC, or key reconciliation.
  • A periodic full refresh runs as a drift safety net.
  • Every model's primary key has a unique test on it.
  • Volume, freshness, schema and distribution are monitored; alerts go somewhere people read.
  • At least one reconciliation test compares a total to an independent source.
  • Every test is explicitly a gate or a warning, decided per test.
  • Freshness is visible on every dashboard as text, not in a tooltip.
  • Every figure carries an "as at" date, and there is an agreed close date.
  • Backfills follow the checklist — announce first, verify idempotency, one partition, then chunk.
  • Every table has a named owner.

Module 10

Statistical reasoning without the maths

Six ideas. None needs a formula, all of them change conclusions, and the second one is the most useful thing in this course that nobody teaches analysts.

1 · The mean is usually the wrong summary

Almost every business quantity is right-skewed: most values are small, a few are enormous, and there is no upper bound. Order values, session durations, customer lifetime value, delivery times, revenue per account. For these, the mean sits somewhere above the bulk of the data and describes nobody.

StatisticWhat it tells youUse it when
MeanThe total divided by the count. Sensitive to exactly the extreme values you care about.You genuinely need the total — capacity, cost, budget. Rarely for describing a typical experience.
MedianThe typical case. Half are worse."What does a normal customer experience?"
p90 / p95The bad-but-not-freak case.Service levels, and the number the business usually cares about — the bad experience, not the average one.
The distributionThe shape. Bimodality, clusters, a spike at a sentinel value.Always look at this first. It is the only summary that cannot mislead you, and it takes one chart.
The habit

Never report a mean without having looked at the distribution first. A mean of 3.7 can come from everyone at 3.7 or from a population split between 1 and 5, and those are opposite situations. Two minutes with a histogram tells you which summary is honest.

2 · Common cause versus special cause

This is the idea that changes the most behaviour and is taught to almost nobody. It answers the question you are asked constantly: "the number moved — is that real?"

Two kinds of variation, and why confusing them makes things worse
COMMON CAUSE — the noise a stable process makes anyway
  Every process varies. Orders per day, conversion rate, delivery time
  — they bounce around a level even when NOTHING has changed. That
  bouncing is not information. It is the process being itself.

SPECIAL CAUSE — an assignable event
  Something genuinely changed: a release, a price change, an outage, a
  competitor, a season. This variation HAS a cause worth finding.

WHY IT MATTERS ENORMOUSLY
  Treating common cause as special cause is called TAMPERING, and it
  makes the process WORSE, not merely wastes time:

    Monday   conversion 3.1%  →  "why is it down?" → someone changes
                                  the checkout button
    Tuesday  conversion 3.4%  →  "the fix worked!"  → the change is
                                  kept, and credited
    Wednesday conversion 3.0% →  "why is it down?" → another change

  Nothing was ever wrong. The process was always bouncing between
  3.0 and 3.5. But you have now made three unvalidated changes,
  attributed a random rise to one of them, and added variation to a
  system that was stable. The dashboard CAUSED the damage.

HOW TO TELL THEM APART, PRACTICALLY
  Plot the metric over a long enough period — 30+ points — and look at
  the range it normally occupies. Then:
    inside the usual range        → common cause. DO NOTHING. Reporting
                                     it as a change is the error.
    outside it, or a sustained    → special cause. Investigate.
      run on one side of the level
    a step that persists          → special cause, almost certainly a
                                     system or process change

  → this is what a control chart formalises, and you do not need the
    formal version to get 90% of the benefit. Just plot the history
    and ask "is this inside the usual noise?"

THE SENTENCE THAT SAVES DAYS
  "That's within the normal range for this metric — I'd wait for a
   sustained move before investigating."

3 · Sampling, and how it fails

You often work with a subset — survey respondents, a sampled event stream, one region, the customers who answered. The subset is only useful if it resembles the whole, and there are specific ways it will not.

FailureWhat happens
Self-selectionPeople who choose to respond differ from those who do not — usually the delighted and the furious, missing the indifferent middle. Every voluntary survey has this.
Coverage biasYour sampling frame excludes part of the population. An in-app survey misses everyone who stopped using the app — which is exactly who you needed for a churn question.
Convenience samplingYou used the data that was easy to get. "We analysed the UK because that is where the clean data is."
Sampling on the outcomeStudying only customers who converted, only projects that shipped, only employees who stayed. This is survivorship bias, and it is the most common serious error in business analysis.
Truncated event samplingAnalytics platforms often sample high-volume days. Your "total" is an estimate, and it is an estimate that changes with volume — so trends can be artefacts of the sampling rate.
The question to ask of any sample

"Who is missing from this, and would they have answered differently?" If the answer to the second half is yes, you have bias rather than merely noise — and more data will not fix it. A bigger biased sample is more confidently wrong. That distinction between noise (which sample size fixes) and bias (which it does not) is the whole of this section.

4 · Correlation, causation, confounding

Everyone knows the slogan. What matters is knowing the three specific reasons two things move together without one causing the other, and how to phrase a claim you cannot prove.

Confounding

A third thing drives both. Customers using the mobile app spend more — but app users are also your most engaged customers, and engagement drives both. The app may have no effect at all.

Reverse causation

The arrow points the other way. "Customers who contact support churn less" — or customers who were going to stay were the ones invested enough to ask for help.

Selection

The groups differ because of how they were formed. Users who opted into a beta are not comparable to those who did not, and no amount of controlling fixes an unobserved willingness to opt in.

Coincidence

With enough variables, strong correlations appear by chance. If you test forty relationships, a couple will look convincing at conventional thresholds and mean nothing.

Best practice · how to phrase a causal claim you cannot prove

Do not write: "the app drives €40 more spend per customer."

Write: "App users spend €40 more on average. This is not evidence that the app causes it — app adoption is concentrated among already-engaged customers, so engagement is a likely confounder. What would settle it is a comparison of similar customers before and after adoption, or a holdout on the adoption prompt. I can do the first in two days; the second needs product's agreement."

That version is unattackable, it names the mechanism, it states the limit, and it prices the path to certainty — which hands the decision back to the person whose decision it is. It is also more persuasive than the confident version, because it survives the first challenge.

5 · Regression to the mean

Extreme values tend to be followed by less extreme ones, for purely statistical reasons. This single fact invalidates an enormous amount of everyday business analysis.

The trap that catches good analysts
  "We identified our 20 worst-performing stores and sent in a
   turnaround team. Six months later, 15 of them had improved.
   The programme works."

  It might. But the 20 worst stores were partly worst because of
  genuine problems and partly because of a bad run — a broken
  freezer, a local roadworks, an unusual month. The bad-luck
  component does not persist. So MOST OF THEM WOULD HAVE IMPROVED
  WITH NO INTERVENTION AT ALL.

  The same logic, uncomfortably:
   · the best-performing salesperson this quarter will probably do
     worse next quarter — and it is not complacency
   · a customer segment identified as "high risk" will look better
     next month even if you do nothing
   · praising after a peak seems to hurt; criticising after a trough
     seems to help. Both are regression, not management.

  HOW TO AVOID IT
   · compare against a CONTROL GROUP of similar stores that were not
     selected — the only real answer
   · or compare the selected group against its own longer-run
     average, not against the extreme month that selected it
   · and never select on an extreme value and then measure change
     on that same value. That design guarantees an apparent effect.

6 · Base rates and the false-positive paradox

The most counter-intuitive item in the module, and it comes up whenever anyone builds a model or a flag. Work it by hand once and you will never forget it.

A "90% accurate" model, worked through
SETUP
  5% of employees leave each year          ← the BASE RATE
  the model catches 90% of leavers         ← sensitivity
  the model correctly clears 90% of stayers ← specificity

  Sounds like a good model. Take 1,000 employees:

                          actually     actually
                          LEAVES       STAYS        total
  model says AT RISK          45           95        140
  model says fine              5          855        860
                          ───────      ───────
                              50          950      1,000

  arithmetic:  50 leavers × 0.90  =  45 caught, 5 missed
              950 stayers × 0.10  =  95 wrongly flagged

  ⇒ OF THE 140 PEOPLE FLAGGED, ONLY 45 WILL ACTUALLY LEAVE.
    45 / 140 = 32%.

  So when the model flags someone, there is a 32% chance they leave
  and a 68% chance they don't — from a model that is "90% accurate"
  on both measures. Nothing is wrong with the model. The rare thing
  is rare, so the 10% error on the huge stayer group swamps the 90%
  success on the tiny leaver group.

WHY THIS MATTERS OUTSIDE MODELLING
  It applies to every rare-event flag: fraud detection, quality
  alerts, anomaly monitoring, medical screening, churn risk. When
  the base rate is low, MOST ALERTS ARE FALSE — always, structurally,
  no matter how good the detector is.

WHAT TO DO ABOUT IT
  · report PRECISION ("32% of those flagged actually leave"), not
    accuracy. Accuracy on an imbalanced problem is meaningless: a
    model predicting "nobody leaves" is 95% accurate and useless.
  · set the threshold by the COST RATIO, not by a default. If acting
    on a flag is cheap and missing a leaver is expensive, tolerate
    false positives. If action is expensive or intrusive, don't.
  · tell the stakeholder the precision BEFORE they act on the list,
    because "here are 140 people at risk" and "here are 140 people
    of whom about 45 will leave" produce very different behaviour.

Significance, materiality and stability

QuestionWhat it asksWhen it applies
Significance Could this difference plausibly be chance? Only when you have a sample and want to generalise. On a full census — every order last year — there is no sampling error and the question is a category error. Asking for a p-value on a complete population is a sign of ritual rather than thought.
Materiality Is it big enough to act on? Always. With enough data, trivial differences become statistically significant — a 0.02pp conversion difference across ten million sessions is significant and worthless. Significance says "probably real"; materiality says "worth doing something about". Report both.
Stability Does it persist, or is it one strange period? Always, and it is usually more convincing than either of the others. A movement visible across six consecutive months needs no statistical test to be believed; a single-month spike needs one and still will not convince anybody.
Trap · the confident subgroup

You slice by region and channel and market and find that Dutch app users' margin collapsed nine points. It is 340 orders. One unusual order moves that number by four points.

The fix: set a minimum cell size before you start slicing, put it in the metric definition, and suppress or interval-bound anything below it. And when a small segment genuinely looks alarming, read the individual rows — with 340 orders you can, and it is faster and more conclusive than any test.

Module 11

The bias catalogue

Twenty-two ways analysts produce confident wrong answers. Grouped by where they come from: the data, your own thinking, and the organisation. Each with the counter-habit that actually prevents it.

These are not exotic. Nearly all of them feel like doing good work while you are doing them, which is precisely why a catalogue is useful — you cannot notice a bias by being careful, only by knowing its shape in advance.

Biases in the data

BiasHow it shows up in a real analysisThe counter-habit
Survivorship "Our availability was 96%" — because SKUs that could not be sourced were deactivated and dropped off the SKU list. Availability improved by deletion. Also: analysing only current customers, only projects that shipped, only employees who stayed. Ask what left the dataset, and when. Then find out whether it was deleted or merely flagged. This single question is the highest-yield item in the whole module.
Selection Comparing app users to non-users, beta participants to non-participants, trained employees to untrained. The groups were formed by a process that also affects the outcome. Ask how each group came to be in it. If people chose, you have selection, and no amount of controlling for observables removes it.
Sampling / coverage An in-app survey about why people leave, which by construction excludes everyone who already left. "Who is missing, and would they answer differently?" More data does not fix bias.
Measurement Delivery times are recorded for 82% of orders — and the missing 18% are disproportionately the late ones, because a delayed parcel is more likely to skip a scan. The metric understates, systematically. Ask what causes a value to fail to be recorded. If the failure correlates with the thing you are measuring, the metric is biased and coverage must be reported next to it.
Missing not at random High earners decline to state income; unhappy customers skip the satisfaction question. Imputing the mean pulls the distribution toward the middle and makes the bias invisible. Test whether missingness correlates with your grouping variable (Module 5). If you cannot rule out MNAR, report coverage and do not impute.
Right censoring "Average customer lifetime is 14 months" — computed only on churned customers, because the ones still active have no end date. You have measured the lifetime of the customers who left, which understates the truth badly. Never compute a duration only on completed cases. Use survival methods, or state it as "average lifetime among churned customers", which is a different and much weaker claim.
Left truncation / cohort age A retention table where the newest cohort looks catastrophic — because it has existed for three weeks and cannot yet have retained anyone for three months. Compare cohorts only at equal age, never at equal calendar date. Read the diagonal of a cohort table, not the last column.
Simultaneity Price and demand move together, so a regression says higher prices cause higher sales — because you raised prices when demand was strong. Ask whether the "cause" was itself chosen in response to the outcome. Very common in pricing and marketing-spend analysis.

Biases in your own thinking

BiasHow it shows upThe counter-habit
Confirmation The stakeholder suspects the new checkout is the problem. You find something in the checkout data and stop looking. The moment a finding matches what someone already believed is the moment to slow down, not speed up — and it feels like the opposite. Write down the hypotheses before querying, including the ones nobody likes. Then explicitly try to falsify your favourite. Ask: "what would I expect to see if I were wrong?"
Anchoring Someone says "I think it's about 15%" and your subsequent estimates cluster near 15%. Or the first number you compute becomes the benchmark you unconsciously validate against. Form your own estimate before hearing theirs. And when your number differs from an anchor, investigate the difference rather than adjusting toward it.
Narrative You construct a clean story — "customers churn because onboarding is too long" — and the coherence of the story becomes the evidence. Coherent stories are easier to believe than true ones, and the data usually supports several. For every story you like, write down a second story that fits the same data. If you cannot, you have not looked hard enough. Then find the observation that distinguishes them.
Hindsight After a bad quarter, the causes look obvious and the data seems to have been shouting them. It was not; you are reading the past through the outcome. Check whether the signal was visible before the outcome. Would you have flagged it in month two? If not, calling it obvious is retrospective.
Availability Three angry customer emails feel like a trend; the eleven thousand silent satisfied ones do not register. Vivid, recent and anecdotal evidence outweighs the aggregate. Ask "how many, out of how many?" of every anecdote — including your own. Quantify before reacting.
Sunk cost Three days into a modelling approach that is not working, you keep going because of the three days. Or you defend a dashboard nobody uses because you built it. Ask "if I were starting now, would I choose this path?" The time already spent is not recoverable and should not be an input.
Automation / tool trust The BI tool produced a number, so the number is right. A library returned a coefficient, so the model is fine. The pipeline said success, so the data loaded. Reproduce the headline a second, independent way. And remember from Module 9: "nothing failed" is not evidence that anything worked.
Streetlight effect You analyse what is measured rather than what matters. The company optimises web behaviour for years because it is instrumented, while the actual driver — the sales conversation — is recorded nowhere. Ask "what would I want to know if measurement were free?" and then explicitly note which of those things you cannot see. That list is often the most valuable part of a report.

Biases the organisation puts on you

BiasHow it shows upThe counter-habit
Motivated reasoning The finding implicates someone senior, so you soften it, add caveats it does not need, or quietly choose the definition that reduces it. Often unconscious and rarely deliberate. Fix the method before you see which way the answer points. Write down the metric definition and the exclusions first — then the answer is whatever it is and you have no room to negotiate with yourself.
HARKing
hypothesising after results are known		 You find an unexpected pattern in the data and present it as though you had predicted it. This inflates its credibility enormously — a confirmed prediction is strong evidence, a spotted pattern is a hypothesis. Say which findings were hypotheses and which were discoveries. "We went looking for X and instead found Y, which we have not yet tested" is honest and still valuable.
p-hacking / specification search Trying twelve segmentations, four date ranges and three outlier rules until something looks significant, then reporting only that. Each individual choice was defensible; the process was not. Decide the segments and the rules before looking. If you must explore, say how many things you tried — "we looked at fifteen segments and one stood out" is a completely different claim from "this segment stands out".
Metric gaming (Goodhart) The metric becomes a target and stops measuring the thing. Support closes tickets faster by closing them prematurely; sales hits volume by discounting away the margin. See Module 12. Pair every target metric with a guardrail that catches the obvious gaming route.
Deadline-driven certainty The deck is due tomorrow, so the caveats get dropped and the range becomes a point estimate. Pressure does not change the evidence, only how you describe it. Decide the caveats when you do the analysis, and put them in the deliverable at that moment — not at the end, when there is a deadline arguing against them.
Authority / seniority anchoring A senior person states a number confidently and it becomes the baseline you must disprove rather than one hypothesis among several. Treat every quoted number as having a source, and find it. "Where did the 15% come from — which report?" is a neutral, always-legitimate question, and it frequently resolves the whole disagreement.
The three counter-habits that cover most of the list

Write the hypotheses and the method down before you query. This one habit disarms confirmation bias, HARKing, p-hacking and motivated reasoning simultaneously, because it removes your freedom to decide after seeing the answer.

Ask what left the dataset. Survivorship, selection, censoring and coverage bias are all answered by this question, and it is the one nobody asks.

Re-derive the headline a second, independent way. Catches tool trust, arithmetic error and pipeline defects at once. If a bottom-up total and a top-down total agree, you have eliminated most of what could be systematically wrong.

Exercise 11.1 · 25 minutes

A colleague presents: "Customers who use our mobile app have 40% higher lifetime value. We should push app adoption hard." Name every bias in that sentence and say what you would ask for.

Show the solution

At least six problems, and the recommendation does not follow from the finding even if every number is correct.

  1. Selection bias — the main one. Nobody was assigned to the app; customers chose it. The people who install a retailer's app are already committed to that retailer. So engagement plausibly causes app adoption, and the app may add nothing. This alone is enough to withhold the recommendation.
  2. Reverse causation. The arrow may run backwards: high-value customers install the app because they shop often, not the other way round.
  3. Confounding. App users are likely younger, more urban, more digitally engaged and possibly newer — each of which independently affects lifetime value.
  4. Right censoring. How was lifetime value computed? If it is realised revenue to date, and app users have been customers longer, they have simply had more time to spend. If it is a projection, whose model, and does it use app usage as an input — which would make the comparison circular.
  5. Survivorship. Are churned customers in the denominator? If the analysis covers current customers only, and app users churn less, then the non-app group has lost its best members to the exit and looks worse than it was.
  6. Non-additivity, possibly. Is "40% higher" a comparison of means on a heavily right-skewed quantity? A handful of very large app-using customers could produce that entire gap. The medians might be identical.

What I would ask for

  • The distributions, not the means — both groups, side by side. This takes one chart and may dissolve the finding immediately.
  • Tenure-matched comparison. Compare app and non-app customers acquired in the same cohort, measured at the same age. If the gap survives, it is much more interesting.
  • The before-and-after within-customer comparison. For customers who adopted the app partway through their life, did their own spend change after adoption? This holds the person constant and is the strongest observational evidence available. Watch for regression to the mean here: people often install the app during a burst of activity, so compare against their longer-run average, not the month before.
  • The experiment, priced. A holdout on the app-install prompt for a random slice of customers. That is the only design that answers the question, and the useful move is to say what it costs: "four weeks, an estimated €X in forgone installs, and it converts this from an argument into a number."

What I would actually say to the colleague

The 40% is probably real as a description and doesn't support the
recommendation yet — app users are self-selected, so we can't tell
whether the app creates value or attracts the customers who already
had it.

Two cheap checks that would move me: the same comparison within an
acquisition cohort at equal tenure, and the within-customer
before-and-after for people who adopted mid-life. Both are a day.

If we want to actually justify spend on adoption, a holdout on the
install prompt is the only thing that settles it — four weeks.

Worth saying: pushing app adoption might still be right for other
reasons. It just isn't justified by this number.

The last paragraph matters. The goal is not to defeat the recommendation — it may well be correct. It is to stop a causal claim being used as the justification, because if the adoption push happens and lifetime value does not move, the analysis is what gets blamed.

Module 12

Metric design

Choosing what to measure is a design act with consequences. A badly designed metric does not merely mislead — it changes behaviour, in the direction of the flaw.

Metric types, and what each one hides

TypeExampleWhat it hides
CountOrders, signups, ticketsGrowth in the base. 500 complaints is meaningless without knowing whether you had 5,000 or 50,000 orders. Almost every count should be a rate.
Rate / ratioConversion, churn, margin %The denominator, which is where nearly all disagreement lives. And the absolute size — a 40% conversion rate on 30 sessions is noise.
AverageAverage order value, average handling timeThe distribution. See Module 10 — on skewed data the mean describes nobody.
Percentilep95 delivery timeMuch less than an average does, which is why it is usually the better choice. Hides only the shape of the extreme tail.

Leading and lagging, input and output

Lagging / output metrics

Revenue, churn, margin, NPS. What you are ultimately judged on — and what you cannot directly act on. By the time they move, the causes are months old.

Leading / input metrics

Trial signups, time to first value, on-time delivery rate, sales activity. Things a team can actually change this week, which move the lagging metric later.

Why the distinction matters

A dashboard of only lagging metrics is a scoreboard: it tells you the result and nothing about what to do. A dashboard of only leading metrics loses sight of whether any of it is working. Every team needs one lagging metric it is accountable for and two or three leading ones it can move.

The test for a leading metric

Can a specific person change it within a week through a decision they are authorised to make? If not, it is not a leading metric — it is another output with a shorter lag.

Goodhart's law and designing against gaming

Goodhart's law

When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. Not because people are dishonest, but because any metric is a proxy for something you actually want, and the cheapest way to move the proxy is usually not to improve the thing.

TargetHow it gets gamed — usually without anyone deciding to cheatThe guardrail
Tickets closed per dayTickets closed prematurely, or split into several so each closes faster.Reopen rate, and customer satisfaction on closed tickets.

Best practice · pair every target with its guardrail

Before a metric becomes a target, ask: "what is the laziest way to move this number without doing the underlying work?" You will always find one, in about thirty seconds. Then measure that too, and put the two side by side.

This is the single most valuable contribution an analyst makes to how a company is run, and almost nobody is asked to make it. Volunteer it when a target is being set, not after it has been gamed for two quarters.

Trap · vanity metrics

Metrics that only go up and that nobody can act on: cumulative registered users, total pageviews ever, social followers. They feel like progress and they cannot fall, so they carry no information and support no decision.

The test: could this metric go down, and if it did, would somebody do something specific? If a metric cannot fall, it is not measuring anything. Replace cumulative counts with active or rate versions.

The metric spec — where the definition lives

Every certified metric needs a written specification: the formula, the numerator and denominator with every inclusion and exclusion, the grain, the timezone, the entity scope, how it aggregates (sum-over-sum, never average-of-averages), what it must not be used for, its owner, and its version history with the restatement impact of each change.

Visualization craft

Chart choice is not taste. It follows from how accurately human vision reads different visual channels — which is measurable, and which makes most chart decisions determinable.

What a chart actually is

A chart encodes data onto visual channels: position, length, angle, area, colour, shape. Every chart type is a particular choice of which channel carries which variable. And people read those channels with very different accuracy — so the choice of channel is the choice of how accurately your reader will perceive the data.

The truncated axis — worked, because it is the most consequential everyday error

The nuance most guides get wrong

"Always start at zero" is usually stated as an absolute rule, and it is not. It depends on the encoding:

Bars must start at zero, always. A bar's length is the value. Truncate the axis and the lengths encode nothing — as above, a 6% difference reads as sevenfold.

Lines may be truncated, deliberately and with a labelled axis. A line encodes change through slope, not magnitude through length. Forcing a zero baseline on a share price or a temperature series flattens genuinely important variation into a straight line, which is its own distortion. The obligation is to label the axis clearly and never crop so aggressively that noise reads as a trend.

So: bars, zero. Lines, judgement plus an honest axis. And if you find yourself truncating to make a movement look bigger, you already know the answer.

Sorting — the cheapest improvement available

What to avoid, and why

AvoidBecause
Dual axesThe single worst common chart mistake. Two y-scales on one plot let you produce any apparent relationship by choosing the scales — the correlation you see is an artefact of your axis choices. Use two charts stacked with a shared x-axis, or index both series to 100 at a common starting point.
Pie beyond ~3 slicesAngle and area are poorly read, so slices of similar size cannot be ranked. Above three categories a sorted bar is strictly better. A pie is defensible only for "one part versus the rest".

Colour, correctly

Colour has exactly four jobs, and the rule for each is different. Most bad chart colour comes from using one job's palette for another's.

JobThe ruleCommon error
Categorical
identity		 Distinct hues, assigned in a fixed order and never cycled. A colour belongs to an entity permanently — filtering the chart must not repaint the survivors. More than about eight series. Beyond that, no palette is distinguishable: fold the tail into "Other", or use small multiples.
Sequential
magnitude		 One hue, light to dark. Light means less, dark means more. A rainbow ramp. Rainbows have no perceptual order — viewers cannot say whether green is more than orange — and they create false boundaries where the hue changes fast.
Diverging
polarity		 Two hues meeting at a neutral grey midpoint, anchored at a meaningful zero (above/below target, gain/loss). Putting a hue at the midpoint, or using a diverging palette where there is no meaningful centre — which invents a polarity that does not exist.
Status

Best practice · never let colour be the only encoding

Roughly 8% of men have a form of colour-vision deficiency, decks get printed in greyscale, and projectors flatten everything. So: a legend for two or more series, direct labels where there are few enough, and a difference in position, order or shape as well as hue. If the chart stops working in greyscale, it was relying on colour alone.

And test it rather than reasoning about it — colour-blindness simulators are free, and the red/green pairing that fails most often is exactly the one business dashboards reach for by default.

Annotation — the most under-used tool in visualization

A chart shows what happened. An annotation says what it means, and it is nearly always the highest-value element on the page. Two sentences of text placed on the plot will do more for comprehension than any styling decision.

The title states the finding, not the topic

Not "Revenue by month". "Revenue grew 9% but margin fell 2.4 points." The title is the most-read element and it is usually wasted on a restatement of the axes.

Mark the events

A vertical line at the price change, the release, the outage. Half of "why did this move?" questions are answered by a labelled line, and it prevents someone investigating a known cause.

Caption the caveat, on the chart

"Excludes the joint venture. September is incomplete." Put it in the chart image, not the surrounding text — because the chart will be screenshotted and the surrounding text will not travel with it.

Always show the data cut-off

"Data as at 02:14, 2 September." One line, and it prevents the incomplete-period false alarm from Module 4 as well as the stale-dashboard failure from Module 9.

Dashboards

Most dashboards are not used. The reason is almost always that they are the wrong artefact for the job — so start by choosing the artefact.

Four artefacts, constantly confused

  5 SECONDS   — the glance
    "is everything normal?"

      twelve tiles is not a glance, it is a search.

    "what changed, and where?"

      last period, versus target — because a bare number is
      uninterpretable.

Communication & influence

Correct work that nobody acts on has produced nothing. This module is short and it is the highest-return material in the course per minute spent.

Answer first

Analysts narrate chronologically — here is what I did, here is what I found, therefore here is the answer — because that is the order the work happened in and because the method feels like the value. Executives read top-down: answer, implication, evidence, method. Delivering chronologically means your conclusion arrives at minute eighteen, by which time the room has formed its own view and is arguing with it.

The structure, and the memo shape
  1 THE ANSWER            one sentence. the finding, with its number.
  2 SO WHAT               what it means for the decision at hand.
  3 WHAT TO DO            recommendations, each with a value, an
                          owner, an effort and a confidence level.
  4 EVIDENCE              the two or three charts that support it.
  5 METHOD & CAVEATS      how you know, and what you don't.
  6 APPENDIX              everything else. Not presented — it exists
                          so you can answer a question in ten seconds.

THE FIRST PARAGRAPH IS 80% OF WHAT GETS READ. Write it last, and
write it as if it were the only thing they will see, because for
most of your audience it is.

Writing for the audience you have

To an executive

The consequence, in money or risk, and the decision you need from them. No method unless asked. Assume ninety seconds of attention and one screen.

To a functional expert

They know the domain better than you. Lead with the finding, then ask — "does this match what you see?" Their scepticism is your best quality check and they will spot the definitional problem you missed.

To another analyst

Method matters. Definitions, exclusions, the reconciliation, the residual. They will check your arithmetic, which is a gift — brief them adversarially: "your job is to find the reason this is wrong."

To a non-technical stakeholder

Lead with the consequence, hold the mechanism until asked, and end with the ask — they need to know what to do with what you told them. No jargon; not because they are not clever, but because jargon transfers the work of translation onto them.

Defending a number

You will be challenged, and challenges come in four kinds. Diagnosing which one you are facing determines the right response — answering the wrong kind is how analysts lose rooms they should have won.

ChallengeSounds likeResponse
Technical doubt"Does that include returns?"Answer precisely and briefly; the appendix exists for this. If you do not know, say so and confirm the same day. Never guess — one guess that turns out wrong costs more than ten admissions.
Different definition"That's not what my report says."Do not defend. Assume you are both right and find the definitional difference out loud: "that sounds like it is before freight — which did improve. Mine includes it. Both are real; here is the bridge." This converts a confrontation into a shared discovery.
Motivated resistance"I don't think that data is reliable enough."Do not argue about reliability in general — unwinnable, and not really the objection. Make it specific: "which part would you want tested? I can check that today." A vague objection either becomes a testable one or evaporates. Stay entirely neutral in tone.
Consequence resistance"Even if that's true, we can't change it now."Not a challenge to the analysis — a business constraint, and not yours to resolve. Accept it and re-aim: "that is your call. What the analysis says is that the current approach costs about €2M a year — so the plan should assume that continues." Never fight for a recommendation; fight for the number being understood.
The two sentences worth memorising

"I don't know — I'll find out and come back to you today." Then do, the same day. Analysts believe not knowing damages credibility; the opposite is true. An analyst who never says it is one whose confident answers cannot be distinguished from their guesses, and everyone eventually works that out.

"Possibly neither." For when two numbers disagree. A difference between two numbers is a question, not a contest: name the two or three choices that would make them differ — grouping granularity, period, definition — and say which you think it is. Have those ready for every headline you present, because this question is asked in most delivery meetings.

Saying no, and writing so a screenshot survives

Accept and price it

Never a flat no, and never a silent yes. "Yes, adding that is useful. It's about a day because of the currency handling — instead of the category breakdown, or in addition, moving delivery to Thursday?" You have not refused; you have made the cost visible. People almost always withdraw it.

Offer the cheaper version

"The full version is two weeks. There's a version covering the common case in two days — it wouldn't handle multi-year ranges. Enough for now?" Almost always yes. The single most valuable sentence an analyst can say to a stakeholder.

Decline with a route

When it genuinely cannot be done, one paragraph: what is missing, why, and the nearest thing you can deliver, with a date. A good decline is never just a no.

Write so a screenshot survives

Every chart carries its own title stating the finding, its caveat, and its data cut-off inside the image. Charts get screenshotted into messages and decks; the surrounding text does not travel. Assume every chart will be seen alone.

Module 17

Working as an analyst

The operating model, the relationships, and the part of the job that is a responsibility rather than a skill.

The request pipeline

Requests arrive faster than you can serve them, permanently. The throughput of an analytics function is set less by how fast it works than by how much work it declines — and nobody teaches analysts to decline, so they do not, and then they are at 140% capacity delivering things nobody reads.

Give every request one of four verdicts, explicitly: take it (real decision, real deadline, you are the right person), answer now (it is a five-minute lookup, or the answer already exists — do not turn a lookup into a project), reduce it (offer a smaller thing, never a later date, first), or decline it (no decision depends on it, or someone else owns it, or the data does not exist).

The full intake and scoping process — finding the decision behind the request, the real deadline versus the stated one, the estimate with a named unknown, the charter — is Stages 00 and 01 of docs/data-analyst-project-playbook.html. This course does not repeat it.

Best practice · publish your triage

Make the queue and the verdicts visible — a board, a channel, anything. It is the highest-leverage political act available to an analyst: it shows you are not ignoring anyone, it establishes that requests get assessed rather than absorbed, and it surfaces the duplicate cluster. On any real queue you will find that a third of the requests are the same question in different words, which usually means one metric definition nobody agrees on.

Self-service versus bespoke

Why self-service is the goal

An analyst answering "can you pull…" forty times a month is a bottleneck and is not doing analysis. Certified tables and governed metrics let people answer their own questions, which frees you for the work only you can do.

Why it usually fails

Because self-service is given as a tool without the definitions. People then build their own metrics, get different numbers, and trust in data collapses. Self-service without a semantic layer produces disagreement at scale.

The split that works

Certified metrics and clean tables are self-service. Interpretation is not. Give people a well-modelled mart with documented, governed measures — and keep "why did this happen" as analyst work, because that is where judgement is required.

The underrated deliverable

A clean, certified table handed to a domain expert who then pivots it themselves. It costs you nothing if the table already exists, transfers the last mile to someone who understands the business better than you, and usually produces better questions than a dashboard would.

Documentation that gets read

Most data documentation is unread because it describes structure — "this column contains the customer identifier" — which the reader could already see. Useful documentation answers the questions a person actually has:

Write this downBecause
The grain, in one sentence"One row per order line per shipment." The single most useful sentence you can write about any table.
What it is for, and what it must not be used forA table description that only says what a table contains will be misused. Naming the wrong uses prevents them.
The exclusions"Excludes internal channel, test orders and the joint venture." This is what makes your number reproducible by someone else.
Known limitations and quirks"Cost is missing for ~3% of drop-ship lines." Whatever surprised you will surprise the next person; write it down the day it surprises you.
Restatement behaviour"Returns are booked back to the original period, so figures move for up to 60 days." The answer to "why did last month change?", written once.
The ownerSo there is a person, not a wiki page, when something breaks.

Working with data engineers

Ask for the mechanism, not the fix

Not "can you add a column". Say what you are trying to answer and what you found: "I need to know department as at the event date, and the dimension is overwriting — is a type 2 possible?" They will often propose something better than what you would have asked for.

Bring evidence, sized

"412 order lines, €18,200, 0.004% of revenue, growing about 30 a week." A quantified, trended problem gets prioritised. "The data looks wrong" does not.

Understand their constraints

They may not control the source. A schema change may need another team's release. Backfilling may be expensive. Knowing this stops you asking for impossible things and makes you the analyst they want to work with.

Own the semantic layer

The natural split: engineering owns getting data in reliably; analytics owns what it means. Metric definitions, marts and documentation are yours. Claim that territory — it is where your leverage is, and engineers are usually glad not to arbitrate business definitions.

Ethics and privacy — the part that is an obligation

You have access to data about people who did not choose to be analysed by you. This section is short because the rules are few, and it matters more than anything else in the course.

PrincipleIn practice
Minimum cell size Never publish a statistic for a group small enough to identify individuals. "Attrition in the Berlin design team: 33%" with three people in it has told everyone which person left. Set a floor — commonly five or ten — and suppress below it. This applies to every slice, and it is the rule most commonly broken by accident, through a filter combination nobody anticipated.
Differencing is a real leak Two aggregates that each respect the floor can reveal an individual by subtraction — "team of 12" and "team of 11 excluding managers" identifies the manager. Suppressing single cells is not sufficient once someone can combine views.
Purpose limitation Data collected for one purpose is not automatically available for another. An address collected to ship an order is not there for you to segment by neighbourhood affluence. Technical access is not authorisation — the query running is not the same as being permitted to run it.
Special-category data Health, ethnicity, religion, sexuality, union membership, biometrics. A stricter regime almost everywhere. Do not join it, export it, or infer it without an explicit lawful basis established by someone qualified to establish one. And note that inferring a protected characteristic creates it as data — segmenting by a therapeutic class infers a medical condition.
Proxy discrimination Excluding a protected attribute does not make a model or a rule fair. Postcode proxies for ethnicity and income; device type proxies for wealth; name proxies for national origin. A rule built on proxies discriminates while appearing neutral — and "we didn't use race" is not a defence.
Aggregate by default Ask what the least identifying data that answers the question is, and use that. Most analyses that people run at individual level do not need to be.
Know your trip-wire Every regulated industry has a finding that creates an immediate legal obligation the moment you see it — a suspected adverse drug reaction, suspicious financial activity, a safety incident, a safeguarding disclosure. Find out what yours is, and who to tell, before you start reading free text. "I didn't know that was my job" is not a defence the company can offer a regulator.
The sentence to have ready

"I can do that technically, but I don't think I should without checking — it joins prescription detail to marketing data at individual level, and I'd want the DPO's view first."

Saying this costs you an hour and occasionally a small amount of goodwill. Not saying it can cost someone their privacy and your employer a regulatory investigation. Deliberately not requesting access to data you do not need — and documenting that you did not — is a professional act, and it reads extremely well if anyone ever reviews the project.

What actually separates levels — it is not tooling

Almost nobody is promoted for knowing another tool. The transitions above are all about what you do before and after the query: finding the real question, knowing whether the data can bear the conclusion, choosing what not to do, and making a finding land. That is why this course has almost no code in it.

What to practise versus what to read

Read once, refer back

The bias catalogue. The quality dimensions. Chart choice. The CDC mechanisms. Dimension type names. These are reference material — you look them up when you need them, and re-reading them does not build skill.

Practise until automatic

The grain test. The top-25-values check. Plotting row count by month. Reconciling to an independent source. Asking "who is missing from this data?" These are habits, and habits only form by repetition on real data.

Practise out loud

Explaining a finding to someone non-technical. Saying "I don't know." Declining a request with a route. Defending a number under challenge. You cannot learn these by reading, and they are what decides whether your work has effect.

Do not spend time on

Collecting tools. A fourth BI product, another orchestrator, a certification in something you have not used. Tooling anxiety consumes the months that should go into grain, reconciliation and writing — and adverts list tools because tools are easy to list, not because they are what the work needs.

Portfolio projects that show judgement

The standard analyst portfolio is a clean Kaggle dataset, a correlation heatmap and a conclusion. It demonstrates that you can use a library. It does not demonstrate any of the judgement this course is about — because the hard parts were removed before you got the file.

Instead, build thisWhat it proves
A data-quality report on a genuinely messy public dataset — government open data is ideal, because it is real and unpolishedThe profiling battery, the sentinel catalogue, quantifying twice, and the discipline to say "this dataset cannot answer that question". Almost nobody does this, and it is the most differentiating thing you can show.
A small pipeline with tests, running on a scheduleLayering, idempotency, freshness, a reconciliation test. That you understand data as something that arrives repeatedly rather than as a file.
A metric definition document for something contested — "active user", "on-time delivery"That you know the definition is the deliverable. Include the competing definitions and the bridge between them.
A one-page memo answering a real question, answer-first, with caveatsCommunication, which is the scarcest of all these skills and the easiest to demonstrate.
A chart makeover — a bad published chart, rebuilt, with the reasoning written outThat chart choice is a principle for you rather than a preference.
The portfolio principle

Show the mess. A project that says "I found the source had 12% disguised nulls, reconciled to 0.1% of the published total, and concluded the regional breakdown was unusable" demonstrates more judgement than a flawless model on a clean file. Analysts are hired for what they do when the data is bad, because the data is always bad.

One last thing

Eighteen modules, and exactly one of them is about drawing charts. Two are about statistics. Six are about data being wrong and the machinery that carries it. That ratio is not an accident of how this course was written — it is the job.

The analysts who become indispensable are not the ones with the best tooling. They are the ones who know what one row means, who find out that the data is dirty before someone else does, who can say what left the dataset, who reconcile to something nobody in the argument controls, and who can make a finding land in a room without anyone having to lose. None of that is taught in a SQL course, and all of it is learnable.

If you keep three things from all of this

Write down the grain in one sentence. It is the concept most errors trace back to, and stating it costs eight seconds.

Reconcile to a source nobody in the argument controls. It is the only check that tests whether you are right rather than merely consistent.

Ask what left the dataset. Survivorship, selection, censoring and coverage bias are all answered by that question, and almost nobody asks it.
