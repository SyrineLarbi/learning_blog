Part I

The map

Foundation 01

What the job actually is

AI engineering is building products on models somebody else trained. That single sentence separates it from everything adjacent, and it explains why the skill set is what it is.

RoleOwnsCore skill
ML researcherNew model architectures and training methodsDeep maths, enormous compute. A few thousand people worldwide.
ML engineerTraining and serving models on your own dataPipelines, features, deployment, scale.
Data scientistExtracting signal, proving it is realStatistics, experiments, framing.
AI engineerProducts built on foundation modelsPrompting, retrieval, tools, evals, cost, safety. Mostly software engineering plus a specific kind of judgement.
The good news, and the trap in it

You do not need to understand transformers mathematically to be excellent at this job. You need to understand what the model can and cannot do, and how to build a system around something that is usually right. That is closer to distributed-systems thinking than to machine learning.

The trap is the inverse: because the barrier to a demo is so low, the field is full of people who can build one and cannot make it reliable, cheap, measurable or safe. That gap is the entire career.

Foundation 02

How we got here — in one page

Not history for its own sake. Each step explains a behaviour you will hit.

The four ideas that produced modern LLMs
1  STATISTICAL LANGUAGE MODELS (n-grams)
   "predict the next word from the previous two or three."
   Worked a little. Could not remember anything further back, and
   had no notion that two different words might mean the same thing.

2  WORD EMBEDDINGS (~2013)
   represent each word as a VECTOR, learned so that words used in
   similar contexts land near each other.
   ⭐ suddenly "king" and "queen" are geometrically related.
   MEANING BECAME MATHEMATICS. This is the ancestor of every
   embedding you will use — see Foundation 11.

3  SEQUENCE MODELS (RNN / LSTM)
   process words in ORDER, carrying a hidden memory.
   ⚠ two fatal limits: strictly sequential, so you cannot
     parallelise training; and the memory fades, so long-range
     dependencies are lost.

4  THE TRANSFORMER (2017) — ATTENTION
   drop recurrence entirely. Let EVERY position look directly at
   EVERY other position, weighting by learned relevance.
   ⇒ fully parallelisable  → you can train on far more data
   ⇒ no memory decay       → long-range dependencies work
   ⭐ THIS IS THE UNLOCK. Everything since is mostly scale plus
     training technique on top of this one architecture.

THEN: SCALE
   same architecture, more parameters, more data, more compute.
   And capabilities appeared that nobody explicitly trained for —
   translation, arithmetic, code, following instructions. That
   surprise is why the field moved the way it did.
Part II

What a model is

Four mechanics. They explain almost every surprising behaviour you will encounter.

Foundation 03

Tokens and next-token prediction

The entire mechanism
STEP 1 — TEXT BECOMES TOKENS
  the model never sees letters. text is split into TOKENS —
  roughly ¾ of a word in English, but model-specific and not
  word-based.

    "unbelievable"  →  un | believ | able        (3 tokens)
    "Larbi"         →  L | arb | i                (3)
    "1234567"       →  splits unevenly

  ⭐ THIS EXPLAINS THE "STUPID" FAILURES. Counting letters in a
    word, reversing a string, precise character manipulation — the
    model does not see characters, it sees token ids. It is not
    being careless; it structurally cannot see what you are asking
    about.
  ⚠ and non-English text costs MORE per unit of meaning, because it
    splits into more tokens.

STEP 2 — PREDICT THE NEXT TOKEN
  given every token so far, produce a probability distribution over
  what comes next. pick one. append it. repeat.

    "The capital of France is" → { " Paris": 0.94, " a": 0.01, ...}

  THAT IS THE WHOLE THING. Everything the model appears to do —
  reasoning, coding, summarising — is this, repeated.

⭐ THREE CONSEQUENCES YOU WILL FEEL EVERY DAY

  · READING is parallel; WRITING is serial.
    the model processes your whole prompt at once (fast, and
    cacheable) but generates one token at a time (slow).
    ⇒ OUTPUT LENGTH DOMINATES LATENCY, and output tokens cost
      roughly 5× input. "Answer in one sentence" is a genuine
      performance and cost control.

  · IT CANNOT REVISE. once a token is emitted it is part of the
    context and conditions everything after it. This is exactly
    what "thinking" gives it — a place to work before committing.

  · IT IS NOT LOOKING ANYTHING UP. there is no database inside.
    See Foundation 08.
Foundation 04

Attention, plainly

The one architectural idea worth understanding, with no maths.

THE PROBLEM ATTENTION SOLVES "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too big." What does "it" refer to? To answer, the model must connect "it" back to "trophy" — six words earlier. Change one word: "The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it was too small." Now "it" means the SUITCASE. Same sentence shape, different referent, and only meaning decides which. HOW ATTENTION HANDLES IT when processing "it", the model asks every other position: "how relevant are you to me, right now?" The trophy didn't fit in the suitcase because it · ███ · · · · ██ · ▲ low HIGH low low low low MEDIUM low (here) it then builds its representation of "it" as a weighted blend, dominated by whatever it attended to most. The weights are LEARNED — nobody wrote a rule about pronouns. WHY THIS WAS THE UNLOCK · every position reaches every other DIRECTLY — no fading memory · all positions computed IN PARALLEL — trainable at huge scale · relevance is learned from data, not hand-specified WHAT IT COSTS every position attending to every other is QUADRATIC in the sequence length. Double the context, quadruple the work. ⇒ this is why context windows were historically small, and why long contexts remain expensive even now that they are large. ⚠ AND A PRACTICAL CONSEQUENCE: attention is not uniform. Material in the MIDDLE of a very long prompt gets less weight than material at the beginning or end. Put the critical instruction and the most relevant document near the ends.
Foundation 05

How the model was trained

Three stages. Knowing them explains why the model behaves helpfully, why it has a knowledge cutoff, and why it sometimes refuses.

Pretraining → instruction tuning → preference tuning
STAGE 1 · PRETRAINING            months · enormous compute
  predict the next token, over an enormous corpus of text.
  no human labelled any of it — the "label" IS the next word.
  (this is SELF-SUPERVISED learning.)

  what it produces: a model with a vast amount of knowledge and
  language ability, that does NOT follow instructions. Ask it a
  question and it might continue with more questions, because that
  is what text often does.

  ⭐ THIS STAGE IS WHERE THE KNOWLEDGE CUTOFF COMES FROM. The corpus
    stops at a date. The model does not know what it does not know
    about anything after it — and will answer confidently anyway.

STAGE 2 · INSTRUCTION TUNING (SFT)      weeks
  fine-tune on curated examples of instruction → good response.
  now it answers questions instead of continuing them.

  ⇒ this is why it behaves like an assistant at all. That behaviour
    was TRAINED IN, not emergent.

STAGE 3 · PREFERENCE TUNING (RLHF / DPO)   weeks
  humans compare pairs of responses; the model is tuned toward the
  preferred ones.

  ⇒ this produces the tone, the helpfulness, the hedging, the
    formatting habits — and the refusals.

⭐ WHAT THIS EXPLAINS, PRACTICALLY
  · "why does it write like that?" — stage 3.
  · "why won't it answer?" — stage 3. a refusal is a trained
    behaviour, not a lookup against a rule list.
  · "why is it confident about things it can't know?" — stage 1
    gave it knowledge with no timestamp and no uncertainty signal.
  · "why does it follow my instructions at all?" — stage 2.

⚠ AND WHAT IT MEANS FOR YOU: you are working with a model whose
  behaviour was shaped by choices you did not make and cannot see.
  Fine-tuning (Foundation 14) adds a fourth stage on top — it does
  not replace any of these.
Foundation 06

What "context" actually is

One budget, and no memory
THE CONTEXT WINDOW holds EVERYTHING for one request:

  system prompt + tool definitions + full conversation history
  + retrieved documents + the output being generated

  input and output share the same budget.

⭐ THE API IS STATELESS. The model remembers NOTHING between calls.
  A chat that "remembers" earlier turns does so because YOUR CODE
  resends the entire conversation every time — and you pay for all
  of it, every turn.

  turn 1:  send [system + msg1]                    → pay for that
  turn 2:  send [system + msg1 + reply1 + msg2]    → pay for ALL
  turn 3:  send [system + msg1 + reply1 + msg2
                 + reply2 + msg3]                  → pay for ALL

  ⇒ cost grows QUADRATICALLY over a long conversation. This is the
    single biggest surprise in the bill for chat products.

WHAT "MEMORY" MEANS IN PRACTICE — three different things
  CONVERSATION HISTORY  resending it. simple, costly, bounded.
  SUMMARISATION /       compress old turns so the window lasts
    COMPACTION          longer. lossy by design.
  EXTERNAL MEMORY       write facts to a store, retrieve them later.
                        the only one that persists across sessions.

⚠ AND THE FAILURE TO DESIGN AGAINST: when the conversation exceeds
  the window, something must go. If your code silently truncates,
  you have removed information without telling anyone — and the
  resulting confusion looks like a model failure rather than a
  design one.
Part III

Capability

Foundation 07

What it can and cannot do

Genuinely good atGenuinely bad at
Summarising, rewriting, explaining, draftingArithmetic and anything auditable — it predicts plausible digits, it does not calculate
Extraction into a schema from messy inputExact reproducibility — same input, same output, forever
Classification where categories are fuzzy or numerousTabular prediction with labels — gradient boosting wins, cheaply and auditably
Translation between formats, languages, registersCharacter-level work — counting letters, reversing strings (see tokenisation)
Code generation and explanationKnowing what it doesn't know — no reliable uncertainty signal
Turning intent into structured action (as an interface)Anything after the knowledge cutoff, without tools or context
Working with unstructured text and imagesBeing the only guard on a destructive action
The architectural pattern that follows

The deterministic system computes; the model narrates. Calculate the number in SQL where it is reproducible and auditable, and have the model write the explanation around it.

Then the worst possible hallucination is a clumsy sentence rather than a wrong figure in a board pack. This single split covers a very large share of real AI features in business software, and recognising it is what separates a design from a demo.

Foundation 08

Why it hallucinates

Not a bug. A direct consequence of Foundation 03.

The mechanism

The model produces plausible continuations. It has no lookup table, no truth flag, and no reliable internal signal for "I don't know". An invented citation and a real one are produced by exactly the same process and look identical to it.

So it will always be possible. You cannot prompt it away — you engineer around it.

The six mitigations, in order of strength
1  COMPUTE IT ELSEWHERE   ⭐ strongest. anything numeric or
                          auditable never comes from the model.
2  GROUND IT              retrieval, plus an instruction to answer
                          only from the provided context
3  CONSTRAIN IT           a schema or an enum cannot be hallucinated
                          into. removes the failure rather than
                          reducing it.
4  VERIFY IT              check claims programmatically. if it cites
                          chunk ids, assert each was in the context —
                          a dozen lines, and it converts an invisible
                          failure into a caught one.
5  PERMIT "I DON'T KNOW"  explicitly. without permission the model
                          fills the gap, because that is what it is
                          for. one sentence measurably helps.
6  SHOW THE SOURCES       so a human can check. converts a silent
                          failure into a visible one.

⚠ WHAT DOESN'T WORK: "be accurate", "don't make things up", "only
  say true things". The model is not withholding accuracy. It has no
  mechanism to comply.
Part IV · the part you came for

The building blocks

Six techniques, each explained by mechanism, then a table for choosing between them.

Block 09

Prompting

What a prompt is made of, and what each part does
SYSTEM PROMPT     role, task, constraints, output contract, domain
                  vocabulary the model won't know.
                  ⭐ KEEP IT FROZEN — it is the cache prefix, and any
                    change invalidates everything after it. No dates,
                    no user names, no per-request anything.

FEW-SHOT EXAMPLES 3–5 input/output pairs showing the edge cases an
                  instruction can't express. Examples beat adjectives:
                  showing a 40-word answer works better than asking
                  for "concise".

DELIMITERS        <document>…</document> around anything untrusted,
                  with a statement that it is data, not instructions.

THE ACTUAL INPUT  last, because it varies per request.

⭐ THE HIERARCHY — in order of payoff
  1  the right CONTEXT      ← biggest win by far. most "bad output"
                              is MISSING INFORMATION, not bad wording.
  2  the right TOOLS        ← let it fetch what it needs
  3  a constrained SHAPE    ← a schema beats a plea
  4  EXAMPLES for edges
  5  the right EFFORT level
  6  rewording              ← smallest lever, most fiddled with

⚠ OBSOLETE: "think step by step". Current models reason by default,
  and explicit don't-think instructions actively make output worse.

⭐ DEBUGGING RULE: when output is bad, READ THE FULLY RENDERED
  PROMPT — the actual bytes sent, every variable interpolated. A
  surprising share of prompt bugs are visible immediately: an empty
  variable, a truncated document, retrieved chunks that are all
  irrelevant. Then ask: could a competent human do this task with
  ONLY this context? If not, the problem is context, and no rewording
  fixes an absence.
Block 10

Structured output

Stop parsing prose
THE PROBLEM   you need a machine-readable answer, and the model
              returns "Sure! Here's the JSON: ```json {...}```"

THE OLD FIXES asking nicely · regex extraction · prefilling the
              assistant turn with "{"
              ⚠ prefill is REMOVED on current models — it returns
                a 400. It used to be the standard trick.

THE FIX       declare a SCHEMA. the model is constrained to produce
              conforming output. you deserialise instead of parsing.

  ⭐ WHY THIS MATTERS MORE THAN IT SOUNDS: a constrained output space
    is also a HALLUCINATION control. If a field is an enum of seven
    categories, the model cannot invent an eighth. You have removed
    a failure mode rather than mitigating it.

TWO MECHANISMS, DIFFERENT JOBS
  STRUCTURED OUTPUT   the RESPONSE is a JSON object matching a
                      schema. for extraction, classification with
                      metadata, anything you deserialise.
  STRICT TOOL USE     the ARGUMENTS to a tool call validate against
                      a schema. for when the model should DO
                      something and you need the parameters right.

⭐ RULE OF THUMB: if you are writing a regex against model output,
  you have skipped a schema.
Block 11

Embeddings

Meaning as geometry
An embedding is a vector of floats representing text, where
SEMANTIC PROXIMITY becomes GEOMETRIC PROXIMITY.

  "cancel my order"     → [0.021, −0.113, 0.087, …]   768–3072 dims
  "how do I get a refund" → [0.019, −0.109, 0.091, …]  ← CLOSE
  "cold chain packaging"  → [−0.204, 0.331, −0.017, …] ← FAR

  two texts meaning the same thing land near each other WITH NO
  WORDS IN COMMON. that is the whole value, and it is exactly what
  keyword search cannot do.

MEASURING CLOSENESS
  COSINE          the angle between vectors, ignoring magnitude.
                  ⭐ the default for text — a long document and a
                    short query on the same topic score as similar.
  DOT PRODUCT     angle and magnitude. on NORMALISED vectors it is
                  equivalent to cosine and cheaper, which is why
                  most stores normalise on write.
  EUCLIDEAN       straight-line distance. rarely right for text.

⚠ THREE THINGS EMBEDDINGS ARE PREDICTABLY BAD AT
  NEGATION        "invoices that are NOT paid" retrieves paid
                  invoices — a sentence and its negation embed close.
  EXACT IDS       product codes, error codes, names. semantically
                  meaningless, so semantic search misses them.
                  ⭐ THIS IS WHY HYBRID SEARCH EXISTS.
  NUMBERS/DATES   "orders over €500 in March" is a FILTER, not a
                  similarity question. use metadata.

⚠ AND: changing the embedding model means RE-EMBEDDING EVERYTHING.
  Vectors from two models live in different spaces and are not
  comparable. There is no migration — it is a full re-index.

BEYOND SEARCH: deduplication · clustering · classification by
nearest labelled example · recommendation · anomaly detection
Block 12

RAG

Retrieval-augmented generation: fetch relevant context at query time and put it in the prompt.

WHY IT EXISTS the model doesn't know your documents, and its knowledge stops at a date. RAG puts the right information in front of it at the moment of the question. THE PIPELINE documents │ ① CHUNK split into passages ▼ chunks ──② EMBED──► vector store │ │ │ query ──embed──────┤ │ ▼ │ ③ RETRIEVE (top-k by similarity) │ │ │ ④ RERANK (a slower, better model reorders) │ │ │ ⑤ ASSEMBLE context + citations │ ▼ └───────────────► ⑥ GENERATE ──► answer + sources ⭐ THE SINGLE MOST USEFUL THING TO KNOW: when the answer is wrong, there are FOUR possible causes and you must be able to tell them apart — ① was it in the corpus AT ALL? (coverage) ② was it in the retrieved set? (recall) ③ did it survive reranking? (precision) ④ did the model use it? (faithfulness) Without this split, debugging RAG is guesswork — and teams spend weeks tuning chunk size when the document was never ingested. THE CHEAPEST QUALITY WIN PREPEND THE DOCUMENT TITLE AND SECTION HEADING TO EVERY CHUNK. A chunk reading "…must be refrigerated at 2–8 °C" is useless without knowing which product it describes. ⚠ BEFORE YOU BUILD IT, CHECK WHETHER YOU NEED IT context windows are now ~1M tokens. If your whole corpus fits, put it in the prompt and cache it — no vector store, no chunking, no retrieval bugs, and cache reads cost ~0.1× input. RAG earns its complexity when the corpus far exceeds the window, changes constantly, or needs per-user access filtering.
Block 13

Tools and agents

Tool use — the mechanism
You describe functions the model may call. It doesn't execute them —
it REQUESTS a call, you run it, and you return the result.

  1  you send: messages + tool definitions
  2  model replies: "call get_weather(location='Paris')"
     (stop_reason: "tool_use")
  3  YOUR CODE runs the function
  4  you send the result back
  5  model continues, now knowing the answer

⭐ THE MODEL NEVER RUNS ANYTHING. It asks. Your code decides whether
  to comply — and that gap is where all your security lives.

THE TOOL DESCRIPTION IS A PROMPT. It is how the model decides
whether to call it. Vague descriptions cause wrong tool choice, and
that is a DOCUMENTATION bug, not a model failure.
Agents — and the test for whether you need one
   ONE CALL          you write the prompt. deterministic.
      │              easiest to evaluate, cheapest, most reliable.
      ▼
   CHAINED CALLS     you write the sequence. each step evaluable
      │              separately. ⭐ MOST PRODUCTION VALUE LIVES HERE
      │                and it is deeply unglamorous.
      ▼
   ROUTED            a classifier picks a branch. still bounded.
      ▼
   AGENT             THE MODEL chooses the sequence. unbounded
                     paths, unbounded cost, every run different —
                     which is exactly what makes evaluation hard.

⭐ THE TEST: CAN YOU ENUMERATE THE STEPS IN ADVANCE?
  yes → pipeline. you get determinism, per-step evals, lower cost
        and debuggability, for free.
  genuinely no → agent, with caps.

WHAT MAKES AN AGENT OPERABLE (non-negotiable)
  · an ITERATION CAP — an unbounded loop is an unbounded bill
  · a TOKEN BUDGET
  · a FULL TRACE of every tool call, argument and result
  · a CONTEXT STRATEGY — tool results accumulate forever
  · TOOLS SCOPED TO THE CALLER, built from the session

⚠ AGENTS ARE THE MOST OVER-APPLIED PATTERN IN THE FIELD. Most tasks
  people build agents for can be enumerated, which means they are
  pipelines wearing a costume.
Block 14

Fine-tuning

THE LADDER — exhaust each rung before the next 1 BETTER PROMPT + CONTEXT minutes ← most problems die here 2 FEW-SHOT EXAMPLES minutes 3 STRUCTURED OUTPUT / TOOLS hours ← fixes most format pain 4 RAG days ← fixes most "it doesn't know our stuff" pain 5 FINE-TUNING weeks ← last, with a measured reason 6 TRAIN FROM SCRATCH don't ⭐ THE TEST BEFORE STARTING: can a prompt get it right MOST of the time? If no, fine-tuning usually won't fix it either. FINE-TUNING MAKES A MODEL DO RELIABLY WHAT IT CAN ALREADY DO OCCASIONALLY. It does not add a capability. GOOD FOR NOT THE ANSWER FOR style, tone, format ⚠ ADDING KNOWLEDGE — facts baked consistency at scale into weights are uncitable, stale domain vocabulary on change, unverifiable. USE RAG. making a SMALL model match ⚠ reasoning ability a large one on ONE task ⚠ anything that changes often dropping a long prompt ⚠ low volume METHODS LoRA / PEFT freeze the base, train small adapter matrices. cheap, fast, swappable, revertible. ⭐ the default. QLoRA LoRA on a quantised base. cheaper hardware. FULL update every weight. expensive, and risks CATASTROPHIC FORGETTING — better at your task, measurably worse at everything else. THE COST NOBODY BUDGETS: the base model improves, and your tuned model is stuck behind a better one you cannot use without redoing the work. Prompt-based systems inherit improvements for free.
Block 15

The choosing table

If the problem is…Reach for
"It doesn't know our documents"RAG — or the whole corpus in a cached prompt if it fits.
"The output format is inconsistent"Structured output. Not prompting.
"It needs live or private data"Tools.
"It doesn't follow our house style"Few-shot examples first; fine-tuning only at scale.
"It makes things up"Grounding + citations + verification. And move any computation out of the model.
"It's too expensive"Caching first, then input/output hygiene, then batch, then effort, then model.
"It's too slow"Stream, shorten the output, lower effort, parallelise.
"We need to know if changes help"Evals. Before anything else.
"The task needs many unpredictable steps"An agent — but check you can't enumerate them first.
"It's tabular data with labels"Not an LLM. Gradient boosting.
"The answer must be exact and auditable"Not an LLM. Compute it; let the model explain it.

The honest summary

AI engineering is 70% software engineering, 20% evaluation discipline, 10% knowing what models can and cannot do. If you can already build and ship a backend service, you are most of the way there — the missing pieces are on this page and are learnable in months, not years.

Beginner to expert

Foundation 18

The ladder

Level 1 · Beginner — "I can call the API"
CAN DO      make a request · get a response · build a chat demo ·
            follow a RAG tutorial
CANNOT YET  tell whether it works
THE GAP     everything you have built has been evaluated by looking
            at three outputs and thinking "that seems fine".

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: ship something to five real users. The gap
  between demo and product will become obvious within a week.

TYPICAL: days to weeks. This rung is genuinely easy now, which is
exactly why it is not worth much.
Level 2 · Builder — "I can ship a feature"
CAN DO      structured output · tool calls · a working RAG pipeline ·
            error handling · streaming
CANNOT YET  improve it deliberately
THE GAP     ⭐ NO EVALS. You change the prompt, it seems better, you
            ship. You cannot tell an improvement from a regression,
            so eventually nobody dares touch the prompt.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: build a 20-case eval set and put it in CI.
  This is THE rung transition in AI engineering, and it is a day's
  work that most people postpone for a year.

TYPICAL: 1–6 months. Most people building with LLMs today are here.
Level 3 · Engineer — "I can make it reliable"
CAN DO      evals with a held-out split · debug RAG across the four
            stages · caching and cost control · handle every failure
            mode · trace and monitor · reason about latency
CANNOT YET  judge whether the feature should exist
THE GAP     you can execute well. You are not yet the person
            deciding what gets built or where the boundary sits.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: two things. Say "this shouldn't be an LLM" —
  and be right. And design a trust boundary properly, so that an
  injection can succeed completely and still achieve nothing.

TYPICAL: 6 months–2 years. Solidly employable and in short supply.
Level 4 · Senior — "I decide what gets built"
CAN DO      everything above, plus: recognise a classical ML problem
            and refuse to LLM it · estimate cost at design time ·
            design the human review surface · put authorisation in
            code rather than prompts · know when a pipeline beats an
            agent · mentor
CANNOT YET  set how the organisation builds AI
THE GAP     your judgement is trusted on your projects; it is not
            yet the standard.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: build the thing that makes everyone else
  faster — the shared eval harness, the internal interface every
  model call goes through, the standard for what ships.

TYPICAL: 2–4 years, and it maps onto general senior engineering
judgement more than onto AI-specific knowledge.
Level 5 · Expert / staff — "I change how the org builds"
CAN DO      set the standard others work to · own the measurement
            system, not just a feature · say which of the last ten
            AI proposals should have been rules · make the
            uncomfortable call in the room where it is decided

NOT DEFINED BY  knowing more techniques. Nobody reaches this rung by
                learning a fifth agent framework.
DEFINED BY      judgement under uncertainty, and making other

The honest thing about this ladder

Rung 1→2 is API skills. Every rung after that is engineering discipline and judgement — evals, failure modes, cost, trust boundaries, knowing when not to use a model at all.

Which means the field's obsession with new frameworks is aimed at the least valuable rung. The fastest genuine progress you can make from level 2 is building an eval set, and it is a day's work.

The six-month plan

Assumes you can already write backend code. Built around shipping one thing properly.

MonthFocusDo this
1The API, hands-onMake twenty calls. System prompts, structured output, streaming, error handling. Deliberately trigger a 400 by sending a removed parameter, so the drift becomes muscle memory.
2One real featureSomething small and genuine — summarise, classify, extract. One call. Ship it behind a flag. Resist the agent.
3EvalsTwenty cases. Programmatic checks first. Into CI. Then improve the prompt and watch the number move. This is the month that changes how you work.
4Cost and cachingLog all four token counts. Restructure for caching and verify the cache read is non-zero. Compute the monthly bill at 10× volume.
5RetrievalBuild RAG over a corpus you know. Build the four-stage measurement first. Add hybrid search and a reranker and measure each separately.
6Tools, safety, opsTwo tools, then a small agent with an iteration cap and a trace. Then prompt-inject your own system through a retrieved document, and fix it at the authorisation layer rather than the prompt.

Portfolio: show the measurement

An eval harness with a documented golden set, a before/after score, and an analysis of the failure modes. Almost nobody shows this, and it is exactly what separates shipped from tutorial.

Portfolio: show the cost

"$0.004 per request, $240/month at current volume, down from $900 — here are the three levers." Real engineering, and rare.

Portfolio: show a failure

A case where the model was confidently wrong, why, and what you changed in the system so it gets caught. Judgement beats polish.

Don't collect frameworks

The orchestration library will change. Evals, retrieval quality, cost and trust boundaries will not. Build directly before you adopt an abstraction over it.
