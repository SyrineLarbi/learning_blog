Cheat sheet · 2 of 4 · data science

The model is rarely the hard part. Framing, leakage and evaluation are.

A dense reference for the concepts a data scientist is expected to hold: statistics without the derivations, the modelling decisions that matter, the evaluation metrics and what each one hides, and the specific mistakes that turn a good score into a useless model.

Scope · concepts, not library APIs Companion · file 2 of 4 Bias · toward what breaks in production
The three that cause most real failures

If you take three things from this sheet: leakage (your model is brilliant offline and useless live), the accuracy paradox (95% accurate and predicts nothing), and framing (you built a model for a question nobody asked). Everything else is craft; those three are the difference between a project that ships and one that quietly dies.

Section 01

Problem framing

The step that decides whether any of the rest is worth doing.

Before any modelling — answer all six
1  WHAT DECISION does this change, and who makes it?
     no decision → it's a curiosity, not a project
2  WHAT IS THE UNIT of prediction? (customer? order? customer-month?)
     this is the GRAIN, and it decides everything downstream
3  WHAT IS THE TARGET, precisely? "churn" is not a target.
     "no purchase in the 90 days after a 30-day observation window" is.
4  WHEN IS IT PREDICTED, and what is knowable AT THAT MOMENT?
     ← this single question prevents most leakage
5  WHAT IS THE BASELINE? (the current rule, the majority class,
     last year's value). If you can't beat it, you have nothing.
6  WHAT IS THE COST of a false positive vs a false negative?
     this — not accuracy — sets your threshold and your metric.

⭐ THE TASK-TYPE QUESTION
   supervised   you have labels             → classification / regression
   unsupervised no labels                   → clustering, anomaly, topics
   ranking      order matters more than value → recommendation, search
   forecasting  the target is its own past  → time series
   causal       "what happens IF we act"    → NOT a prediction problem
                                              (see section 07)
The most common framing error

Building a predictive model when the question is causal. "Which customers will churn?" is prediction. "Will this discount stop them churning?" is causation, and a churn model cannot answer it — a model that learns "customers who contact support churn less" will happily suggest you generate more support tickets.

Prediction answers what will happen if things continue. Causation answers what happens if we intervene. Different question, different method, different data requirements.

Section 02

Data & sampling

ConceptWhat to know
Population vs sampleYou almost never have the population. Every statistic is an estimate with uncertainty — and every claim inherits the sample's biases.
Sampling biasSystematic mis-representation. More data does not fix it — a bigger biased sample is more confidently wrong. This is the distinction that matters: noise shrinks with n; bias does not.
Selection biasThe groups you compare were formed by a process that also affects the outcome. Anyone who opted in is not comparable to anyone who did not.
Survivorship biasYou only observe what remained. Analysing current customers to understand churn; studying funds that still exist; measuring availability after deactivating unsourceable SKUs.
Measurement biasThe recording process is systematically wrong — and often correlated with the thing you measure. Delivery times missing precisely for the late deliveries.
CensoringRight: the event hasn't happened yet (customer still active — don't compute lifetime only on churned ones). Left: it happened before observation began.
Stratified samplingSample within subgroups to guarantee representation. Use it whenever a subgroup is small and matters.
MissingnessMCAR — unrelated to anything; deletion is unbiased. MAR — depends on observed variables; conditional imputation works. MNAR — depends on the missing value itself; nothing fixes it from inside the data.
The question to ask of every dataset

"Who or what is missing from this, and would they have behaved differently?" If yes, you have bias, not noise. Also: make an is_missing flag and check whether it correlates with your grouping variable — if it does, deletion is biased and you have evidence rather than a hunch.

Section 03

Distributions & descriptive statistics

Summaries, and what each one hides
CENTRE
  mean      sensitive to outliers. on right-skewed business data
            (revenue, duration, LTV) it describes NOBODY.
  median    the typical case. robust. usually the honest default.
  mode      the most common value. the only centre for categoricals.

SPREAD
  variance / std dev   in squared / original units. assumes symmetry.
  IQR (Q3−Q1)          robust to outliers. pairs with the median.
  MAD                  median absolute deviation. most robust.
  range                min–max. dominated by the two weirdest rows.

SHAPE
  skew        right-skewed (long right tail) = most business data
              → mean > median. log-transform often fixes the analysis.
  kurtosis    fat tails = extreme events more likely than normal
  bimodal     TWO populations mixed. Any single summary is a lie.
              ⭐ this is why you plot before you summarise.

POSITION
  percentiles / quantiles   p50 = median · p95 = the bad case
  z-score                   (x − mean) / sd. assumes roughly normal.

⭐ NEVER report a mean without having looked at the distribution.
   A mean of 3.7 fits "everyone at 3.7" and "half 1s, half 5s"
   equally well, and those are opposite situations.

⚠ AND: never AVERAGE A RATE across groups. Sum the numerators and
  the denominators, then divide. AVG(margin_pct) weights a tiny
  category equally with a huge one and can be many points wrong.
DistributionShows up asWhy you care
NormalMeasurement error, sums of many small effectsThe basis of most classical tests. Business quantities are usually not normal.
Log-normalRevenue, session duration, income, LTVRight-skewed. Analyse on a log scale and it becomes normal-ish — often the whole fix.
PoissonCounts per interval — arrivals, defects, clicksVariance equals the mean. If your counts are more variable than that, you have overdispersion and need a negative binomial.
Binomialk successes in n trialsThe distribution behind every conversion rate.
Exponential / WeibullTime until an eventSurvival analysis — time to churn, time to failure.
Power lawCustomer value, city sizes, word frequencyNo meaningful "typical" value. The top few dominate the total; means are close to useless.
Central limit theorem — what it actually says

The distribution of the sample mean approaches normal as n grows, regardless of the underlying distribution's shape. That is why you can build confidence intervals around a mean from skewed data.

What it does not say: that your data becomes normal. It does not. And with very heavy tails, convergence needs a much larger n than the "n > 30" rule of thumb implies.

Section 04

Probability essentials

The pieces that actually come up
CONDITIONAL      P(A|B) = P(A and B) / P(B)
INDEPENDENCE     P(A and B) = P(A)·P(B)  ⟺  P(A|B) = P(A)

BAYES            P(A|B) = P(B|A) · P(A) / P(B)
                          ↑          ↑
                     likelihood    PRIOR ← the part people drop

EXPECTED VALUE   E[X] = Σ x · P(x)
                 → the basis of every cost-sensitive threshold

⭐ THE BASE-RATE TRAP — work it once, remember it forever
   5% of employees leave. Model: 90% sensitivity, 90% specificity.
   Per 1,000 people:

                    ACTUALLY LEAVES   ACTUALLY STAYS   TOTAL
     flagged             45                95           140
     not flagged          5               855           860
                    ───────────       ───────────
                         50               950         1,000

     PRECISION = 45 / 140 = 32%

   A "90% accurate" model flags 140 people and 95 of them are fine.
   Nothing is wrong with the model. THE EVENT IS RARE, so the 10%
   error on the huge negative class swamps the 90% success on the
   tiny positive one.

   ⇒ When the base rate is low, MOST ALERTS ARE FALSE. Structurally.
     Report PRECISION, not accuracy. Tell the stakeholder the
     precision BEFORE they act on the list.
Section 05

Inference

Hypothesis tests, p-values and intervals — including the two definitions almost everyone gets wrong.

The two definitions to get exactly right

A p-value is the probability of observing data at least as extreme as yours, assuming the null hypothesis is true.

A p-value is NOT the probability that the null is true, nor the probability your result is a fluke, nor a measure of effect size. A p of 0.03 does not mean "97% chance this is real".

A 95% confidence interval means that if you repeated the procedure many times, 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true value. It is a statement about the procedure, not a 95% probability that this particular interval contains the truth.

ConceptWhat to know
Null hypothesisThe boring explanation — no difference, no effect. You never "accept" it; you fail to reject it.
Type I error (α)False positive — you claim an effect that isn't there. Conventionally 5%.
Type II error (β)False negative — you miss a real effect. Power = 1 − β, conventionally 80%.
PowerThe chance of detecting an effect that is really there. An underpowered test that finds nothing tells you nothing — absence of evidence, not evidence of absence.
Effect sizeHow big the difference is (Cohen's d, lift, absolute difference). Report this always. Significance says "probably real"; effect size says "worth caring about".
Multiple comparisonsTest 20 things at α = 0.05 and you expect one false positive by chance. Correct with Bonferroni (divide α by the number of tests — strict) or Benjamini-Hochberg FDR (controls the false-discovery rate — usually the better choice for exploratory work).
Which test — the practical map
COMPARING MEANS
  2 groups, independent      t-test (Welch's, which doesn't assume
                             equal variance — use it by default)
  2 groups, paired           paired t-test
  3+ groups                  ANOVA, then a post-hoc with correction
  non-normal / small n /     Mann-Whitney U (2 groups)
  ordinal                    Kruskal-Wallis (3+)

COMPARING PROPORTIONS
  2 rates                    two-proportion z-test
  contingency table          chi-square (needs expected counts ≥ 5)
  small counts               Fisher's exact test

RELATIONSHIP
  two continuous             Pearson (linear), Spearman (monotonic,
                             rank-based — robust to outliers)
  categorical                chi-square

⭐ SIGNIFICANCE vs MATERIALITY vs STABILITY
  significance  only meaningful on a SAMPLE. A p-value on a full
                census (every order last year) is a category error —
                there is no sampling error to test.
  materiality   with enough data, trivial effects become significant.
                A 0.02pp difference over 10M sessions is significant
                and worthless.
  stability     does it persist across periods? Usually more
                convincing than either. Six consecutive months needs
                no test; a one-month spike needs one and still won't
                convince anyone.
Section 06

Experiments (A/B tests)

Randomisation is the only thing that cleanly answers a causal question — which is why the design happens before launch, not after.

The design checklist — all of it before you ship
1  HYPOTHESIS      one, specific, falsifiable, written down
2  PRIMARY METRIC  ONE. decided in advance. everything else is
                   secondary and cannot be promoted later.
3  GUARDRAILS      what must NOT get worse (latency, margin, refunds,
                   unsubscribes). a win on the primary that breaks a
                   guardrail is not a win.
4  RANDOMISATION   at what unit? user > session, or the same person
                   sees both variants and contaminates the result.
                   ⚠ if users interact (marketplace, social) you have
                     INTERFERENCE — randomise by cluster/region.
5  SAMPLE SIZE     computed BEFORE launch, from:
                     · the minimum effect worth detecting (MDE)
                     · baseline rate and its variance
                     · α (0.05) and power (0.80)
                   → smaller effects need quadratically more traffic.
                     halve the MDE, quadruple the sample.
6  DURATION        at least one full business cycle — usually a week,
                   to cover weekday/weekend. Fixed in advance.
7  STOPPING RULE   fixed. see peeking below.
8  ANALYSIS PLAN   which test, which segments, written down first.

RUNNING IT
  A/A TEST FIRST   run the same variant against itself. if you find a
                   "significant" difference, your assignment or your
                   pipeline is broken. Catches most instrumentation bugs.
  SRM CHECK        sample ratio mismatch — expected 50/50, got 52/48?
                   Something is wrong with assignment. STOP. Do not
                   analyse. This is the single best experiment health check.
  NOVELTY EFFECT   returning users react to change itself. the first
                   days can reverse later.
Peeking — the error that manufactures results

Checking significance repeatedly and stopping the moment p < 0.05 inflates your false-positive rate far above 5% — with enough looks, a null test will cross the threshold almost surely at some point, purely by random walk.

The fix: fix the sample size and duration in advance and analyse once. If you genuinely need to monitor continuously, use a method built for it — sequential testing or always-valid confidence sequences — not repeated fixed-horizon tests.

When you cannot randomise

Often you cannot: the change is legally required, it affects everyone, it is a one-off launch, or randomising is unethical. Say so plainly, use a quasi-experimental method, and state the assumption it rests on. What you must not do is run a before/after comparison and present it as causal — that attributes to your change everything else that happened in the same period, including seasonality.

Section 07

Causal inference

What to reach for when an experiment is impossible.

MethodIdeaThe assumption it lives or dies on
RCTRandomise, compare.Proper randomisation and no interference. The gold standard because it needs no untestable assumption about confounders.
Difference-in-differencesCompare the change in treated vs the change in an untreated control over the same period.Parallel trends — without treatment, both groups would have moved the same way. Check it on pre-period data; if the lines weren't parallel before, the method is void.
Regression discontinuityCompare units just above and just below an arbitrary cut-off (a score, an age, a spend threshold).Units can't precisely manipulate which side they land on. Strong when it holds, and it only tells you about the effect near the cut-off.
Instrumental variablesFind something that affects treatment but affects the outcome only through treatment.The exclusion restriction — untestable, and usually the weakest link. Good instruments are rare and mostly come from natural quirks.
Propensity matchingMatch treated units to similar untreated ones on observed covariates.No unobserved confounders — a very strong assumption. It balances what you measured and can do nothing about what you didn't.
Synthetic controlBuild a weighted combination of untreated units that reproduces the treated unit's pre-period.Good pre-period fit and no spillover. Useful for one treated unit — a country, a store, a market.
Confounding, and the three structures to recognise
CONFOUNDER — a common cause of both. CONTROL FOR IT.
      Z
     ↙ ↘
    X → Y        ice cream → drowning, confounded by temperature

MEDIATOR — on the causal path. Controlling for it REMOVES the
effect you were trying to measure.
    X → M → Y    training → skill → performance
                 control for skill and training looks useless

COLLIDER — a common EFFECT of both. Controlling for it CREATES
a spurious association where none existed.
    X → C ← Y    ⚠ THE SUBTLE ONE. Conditioning on a collider
                   (e.g. analysing only hired candidates, only
                   hospitalised patients, only successful projects)
                   manufactures correlations out of nothing.

⭐ SO "CONTROL FOR EVERYTHING" IS WRONG. Which variables to adjust
   for depends on the causal structure, not on what improves fit.
   Draw the DAG before choosing the controls.

REGRESSION TO THE MEAN — select on an extreme, measure change on
that same value, and you GUARANTEE an apparent effect. "We fixed
our 20 worst stores and 15 improved" — most would have anyway.
Fix: a control group, or compare to the group's own long-run mean.
Section 08

Feature engineering

TechniqueForWatch out
Scaling
standardise / min-max		Distance- and gradient-based models: k-NN, SVM, neural nets, k-means, regularised regressionTrees don't need it. Fit the scaler on train only and apply to test — fitting on all data is leakage.
One-hot encodingLow-cardinality categoricalsExplodes dimensionality on high-cardinality columns.
Target encodingHigh-cardinality categoricalsA leakage machine unless computed inside cross-validation folds with smoothing. The most common way people leak without noticing.
BinningNon-linear effects in linear models; robustness to outliersThrows away information. Choose cut points from domain knowledge, not from the target.
Log / Box-CoxRight-skewed features and targetsUndefined at zero — use log1p. Remember to invert for interpretation.
Interactions & ratiosEffects that only exist in combinationTrees find interactions themselves; linear models need them handed over. Ratios often beat raw pairs.
Lag & rolling windowsTime-dependent behaviourMust be computed strictly backwards in time. A centred rolling mean sees the future.
AggregationsEntity-level summaries from event data — count, mean, recencyMust respect the observation cut-off. Aggregating over the whole history for a mid-history prediction is leakage.
The rule that beats most feature engineering

Domain knowledge beats cleverness. One feature an operator suggests — "was this ordered after the cut-off time?", "is this their first order since a complaint?" — usually outperforms fifty automatically generated polynomial terms. Ask the people who do the work.

And the practical corollary: build a baseline with the five obvious features first. It tells you how much headroom exists, and it is often 80% of the final model.

Section 09 · read this twice

Leakage

Information from outside the training context sneaking in. Produces excellent offline scores and a model that fails in production — and it is the single most common serious error in applied machine learning.

TypeWhat it looks likeCatch it by
Target leakage A feature that is a consequence of the target, or recorded after it. cancellation_reason in a cancellation model. days_since_last_login where inactivity defines churn. For every feature ask: "would this value be available, with this value, at prediction time?" That one question catches most of it.
Train-test contamination Scaling, imputing, encoding or selecting features using the full dataset before splitting. Fit every transformer inside the CV fold. Use a pipeline object so it is structurally impossible to do otherwise.
Temporal leakage Random splits on time-ordered data, so the model trains on the future to predict the past. Always split by time for anything temporal. Never shuffle=True.
Group leakage The same entity in train and test — several orders from one customer, several images of one patient. The model memorises the entity. GroupKFold on the entity id. Ask "what is the unit that must not span the split?"
Duplicate rows Exact or near-duplicates landing on both sides of the split. Deduplicate before splitting.
Leaky target encoding Encoding a category by its mean target, computed over all rows including the row itself. Compute inside folds, with smoothing and out-of-fold estimates.
The diagnostic

A suspiciously good score is a leakage alarm, not a success. AUC 0.99 on a real business problem almost always means leakage. So does one feature dominating importance, or offline performance that collapses in production.

When you see it, do not celebrate — go and look for the leak. The instinct to distrust your own good result is the single most valuable habit in modelling.

The order to try things

1 · A non-model baseline — the current rule, the majority class, last period's value. 2 · Logistic or linear regression — fast, interpretable, tells you if the signal exists at all. 3 · Gradient boosting — where you will probably end up on tabular data. 4 · Anything else — only with a reason.

Most projects that fail did not fail because step 3 wasn't tuned enough. They failed at framing, leakage, or because step 1 was never established and nobody knew whether the model helped.

Section 11

Deep learning basics

Enough to reason about it, choose when to use it, and understand what an LLM is underneath.

What a neural network is
A stack of layers. Each layer does two things:

   1  a weighted sum:      z = Wx + b       ← the learnable part
   2  a NON-LINEARITY:     a = f(z)         ← what makes it powerful

  input ──►[ layer ]──►[ layer ]──►[ layer ]──► output
            W₁,b₁        W₂,b₂        W₃,b₃

⭐ WITHOUT THE NON-LINEARITY the whole stack collapses to a single
  linear function, no matter how many layers. The activation is not
  a detail — it IS the reason depth buys you anything.

WHAT "LEARNING" MEANS
  find the W and b that minimise a LOSS on the training data.
  Nothing more mysterious than that.
The training loop — every deep model, without exception
   repeat for each BATCH, for each EPOCH:

   1  FORWARD PASS      push the batch through → predictions
   2  LOSS              compare predictions to labels → one number
   3  BACKPROPAGATION   the chain rule, computing how much each
                        weight contributed to the loss (the gradient)
   4  OPTIMISER STEP    GRADIENT DESCENT: nudge every weight against
                        its gradient, scaled by the LEARNING RATE.
                        ("stochastic" gradient descent = doing this on
                         a random batch rather than the whole set)
   5  repeat

  BATCH      how many examples per update. bigger = more stable
             gradients, more memory, fewer updates per epoch.
  EPOCH      one full pass over the training set.
  LEARNING   ⭐ THE MOST IMPORTANT HYPERPARAMETER BY FAR.
   RATE      too high → loss oscillates or explodes to NaN
             too low  → trains forever, or stalls in a bad place
             → almost always use a SCHEDULE (warmup, then decay)

⭐ IF A NETWORK ISN'T LEARNING, SUSPECT THE LEARNING RATE FIRST.
  It is the single most common cause, before architecture, before
  data, before anything else.
ComponentCommon choicesNotes
Activation ReLU, GELU, tanh, sigmoid ReLU/GELU are the default in hidden layers — cheap and they avoid the vanishing-gradient problem that killed deep sigmoid networks. Sigmoid and tanh saturate, so gradients die.
Output layer none (regression), sigmoid (binary), softmax (multi-class) Softmax makes outputs sum to 1 so they read as probabilities — read as, which is not the same as being calibrated.
Loss MSE / MAE (regression), cross-entropy (classification) Cross-entropy punishes confident wrong answers hard, which is what you want. Match the loss to the output layer.
Optimiser Adam / AdamW, SGD + momentum AdamW is the sane default. SGD with momentum can generalise slightly better with careful tuning — rarely worth the effort outside research.
Regularisation Dropout, weight decay, early stopping, batch/layer norm, augmentation Early stopping is the cheapest and most effective. Normalisation layers stabilise training as much as they regularise.
ArchitectureBuilt forKey idea
MLP
feed-forward		Generic tabular / vector inputFully connected layers. Usually loses to gradient boosting on tabular data — see below.
CNNImages, spectrograms, anything with spatial localityConvolutions share weights across positions, so a feature detector learned in one place works everywhere. Far fewer parameters than an MLP on the same input.
RNN / LSTM / GRUSequences — historically text and time seriesA hidden state carried along the sequence. Processes strictly in order, so it cannot parallelise, and it struggles with long dependencies. Largely superseded by transformers.
TransformerText, and increasingly everything elseSelf-attention: every position attends to every other, weighted by learned relevance. Parallelisable across the sequence, and handles long-range dependencies. This is what an LLM is. Cost grows quadratically with sequence length — which is why context windows were historically small.
Embedding layerTurning discrete tokens or categories into vectorsA learned lookup table. The first layer of every language model, and a good way to handle very high-cardinality categoricals in any network.
Transfer learning — the only practical path for most teams

Almost nobody trains a deep model from scratch. You take a model pre-trained on an enormous corpus and adapt it:

Feature extraction — freeze the pre-trained body, train a small head on your labels. Works with hundreds of examples. Fine-tuning — unfreeze some or all layers and continue training at a low learning rate. Needs more data and more care. Parameter-efficient (LoRA/adapters) — train a small number of injected parameters and leave the base frozen; the standard approach for large models.

The practical consequence: "we don't have enough data for deep learning" is usually false. You do not need enough data to learn vision or language from nothing — only enough to adapt a model that already has.

When deep learning is the wrong tool — which is often

On tabular data, gradient boosting usually wins. Not by a little, and not only on small data. Boosting handles mixed types, missing values and non-linearity natively, trains in minutes on a laptop, needs almost no tuning to be good, and gives you feature importances. A neural network on the same table typically needs more preprocessing, more tuning, more compute, and arrives at similar or worse performance.

Reach for deep learning when the input has structure a network can exploit — spatial (images), sequential (text, audio), or relational (graphs) — or when you can start from a pre-trained model. For a CSV of 40 numeric and categorical columns, use boosting.

Failure modes, and what each one looks like
LOSS IS NaN                 learning rate too high · exploding
                            gradients · a divide-by-zero or log(0) in
                            a custom loss · unnormalised inputs
                            → lower the LR, clip gradients, check inputs

LOSS DOESN'T MOVE           learning rate too low · dead ReLUs · labels
                            not aligned with inputs · bug in the loss
                            → ⭐ FIRST TEST: can it OVERFIT 10 EXAMPLES?
                              If it can't drive loss to ~0 on a tiny
                              batch, you have a bug, not a data problem.

TRAIN LOSS ↓, VAL LOSS ↑    overfitting → more data, augmentation,
                            dropout, weight decay, early stopping,
                            smaller model

BOTH PLATEAU HIGH           underfitting → bigger model, train longer,
                            better features, higher LR

GREAT OFFLINE, BAD LIVE     ⭐ suspect LEAKAGE (section 09) or
                            train/serve skew before anything else

VANISHING GRADIENTS         very deep nets with saturating activations
                            → ReLU/GELU, residual connections,
                              normalisation layers

REPRODUCIBILITY             GPU non-determinism means runs differ even
                            with a fixed seed. Report mean ± spread
                            over several seeds, not one number.

LeverEffect
L1 (Lasso)Penalises absolute coefficient size. Drives some coefficients to exactly zero — so it selects features. Good when you believe few features matter.
L2 (Ridge)Penalises squared size. Shrinks all coefficients toward zero without eliminating them. Good with many correlated features.

Section 13

Validation

Splits — and the ones people get wrong
TRAIN / VALIDATION / TEST
  train       fit the model
  validation  tune hyperparameters, select features, pick a threshold
  test        touched ONCE, at the end, to report an honest number

⚠ IF YOU TUNE AGAINST THE TEST SET, IT IS NO LONGER A TEST SET.
  Every peek leaks a little. The number you report becomes optimistic
  and you will not know by how much.

K-FOLD CV            split into k parts, train k times, average.
                     better use of small data, gives a variance
                     estimate as well as a mean.
STRATIFIED K-FOLD    preserves class balance in each fold.
                     ⭐ the default for classification.
GROUP K-FOLD         keeps all rows of one entity in one fold.
                     ⭐ required whenever rows share an entity.
TIME-SERIES SPLIT    train on the past, test on the future, rolling
                     forward. ⭐ NEVER shuffle temporal data.

   ┌──train──┐ test
   ├────train────┐ test
   ├──────train──────┐ test        ← expanding window

NESTED CV            an inner loop for tuning, an outer loop for
                     evaluation. The honest way to report performance
                     when you also tuned. Expensive; worth it when the
                     number will be quoted.

⭐ CHOOSE THE SPLIT THAT MATCHES HOW THE MODEL WILL BE USED.
  If it will predict next month from this month, validate that way.
  A random split on temporal data reports a score you will never see
  again.
Section 14

Classification metrics

THE CONFUSION MATRIX — everything derives from these four cells PREDICTED positive negative ┌──────────────┬──────────────┐ actual │ TP │ FN │ ← the ones you missed positive │ (caught) │ (missed) │ ├──────────────┼──────────────┤ actual │ FP │ TN │ negative │ (false alarm)│ (correct) │ └──────────────┴──────────────┘ PRECISION = TP / (TP + FP) of those I FLAGGED, how many were right? ← the cost of acting on false alarms RECALL = TP / (TP + FN) of those that EXIST, how many did I catch? (sensitivity, TPR) ← the cost of misses SPECIFICITY = TN / (TN + FP) of the negatives, how many did I clear? F1 = 2·P·R / (P + R) harmonic mean — punishes imbalance between the two. Fβ weights recall (β>1) or precision (β<1). ACCURACY = (TP+TN) / all ⚠ MEANINGLESS ON IMBALANCED DATA ⭐ PRECISION AND RECALL TRADE OFF VIA THE THRESHOLD. Lower the threshold → catch more (recall up), more false alarms (precision down). There is no "best" — the COST RATIO decides.
MetricUse whenCaveat
AccuracyBalanced classes and symmetric costs — which is rareThe accuracy paradox: 95% of employees stay, so "nobody leaves" is 95% accurate and useless. Never report it alone on imbalanced data.
PrecisionFalse alarms are expensive — spam filters, fraud blocks, anything that interrupts a personTrivially 100% if you flag only the single most confident case.
RecallMisses are expensive — disease screening, safety, recall listsTrivially 100% if you flag everything.
F1You need one number and both errors matterHides which side is failing. Report precision and recall too.
ROC-AUCRanking quality across all thresholds; comparing modelsOptimistic on heavy imbalance — the huge negative class makes FPR look small. Also: it measures ranking, not calibration or the operating point you'll actually use.
PR-AUCThe right default for rare positivesBaseline is the positive rate, not 0.5 — so 0.4 can be excellent or terrible depending on prevalence. Always state the base rate.
Log loss / BrierWhen you need the probability to be right, not just the rankingThe metrics for calibration.
Cohen's kappa / MCCBalanced single number on imbalanced dataMCC is the most robust single summary of a confusion matrix; harder to explain to stakeholders.
Calibration — the thing nobody checks

A model is calibrated if, among cases it scores 0.7, about 70% are actually positive. Ranking metrics (AUC) say nothing about this — a model can rank perfectly and have wildly wrong probabilities.

It matters whenever the probability is used as a number: expected-value decisions, pricing, prioritising by risk × cost. Check with a reliability diagram (predicted vs observed frequency, binned); fix with Platt scaling or isotonic regression on a held-out set. Boosted trees are often poorly calibrated out of the box.

Section 15

Regression & ranking metrics

MetricMeaningWhen it misleads
MAEMean absolute error — average miss, in the target's unitsTreats all errors linearly. Robust to outliers, and easy to explain: "we're off by €40 on average."
RMSERoot mean squared error — penalises large errors moreDominated by a few big misses. Use it when large errors are disproportionately costly; otherwise it just reports your outliers.
MAPEMean absolute percentage errorUndefined at zero and explodes near it. Also asymmetric — it punishes over-prediction more than under-prediction. Popular with business, dangerous with low-volume series.
sMAPE / WAPESymmetric / weighted alternativesWAPE (total absolute error ÷ total actual) is usually the honest choice for demand forecasting.
R²Share of variance explainedAlways increases when you add features — use adjusted R². Can be negative. Says nothing about whether predictions are useful in absolute terms.
Quantile lossFor predicting an interval or a specific percentileThe right metric when the business needs "the value we'll beat 90% of the time", not the mean.
Ranking & recommendation
PRECISION@K   of the top k recommended, how many were relevant?
RECALL@K      of all relevant items, how many made the top k?
MAP           mean average precision — rewards putting relevant items
              higher, across all queries
NDCG          normalised discounted cumulative gain — like MAP but
              handles GRADED relevance (very relevant > somewhat) and
              discounts by position. The standard for search.
MRR           mean reciprocal rank — how far down was the FIRST
              relevant result? Right when there is one correct answer.
HIT RATE      did anything relevant appear at all?

⭐ POSITION MATTERS. That is the whole point of ranking metrics:
  being right at rank 1 is worth much more than being right at rank
  20, and accuracy-style metrics cannot express that.

⚠ OFFLINE RANKING METRICS ARE MEASURED ON LOGGED DATA, WHICH YOUR
  OLD SYSTEM SELECTED. That is a feedback loop: you never see whether
  the items you never showed would have been clicked. Offline gains
  routinely fail to reproduce online — which is why recommendation
  teams A/B test everything.
Section 16

Imbalanced data

ApproachNotes
Do nothingTry this first. Modern boosting handles moderate imbalance well. Change the metric and the threshold, not the data.
Class weightsTell the loss function that a positive error costs more. Cheap, principled, no data invented. Usually the right first move.
Threshold tuningThe default 0.5 is arbitrary. Choose the threshold from the cost ratio on a validation set. Often the single biggest improvement available, and it requires no retraining.
UndersamplingDrop majority examples. Fast, and throws away real information.
Oversampling / SMOTEDuplicate or synthesise minority examples. Apply inside the CV fold only — resampling before splitting puts synthetic copies of test rows into training, which is leakage and produces spectacular fake scores.
Anomaly detection framingAt extreme imbalance (< 0.1%), stop treating it as classification. Model "normal" and flag deviations.
The reframing that usually solves it

Imbalance is rarely a data problem — it is a metric and threshold problem. Report precision, recall and PR-AUC instead of accuracy; pick the threshold from the cost of each error type; and give the stakeholder the precision before they act on the list. Resampling is the last resort, not the first move.

Section 17

Interpretation

MethodTells youCaveat
Coefficients
linear / logistic		Direction and size of each effect, holding others constantOnly comparable if features are scaled. Logistic coefficients are in log-odds — exponentiate for odds ratios.
Impurity importance
tree default		How much each feature reduced impurityBiased toward high-cardinality and continuous features. Fast and not to be trusted alone.
Permutation importanceHow much performance drops when you shuffle a featureModel-agnostic and honest — but splits importance arbitrarily between correlated features, so a genuinely important variable can look unimportant if a twin exists.
SHAPPer-prediction contributions that sum to the prediction — the only common method that explains an individual caseExpensive on large data. Still associational: a SHAP value is not a causal effect, however much the plot invites that reading.
Partial dependence / ICEThe shape of a feature's effect across its rangePDP averages over the data and can hide heterogeneity; ICE plots the individual curves and reveals it.
The sentence to keep saying

Feature importance is not causal effect. "Tenure is the top driver of attrition" means tenure predicts attrition — not that changing someone's tenure changes their probability of leaving. Stakeholders will make that leap automatically and act on it; saying the sentence out loud is part of the job.

Section 18

Time series

The structure, and the rules that follow from it
DECOMPOSITION      series = trend + seasonality + residual
                   (or multiplicative: trend × seasonality × residual —
                    use it when the seasonal swing GROWS with the level)

STATIONARITY       constant mean, variance and autocorrelation over
                   time. Most classical methods require it.
                   → achieve it by differencing, log-transforming,
                     or removing trend/seasonality.

AUTOCORRELATION    a value correlates with its own past. This is why
                   ordinary CV is invalid — the observations are NOT
                   independent.
  ACF / PACF       the diagnostic plots for choosing lag orders.

⭐ THE FOUR RULES YOU CANNOT BREAK
  1 NEVER shuffle. Split by time, always.
  2 NEVER use future information in a feature. Rolling windows must
    look strictly backwards; a centred window sees ahead.
  3 ALWAYS beat the naive baseline before claiming anything:
      naive        ŷ(t) = y(t−1)
      seasonal naive ŷ(t) = y(t−season)   ← surprisingly hard to beat
      drift        naive plus average trend
  4 BACKTEST with a rolling origin, not a single split. One split
    tells you about one moment; the business runs every month.

METHODS
  ETS / Holt-Winters   trend + seasonality, fast, strong baseline
  ARIMA / SARIMA       classical, interpretable, needs stationarity
  Prophet              handles holidays and multiple seasonalities,
                       tolerant of missing data, easy to run
  Gradient boosting    with lag/rolling/calendar features — usually
                       wins on many related series
  Deep (LSTM/TFT)      only with lots of data and lots of series

⚠ INTERMITTENT DEMAND (many zeros) breaks most of the above and
  MAPE entirely. Use Croston's method or a count model, and WAPE.

Unsupervised

MethodForWatch out
k-meansFast general-purpose clusteringYou must choose k; assumes round, similar-sized clusters; needs scaling; sensitive to initialisation.
HierarchicalWhen you want a dendrogram and no fixed kDoesn't scale to large n.
DBSCAN / HDBSCANArbitrary shapes; identifies noise points explicitlyStruggles with varying densities; parameters are unintuitive.

Evaluating clusters — there is no test set

Internal: silhouette score, Davies-Bouldin, the elbow method. They measure geometry, not usefulness. External: if you have some labels, adjusted Rand index. The one that matters: can a domain expert name each cluster and act differently on it? A statistically excellent segmentation nobody can describe is not a segmentation — it is a colouring.

Section 20

Production & drift

ConceptWhat to know
Training/serving skewFeatures computed differently offline and online. The most common production failure. Fix by sharing the transformation code, or a feature store.
Covariate shiftThe input distribution changed; the relationship didn't. New market, new traffic source.
Label shiftThe class balance changed — fraud rate doubled.
Concept driftThe relationship itself changed — the same inputs now imply a different outcome. The hardest, and only detectable once labels arrive.
Feedback loopsThe model's own actions change the data it is retrained on. You stop showing items nobody clicks, so they never get clicks, so they stay unshown. Needs deliberate exploration to break.
Shadow deploymentRun the new model alongside the old without acting on it; compare. The safest way to ship a model.
Champion / challengerServe a small share of traffic to the new model. Real feedback, bounded risk.
What to monitor — in this priority order
1  IS IT RUNNING?      request volume, error rate, latency
                       (the failure people forget: it stopped and
                        nothing said so)
2  INPUT DISTRIBUTIONS  per-feature mean/percentiles/null rate vs
                        training. drifts here precede everything else
                        and are visible IMMEDIATELY.
3  PREDICTION DISTRIB.  score histogram over time. a sudden shift
                        without an input shift means the pipeline
                        changed.
4  BUSINESS METRIC      the thing you were actually hired to move.
                        ⭐ the only one that ultimately matters.
5  MODEL PERFORMANCE    ⚠ requires LABELS, which arrive with a delay —
                        sometimes months. Plan for the gap: that is
                        why 2 and 3 exist.

RETRAINING
  scheduled   simple, predictable, often wasteful
  triggered   on drift or performance decay; needs the monitoring above
  ⚠ always validate a retrained model against the current one before
    promoting. "Newer" is not "better", and an automated retrain that
    ships unchecked is how a bad data day becomes a bad model.
Section 21

Fairness & ethics

ConceptWhat to know
Proxy discriminationExcluding a protected attribute does not make a model fair. Postcode proxies for ethnicity; device type for wealth; name for national origin. "We didn't use race" is not a defence.
Demographic parityEqual positive rates across groups.
Equal opportunityEqual recall across groups — among those who qualify, equal chance of being selected.
Equalised oddsEqual TPR and FPR across groups.
Calibration within groupsA score of 0.7 means 70% in every group.
The impossibility resultThese definitions are mathematically incompatible when base rates differ across groups — you cannot satisfy them all. So fairness is a choice you must make explicitly and document, not a box that gets ticked.
Feedback amplificationA biased model changes who gets opportunities, which changes the next training set. Bias compounds unless something breaks the loop.
Minimum cell sizeNever report a statistic for a group small enough to identify individuals. And beware differencing — two suppressed-safe aggregates can reveal a person by subtraction.
The professional obligation

Technical access is not authorisation. The query running is not the same as being permitted to run it. Health, ethnicity, religion, sexuality and biometrics sit under stricter rules almost everywhere — and inferring a protected characteristic creates it as data, whether or not it was collected.

The sentence worth having ready: "I can do that technically, but I don't think I should without checking — it infers a protected characteristic, and I'd want the DPO's view first."

Section 22

Mistakes to avoid

MistakeWhy it costs you
No baselineYou cannot say whether the model helps. A 0.82 AUC means nothing until you know the simple rule scores 0.79.
Optimising a metric nobody choseMaximising F1 when the business cares about precision at a fixed alert volume.
Reporting accuracy on imbalanced data95% accurate, predicts nothing. The classic.
Random split on temporal dataTrains on the future. The score is unreproducible in production.
Fitting transformers before splittingLeakage. Scores are optimistic by an unknown amount.
Tuning on the test setIt stops being a test set at the first peek.
Trusting a suspiciously good scoreAUC 0.99 on a business problem is a leak until proven otherwise.
Treating feature importance as causalStakeholders act on it. You are responsible for the framing you allowed.
Ignoring calibrationAny expected-value decision built on uncalibrated probabilities is wrong even when the ranking is right.
Reporting a point estimateNo interval, no variance across folds — so nobody knows whether the difference between two models is real.
Not shippingA model in a notebook has produced zero value. The gap between 0.84 and 0.86 matters far less than deployment, monitoring and someone acting on the output.
Skipping the "when not to model" questionThe best outcome of many projects is a rule, a dashboard, or fixing the data collection.
Section 23

Glossary

Supervised / unsupervisedWith labels / without.
Feature / targetInput variable / the thing predicted.
GrainWhat one row means. Decides what is answerable, and what one prediction refers to.
HyperparameterA setting you choose (tree depth, learning rate), as opposed to a parameter the model learns.
OverfittingLearning the training data's noise. Low train error, high test error.
RegularisationAny constraint that trades a little bias for less variance.
EnsembleCombining models. Bagging (parallel, reduces variance — random forest); boosting (sequential, reduces bias — XGBoost); stacking (a model over model outputs).
LeakageInformation in training that won't exist at prediction time.
Base rateHow common the positive class is. Determines whether precision can ever be high.
ThresholdThe cut-off converting a score into a decision. Choose it from costs, not from 0.5.
CalibrationWhether predicted probabilities match observed frequencies.
StationarityStatistical properties constant over time. Required by most classical time-series methods.
ConfounderA common cause of both treatment and outcome. Control for it.
ColliderA common effect of both. Controlling for it creates spurious association.
PowerProbability of detecting a real effect. Low power makes a null result uninformative.
MDEMinimum detectable effect — the smallest difference your test can find. Drives sample size.
SRMSample ratio mismatch — assignment didn't split as intended. Invalidates the experiment.
DriftThe world changing out from under the model. Covariate / label / concept.
Feature storeShared, versioned feature definitions used by both training and serving — the structural fix for training/serving skew.
Model cardDocumentation of intended use, training data, performance by segment, and limitations. Increasingly expected.
