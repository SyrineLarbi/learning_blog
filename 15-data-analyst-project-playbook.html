A job simulation on paper

Revenue is up nine percent. Margin is down 2.4 points. Find out why.

That is the whole brief. No dataset, no spec, no dashboard — one sentence from a CFO and three weeks until the board meets. This playbook follows that request through every stage a corporate data analyst actually passes, at an EU online pharmacy, and ends with an answer that survives the room.

Domain · pharmaceutical e-commerce Stages · ten, in order Tasks · 18, with worked solutions Traps · 60+, named

Most analyst training teaches the skills — window functions, cohort tables, confidence intervals, DAX. Then you land in a company and discover that the skills were never the binding constraint. The work that decides whether you succeed happens before and after the query: working out what was actually being asked, finding out which of the four tables called orders is the real one, discovering that the number you were hired to explain is defined differently in two departments, and then standing in a room where a Chief Commercial Officer does not want your finding to be true.

This document is the wrapper around the skills. It is written as a single project, followed end to end, because that is the only way the sequence becomes visible. Read it as a simulation: you are the analyst, the brief above is yours, and at each stage there is a task you should attempt before you open the solution.

How to use it

  1. Read it once, straight through, without doing the tasks. You are building a map of the sequence. Roughly two hours. The point is that you finish knowing what Stage 04 is for, which is the thing nobody tells you on your first job.
  2. Read it a second time and do every task. Write the answer down before you open the reveal — the gap between your answer and the solution is the actual lesson, and it disappears if you read the solution first. Eight to twelve hours.
  3. Then use it as a checklist on a real project. The exit checklist at the foot of each stage is written to be run against live work. The transferable kit compresses the whole thing into one screen and a swap table for translating pharmacy vocabulary into your own industry's.
On the shape of this document

Each stage carries a header block set like a dispensing label — number, time budget, the artifact you produce, and who signs it off. That is not decoration. Real analyst work has exactly those four properties, and the reason juniors drown is that they are given the task without ever being told what the deliverable is or whose signature ends the stage.

About the company in this playbook. MedixDirect does not exist. Its structure, systems and numbers are constructed to be realistic for a mid-size European online pharmacy, and the arithmetic in Stage 06 is internally consistent so you can check it by hand. Nothing here is a real corporate record, and no figure should be quoted as one. The regulatory points (fixed prescription pricing in Germany, Good Distribution Practice, health data under GDPR Article 9) are real in outline and are the kind of thing you must confirm with your own legal team rather than with a document like this one.

Nine sources, three of which contradict each other. You will meet all of them.

The brief, verbatim

“Revenue is up nine percent year on year and gross margin is down 2.4 points. That is roughly eleven million euros of profit we did not make. I present to the board on the 24th. I need to know why, and I need to know what to stop doing.” Elke Brandt · CFO · forwarded to you by your manager with the word “thoughts?”

Read that again and notice what it does not contain. No definition of gross margin. No statement of which entity or which markets. No indication of whether “year on year” means calendar 2025 versus 2024 or a rolling twelve months. No dataset. No mention of who else has already looked at it. And one instruction disguised as a question: what to stop doing — the CFO has already decided that something is being done wrong, which is a hypothesis you are being handed, not a neutral question.

An inexperienced analyst opens a SQL editor here. That is the mistake this entire playbook exists to prevent, and the next three stages are what should happen instead.

Before Stage 00

Your first five days in any company

If you have just joined, you cannot execute a project yet, because you do not know what is true. This is the week that makes every later project cheaper — and it is the part of the job that transfers completely between industries.

The goal of week one is not to produce analysis. It is to build three maps: a map of the decisions the company makes repeatedly, a map of the data that exists, and a map of the people who know things. Analysts who skip this spend their first six months re-answering questions that were already answered and re-discovering tables that were already deprecated.

Day 1–2 · Read before you ask

Ask for read access and then actually read, in this order. Each item answers a question you would otherwise burn someone's goodwill asking.

  1. The last two board or investor decks. This is the highest-value document in any company and almost no analyst asks for it. It tells you which five numbers leadership believes are the business, what direction they are supposed to be moving, and what vocabulary is used at the top. Every dashboard you ever build should reconcile to these.
  2. The monthly management accounts, and the finance definition of revenue and margin. Finance is the closest thing to a system of record for money. If your number disagrees with finance, you are wrong until proven otherwise — not because finance is smarter, but because the auditors ratified their version.
  3. The existing dashboards, ranked by usage. Ask the BI platform for view counts. You will typically find that of 140 reports, eleven are used weekly and the rest are abandoned. The eleven tell you what the company actually runs on; the rest are a graveyard that tells you what analysts before you got wrong.
  4. The data warehouse documentation and dbt docs, if they exist. Read the model names and the tests, not the descriptions. Which models have no tests? Those are the ones nobody trusts.
  5. The last six months of analytics tickets. The pattern of requests is the shape of your future job. If forty tickets say “can you pull …”, your real problem is self-service, not analysis.

Day 3–4 · Meet eight people, ask the same four questions

Book 30 minutes each with: your manager, the finance controller, a commercial or category manager, the supply chain planner, a data or analytics engineer, a product manager, someone in customer service, and — this is the one people skip — the most senior operational person who has been there longest, regardless of title. That last person knows why the data looks strange, and their knowledge is written down nowhere.

Ask each of them exactly these four questions, and write the answers verbatim:

Intake interview · the four questions
1. "What decision do you make regularly that you wish you had better numbers for?"
   → gives you your project pipeline, in their words, ranked by their pain.

2. "Which number do you personally not trust, and why?"
   → this is the single most productive question in the job. The answers
     point straight at the broken pipelines, the disputed definitions and
     the political fault lines. Nobody minds answering it.

3. "If a number on a report looks wrong to you, what do you do?"
   → tells you whether there is a real data-quality process or whether
     people quietly maintain private spreadsheets. There are always
     private spreadsheets. Find them; they encode the real definitions.

4. "Who else should I be asking about this?"
   → the referral graph. Follow it. The person named by three different
     people in week one is the person you will depend on for two years.

Keep the answers in one document, attributed. In month three, when two people insist on contradictory definitions of “active customer”, this document is what lets you say “in January you told me it was X” without it becoming an argument.

Trap · the eager first month

The mistake: building an ambitious dashboard in your first month to make an impression.

Why it is deadly: you do not yet know the definitions, so you will encode wrong ones into something people start using. Wrong numbers that nobody uses are harmless; wrong numbers that get adopted are extremely expensive to withdraw, and withdrawing them is now your reputational cost. The dashboard also becomes yours to maintain forever.

The fix: in month one, ship answers, not assets. A well-written memo answering a real question costs nothing to retire. Build durable assets from month two, once you can defend every definition in them.

Task 01 · 20 minutes

You are on day 3. The supply chain planner tells you: “I don't trust the availability report. It says we were 96% in stock last month but I know we had a bad month.” Before you look at any data, write down the four most likely explanations — and note which of them are data problems and which are definition problems.

Show the solution

Two of the four are definition problems, and that ratio is typical — which is why the reflex of “let me check the data” is usually the slower path.

  1. Definition · the denominator is all SKUs, not the ones that matter. 96% availability across 64,000 SKUs is dominated by the long tail of slow-movers that are always in stock. Weight by demand or revenue and the same month might read 84%. The planner experiences the demand-weighted number; the report shows the unweighted one. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
  2. Definition · availability is measured at the wrong moment. If stock is snapshotted nightly at 02:00, every SKU that sells out at 14:00 and is replenished overnight reads as available all month. The planner lived through the 14:00s.
  3. Data · drop-ship is counted as in-stock. Lines the wholesaler can supply may be flagged available even though they add two days to delivery. The report says yes, the customer experienced a delay, and the planner is judged on the customer's experience.
  4. Data · the metric excludes what was never orderable. A SKU that was deactivated because it could not be sourced disappears from the SKU list entirely, so it cannot be counted as out of stock. Availability improves by deleting the problem. This is survivorship bias, and it is extremely common in operational reporting.

What to do with this on day 3: nothing yet. You write it in the intake document under the planner's name, and you now have a candidate project with a known stakeholder and known pain. That becomes side quest A.

Exit checklist · week one
  • You can name the five numbers leadership believes are the business, and where each is defined.
  • You have read the last two board decks and the finance definition of revenue and margin.
  • You have met eight people and recorded their answers to the four questions, attributed.
  • You have a one-page map of where each fact is born and which copy is authoritative.
  • You have written down at least three known contradictions between sources.
  • You have shipped one trivial thing end to end and know how long access takes.
  • You have not built a dashboard.
Stage 00Triage · before any data

The request lands

Turning a sentence someone typed in a hurry into a question that has an answer, a decision that depends on it, and a date.

Time budget
2–4 hours
Deliverable
Intake note + triage verdict
Signed off by
Your manager
Done when
You can state the decision the answer will change

Why this stage exists

The failure it prevents is the most common failure in the entire profession: delivering an excellent answer to the wrong question. It costs weeks, it is invisible until the delivery meeting, and it is almost never the stakeholder's fault. People ask for what they think is available rather than what they need, because they do not know what is possible. Your job at intake is to translate.

There is a second, quieter failure: accepting work that should not be done at all. An analytics team's throughput is set less by how fast it works than by how much work it declines. Nobody teaches juniors to decline, so they don't, and then they are at 140% capacity delivering things nobody reads.

What you actually do

  1. Find the decision. Every request is a proxy for a decision somebody is about to make. Write it as a sentence beginning “After seeing this, X will decide whether to …”. If you cannot complete that sentence, you do not have a project — you have a curiosity, and curiosities go to the back of the queue. Here: “After seeing this, the CFO and the commercial director will decide which categories or channels to stop investing in, and what to commit to the board as a margin recovery plan.” That is a real decision with money attached.
  2. Find the real requester. The person who forwarded it is often not the person who needs it. Ask: who will be in the room when this is presented, and who will be unhappy with the answer? Here the CFO asked, but the answer will implicate the commercial team's paid-search bidding. The commercial director is therefore a stakeholder even though they never asked — and if you find out at the delivery meeting rather than at intake, your finding gets ambushed.
  3. Find the real deadline, and what happens on it. “The 24th” is a board meeting, which means the deck is written on the 19th, which means the number is frozen on the 17th, which means you have eleven working days, not three weeks. Always walk the deadline backwards to the freeze date. Ask explicitly: “what happens if this is late?” — sometimes the answer is “nothing”, and that changes your priority.
  4. Establish what is already known. Ask who has looked at this before and what they found. Search the ticket history and the BI folder. Here: someone in finance built a margin bridge in Excel four months ago and abandoned it. Getting that file saves you three days and, more importantly, tells you which definition finance was using.
  5. Estimate, in ranges, with a named unknown. Never give a single-point estimate. Say: “Four to nine working days. The width is because I do not yet know whether purchase cost is available at line level or only at invoice level — I will know by Thursday and will narrow the estimate then.” This does three things: it is honest, it educates the stakeholder about what makes analysis slow, and it gives you a legitimate checkpoint to renegotiate.
  6. Issue a triage verdict. Every request gets one of four, explicitly:
VerdictWhenWhat you say
Take itReal decision, real deadline, data plausibly exists, you are the right personScope it properly (Stage 01) before touching data.
Answer nowIt is a five-minute lookup, or the answer already existsSend the existing report or the number, with its definition. Do not turn a lookup into a project.
Reduce itThe ask is 5× the decision's value, or the deadline is impossible“I can't do all of that by the 17th. I can tell you the three biggest drivers with confidence, and leave the long tail unexplained. Is that enough for the board?” Offer a smaller thing, never a later date, first.

Traps

Trap · answering the literal question

The mistake: the brief says “gross margin is down 2.4 points”, so you set out to explain 2.4 points.

Why it is deadly: the 2.4 came from somewhere — a specific report, with a specific definition. If finance's gross margin excludes freight and yours includes it, you will spend nine days explaining a number that is not the number in the board deck, and everything downstream is wasted. The figure in the brief is a clue about which system the stakeholder was looking at, not a specification.

The fix: at intake, ask “where did the 2.4 come from — which report, which screen?” Then reproduce that exact figure yourself before starting the analysis. Reproducing the stakeholder's own number is the first technical act of the project (see Stage 04).

Trap · the deadline you were told

The mistake: planning to the date in the request.

Why it is deadly: stated deadlines are almost always the date of the event, not the date the work is needed. Deck-writing, review cycles and someone's holiday sit in between. You deliver on the 23rd for a meeting on the 24th and discover the deck was locked on the 19th.

The fix: ask “when does this have to be in someone else's hands?” and then subtract a review buffer. Put the freeze date, not the event date, in the intake note.

Trap · accepting a hypothesis as a scope

The mistake: “what to stop doing” tells you the CFO believes someone is doing something wrong, so you go hunting for the culprit.

Why it is deadly: you will find one, because with 64,000 SKUs and nine months of data you can always find something that looks bad. Motivated searching produces confident, wrong answers, and it is how analysts get used as ammunition in other people's fights.

The fix: accept the question, decline the premise. Write in the intake note that the analysis will quantify all material drivers including ones nobody is doing wrong — mix shift caused by genuine customer demand is not a mistake anybody made, and it turned out to be the largest single driver here. Being the person who says “nobody did anything wrong, the business changed shape” is more valuable than being the person who finds culprits.

Fatal · starting without a decision

The mistake: taking on work where you cannot name the decision it changes.

Why it is deadly: work with no decision attached has no definition of done, so it expands until you abandon it. Worse, it has no defender — when priorities shift, nobody in the room advocates for it, and the weeks you spent become invisible. Analysts who do a lot of this get performance-reviewed as unproductive while working the hardest hours in the building.

The fix: if you cannot complete “after seeing this, X will decide whether to …”, go back to the requester once with exactly that sentence half-finished and ask them to complete it. If they cannot either, it is a curiosity. Curiosities are fine — log them, batch them, do them in slow weeks — but they are not projects and must not be planned like projects.

Task 02 · 25 minutes

Three requests arrive on the same morning. Issue a triage verdict for each and justify it in two sentences.

  1. Head of Marketing: “Can I get a dashboard showing all our KPIs by channel, daily, updated hourly?”
  2. Customer service team lead: “Complaints about wrong deliveries feel higher since November. Are they?”
  3. The CEO's assistant: “The CEO would like to know our market share in the Netherlands.”
Show the solution

1 · Reduce it

“All our KPIs”, “daily” and “hourly refresh” are three separate red flags. There is no decision attached — a dashboard of everything is a request for reassurance, not a decision support tool, and hourly refresh on daily-grain metrics is a cost with no user. Reduce by asking the decision question: “which decision do you make most often, and how often do you make it?” If marketing reallocates budget weekly, you build a weekly channel contribution view with four metrics, not forty. Hourly refresh gets declined explicitly on cost grounds, and you name the cost.

2 · Take it — and it is the best of the three

It has a real decision (whether to escalate a fulfilment problem), a named owner who feels the pain, a falsifiable question, and it is cheap: complaint counts by reason code over time is a day's work. It is also the kind of question where the answer is genuinely uncertain, which means it has information value. Note the trap in it: “feel higher” may be true in absolute count while flat as a rate, because order volume grew — so define the metric as a rate per thousand orders before you look.

3 · Decline, then redirect

Market share requires total market size, which is external data MedixDirect does not have. You cannot compute it; you can only buy it (IQVIA, Nielsen, or the national pharmacy association's published figures). Declining is the correct answer and it takes one paragraph: “Market share needs a market denominator we don't hold internally. Options: (a) IQVIA subscription, roughly €X, four weeks to onboard; (b) the ABPI/KNMP published channel totals, free but annual and lagging nine months; (c) I can give you our growth rate against the published online-pharmacy channel growth rate as a proxy for share direction, by Thursday.”

Notice option (c). A good decline is never just a no — it names what you can do, with a date. And note who asked: a CEO request routed via an assistant usually has a specific trigger (a press article, an investor question). Ask what prompted it; the real question is often narrower and answerable.

Scoping the work

The conversation that converts a decision into a specification — and the one-page charter that stops the scope moving underneath you.

Time budget

Deliverable

Signed off by
The requesting stakeholder, in writing

Why this stage exists

Stage 00 established that the project is worth doing. Stage 01 establishes what it is. The failure it prevents is scope drift — not the dramatic kind, but the ordinary kind where each of six small additions is individually reasonable and collectively doubles the work, and nobody noticed because nothing was written down.

It prevents a second failure that is harder to see: delivering something true that nobody can act on. The test is brutal and you should apply it out loud in the meeting: “if I come back and tell you the answer is X, what will you do differently on Monday?” If the honest answer is “nothing”, you have found out now rather than in three weeks.

The interview

Book 45 minutes with the requester. Bring the intake note. Ask these, roughly in order. You will not get through all of them and that is fine — the first six are the ones that matter.

 3. Who else has to agree before that action happens?

CONSTRAINTS AND RISK
13. Is there anything I should not look at, or anyone I should not ask?
14. Is anyone else already working on this?
15. Has anything changed in the business this year that I should know about —
    a system migration, a pricing change, an acquisition, a new warehouse?
16. What answer would you find hardest to believe?

CLOSING
17. Who should I brief before the meeting, so nobody is surprised in it?
18. If I find something outside this scope that you'd want to know, do you
    want me to chase it or park it?

Question 15 is the highest-yield question in the set and almost nobody asks it. At MedixDirect the answer was “we replatformed the promotions engine in March” — which turned out to explain one of the four drivers and to make any comparison spanning March structurally unreliable. Question 16 is the second best: it tells you which finding will need the most evidence, so you know where to spend your verification effort.

The charter

One page. Sent as an email or a document, not spoken. It exists to be pointed at later, so it must be short enough that the stakeholder actually reads it.

QUESTION
  Which drivers account for the 2.4pp decline in gross margin % in FY2025

DECISION IT SERVES

  decisions on channels and categories.

  Gross margin % = (net revenue excl. VAT − product cost at weighted
  average purchase price − inbound freight − outbound freight)
  / net revenue excl. VAT

    the 2.4pp in the flash report. Fulfilment freight IS in cost of sales.
  · Excludes: payment fees, warehouse labour, returns processing
    (those belong to contribution margin, which is out of scope)
  · Net revenue is after discounts, after returns, excluding cancelled
    orders, excluding VAT

    cannot be attributed to a line) — this is a known limitation, stated

     with a stated unexplained residual.
  2. For each driver: is it controllable, by whom, and what action would

  3. Three slides plus a two-page memo. NOT a dashboard (see below).

WHAT I WILL NOT DELIVER

  · Competitor or market pricing comparison

Traps

Trap · agreeing the definition verbally

The mistake: finance tells you gross margin excludes freight, you nod, you move on.

Why it is deadly: definitions have five or six sub-clauses (returns, VAT, rebates, cancellations, currency) and people answer about the one you asked and forget the rest. Two weeks later your number is off by 0.3pp and the conversation restarts from zero, except now there is a deadline.

The fix: write the formula out with every exclusion listed, send it to the definition owner, and get a written “yes”. Name them in the charter — agreed with M. Reuter, Finance, 15 Jan. This is not bureaucracy; it converts a future argument into a citation.

Trap · the invisible “and also”

The mistake: accepting small additions in Slack as they arrive, because each is ten minutes.

Why it is deadly: they are never ten minutes — a new market means new currency handling, a new segment means the whole analysis re-runs and re-checks. Six of them consume a week, and because none was ever discussed as a change, you appear simply slow.

The fix: accept the request and price it, in the same message. “Yes, adding FR separately is useful. It's about a day because of the currency and VAT handling — do you want it instead of the category breakdown, or in addition, moving delivery to the 24th?” Almost always they withdraw it. You have not said no; you have made the cost visible.

Fatal · no stated definition of done

The mistake: starting analysis without a threshold for “good enough”.

Why it is deadly: margin decomposition has no natural end. You can always split a driver further, chase the residual from 12% to 9%, add another dimension. Without a threshold you will work until the deadline, then deliver something unfinished under pressure, which is exactly when arithmetic errors get made.

The fix: agree the threshold in advance and in the stakeholder's terms — “≥80% explained, reconciles to management accounts within 0.1pp”. This lets you stop, and it lets you say “this is done” with a straight face. It also reveals when the threshold is impossible, which is information you want on day two, not day nine.

Domain immersion

Why this stage exists

Every industry has a small number of structural facts that make naive analysis wrong — not imprecise, wrong. In pharmaceutical retail there are about twelve. An analyst who does not know them will produce a margin analysis that recommends discounting prescription medicines, which is illegal in Germany, in front of a CFO who will never ask them for anything again.

This is also the stage that generalises worst and matters most. You cannot take pharmacy facts to a bank. What you can take is the method: identify the handful of rules that constrain the business, and the handful of physical or legal realities that the data does not represent.

The twelve facts

FactWhat it meansWhat it does to your analysis
1 · Rx prices are fixed by law in Germany Prescription medicine prices are set nationally. A pharmacy cannot discount them, cannot run promotions on them, and earns a regulated fixed fee plus a small percentage. Any recommendation involving Rx pricing is void. Rx margin cannot be improved by commercial action — only by mix, purchasing terms, or cost to serve. This single fact removes a third of the obvious recommendations.
2 · An Rx order is not a purchase, it is a dispensation A pharmacist must validate the prescription. Orders can be legally refused, partially dispensed, or substituted for a generic equivalent. Conversion, cancellation and “fulfilment rate” all need Rx-specific definitions. A refused Rx is not a lost sale caused by you, and counting it as one makes your funnel look broken and your fixes pointless.
3 · Reimbursement means the patient is not the payer For most Rx, a statutory health insurer pays most of the price; the patient pays a small statutory co-payment. “Average order value” mixes two entirely different quantities. Revenue per Rx order is mostly insurer money, arriving later, with claim rejections. Never combine Rx and OTC AOV in one chart without saying so.
4 · Medicines are identified by national codes, not your SKU Germany uses the PZN, France the CIP, plus EAN barcodes and the international ATC therapeutic classification. The same molecule has different codes per market, and a repackaging changes the PZN without changing the product. Joining on the wrong code silently splits or merges products. Cross-market product analysis must go through ATC, not SKU.
5 · Batch, lot and expiry are tracked per unit Regulation requires knowing which batch went to which customer, for recall. Inventory is not fungible. A “stock” number that ignores expiry overstates sellable stock. Short-dated stock becomes a write-off with a predictable date — which is a margin driver you can forecast, and one of our four.
6 · Cold chain cannot share a box 2–8 °C products ship in validated insulated packaging, alone. One cold-chain line in a basket splits the order into two shipments. Freight per order is therefore not a function of order value — it is a function of basket composition. If you allocate freight per order rather than per shipment, you will miss this entirely. We did not, and it was −0.55pp.
7 · Returned medicines usually cannot be resold Once dispensed and out of pharmacy custody, medicinal products generally cannot re-enter saleable stock. A return in a pharmacy is a 100% loss of goods plus return freight, unlike fashion retail where it is a 15% loss. Return rate is a first-order margin metric here, not a customer experience metric. Never reuse a retail returns model.
8 · You may not advertise prescription medicines to the public Advertising Rx to consumers is prohibited in the EU. Paid search can only bid on conditions, brands of OTC, and generic terms — which is exactly how the traffic mix shifted toward low-margin categories. The constraint caused our largest driver. Recommendations must respect it.
9 · Good Distribution Practice governs the supply chain GDP rules dictate storage, transport, temperature monitoring and audit trails; suppliers must be licensed wholesalers. “Just source it cheaper” is often not available. Supplier switching has a qualification lead time measured in months. Any cost recommendation needs a feasibility check with Quality/Regulatory before it goes on a slide.
10 · Adverse events must be reported, on a clock Pharmacovigilance: a suspected adverse drug reaction reaching the company must be forwarded to the authority within a legally defined window. If your text analysis of support tickets finds something that looks like an adverse event, you have a legal obligation, not a finding. Route it to the qualified person immediately. See side quest C.
11 · Prescription data is special-category personal data Health data under GDPR Article 9 — a stricter regime than ordinary personal data. You probably may not join Rx product detail to marketing data at individual level, even though the join key exists and the query would run. Aggregate, pseudonymise, apply minimum cell sizes. “Technically possible” and “lawful” are different questions and the second one is not yours to answer alone.
12 · Demand is seasonal, and the season is biological Cold and flu, allergy, dermatology and vitamin D all have strong annual cycles driven by weather and pollen, not marketing. Never compare consecutive months. Never attribute a January uplift to a campaign. Year-on-year at equal calendar position, or nothing — and even then, a mild winter moves the whole comparison.
The general method · learning any domain in a day

Four questions, asked of two or three operational people, get you 80% of any industry's constraint set:

  1. What are we not allowed to do? Regulation, contracts, licensing. This finds the recommendations that are void before you make them.
  2. What is physically true that the data does not say? Cold chain splitting boxes, a machine that cannot change tooling mid-shift, a truck that must be full. The data records outcomes, not constraints.
  3. What is the unit the business actually thinks in? Pharmacies think in packs and molecules; airlines in seats and legs; banks in accounts and exposures. Analyse in the business's unit, not the database's.
  4. Which of our numbers do outsiders define for us? Regulated prices, reimbursement rates, index benchmarks. These move without anyone in the company doing anything, and they will show up in your variance analysis as unexplained.
Fatal · recommending something illegal

The mistake: concluding that margin is worst in Rx and recommending selective price increases or promotional pullback on prescription lines.

Why it is deadly: it is not merely wrong, it demonstrates that you do not understand the business you are analysing. Every other finding in the deck is then re-read with suspicion, and reasonably so. Analysts recover from arithmetic errors; they do not recover quickly from showing that they never asked how the industry works.

The fix: before the recommendation slide exists, run every proposed action past one domain person with the question “is there any reason we cannot do this?” It takes fifteen minutes. At MedixDirect that conversation killed two of six draft recommendations and improved a third.

Task 04 · 20 minutes

You build a chart of gross margin % by market and see: Germany 16.1%, France 19.4%, Netherlands 21.8%. A colleague concludes “Germany is our problem market; the German commercial team is underperforming.” Using only the twelve facts, give three reasons that conclusion may be unsafe — and say what you would put on the chart instead.

Show the solution
  1. Mix, not performance (facts 1 and 3). If Germany has a much higher Rx share — plausible, since it is the largest market with the most mature e-prescription adoption — then its blended margin is lower by construction, because Rx margin is 8–11% and fixed by law. A market with more of the low-margin regulated product will always look worse in a blended number, and no commercial team can change that.
  2. Regulated prices differ by country (facts 1 and 4). Rx margin structure is set by national law: the German fixed fee, the French margin schedule and the Dutch system are different regimes. Comparing blended margin across markets partly compares three legislatures, not three teams.
  3. Cost to serve and returns differ (facts 6 and 7). If cold-chain share or return rate differs by market — different insurer rules, different delivery geography — the margin gap may be logistics and write-offs rather than commercial execution.

A fourth, worth mentioning: the scope trap. The charter excluded the NL joint venture. If NL's 21.8% quietly includes it, the comparison is broken for reasons that have nothing to do with pharmacy.

What to put on the chart instead

Two changes. First, split by line before comparing markets — a small multiple of three panels, Rx / OTC / Para, each showing margin % by market. Now you are comparing like with like, and if the German commercial team really is underperforming it will show up as a gap within OTC, where they actually control price.

Second, add a mix-adjusted margin line: what each market's margin would be if it had the group-average line mix. The gap between actual and mix-adjusted is the part attributable to structure; what remains is the part attributable to execution. That is the same decomposition logic Stage 06 applies to the whole business, and it is the single most reusable analytical move in this document.

Then write the caption yourself, because someone will screenshot the chart without it: “Blended margin differs mainly because Rx share differs; Rx prices are set by national law. Compare within line, not across markets.”

Why this stage exists

The failure it prevents is discovering on day eight that the analysis is impossible. Not hard — impossible, because the column that would answer the question was never recorded. That is not a rare event; it is the normal state of affairs, and the difference between a senior and a junior analyst is mostly how early they find out.

The most important idea in this stage is the distinction between a system of record and a system of report. The system of record is where a fact is created and where it is legally or operationally authoritative. A system of report is any downstream copy. Copies drift, lag, filter and get patched. When two numbers disagree, the question is never “which query is right” — it is “which of these is the system of record for this fact”.

What you actually do

  1. Inventory the sources, with grain. For each table you might use, record: what one row means, the row count, the date range, who owns it, its refresh cadence, and whether it is a record or a report. The “what one row means” column is the one that saves you — it is called the grain, and getting it wrong is the leading cause of quietly duplicated revenue.
  2. Resolve the assumption log. You flagged A1–A3 in the charter. Each becomes a query. Do these before anything else; they gate the whole project.
  3. Ask the data owner the nine questions. Twenty minutes with the person who owns a table is worth two days of reverse engineering, and they are almost always pleased to be asked.
  4. Request access with a purpose, and request the minimum. Access requests that say “I need read on the warehouse” get queued. Requests that say “I need read on marts.fct_order_line and dim_product for the FY25 margin analysis requested by the CFO, no patient-level or Rx-detail columns needed, for four weeks” get approved the same day. Naming the sponsor and excluding the sensitive columns yourself does most of the work.
  5. Establish the lineage of the number you must reproduce. The CFO's 2.4pp came from a specific report. Trace it back through the BI layer to the models to the sources, and write the chain down. You will be asked “why is your number different from the dashboard” and this is the only answer that ends the conversation.
The nine questions for a data owner
1. What does one row in this table mean?               ← the grain. Always first.
2. What is the primary key, and is it actually unique?
3. Which columns should I never trust, and why?
4. Is anything soft-deleted or logically deleted rather than removed?
5. When does it refresh, and what happens when the load fails —
   does it retry, skip, or partially load?
6. Do rows ever change after they are written? Which columns?
   (this is the difference between a fact and a slowly-changing dimension,
    and it decides whether "as at" reporting is even possible)
7. Are there rows in here that are not real business events —
   tests, internal orders, staff purchases, migrated legacy data?
8. What is upstream of this, and is that the system of record?
9. Who else queries this, and is there an agreed query I should reuse?

Question 7 is the one that has saved me the most time in practice. Nearly every commercial database contains a few hundred non-real orders — QA tests with €0.01 prices, internal staff purchases at cost, a data migration that created 1,400 orders dated 1970-01-01 — and they are invisible in aggregate but destroy any analysis of extremes, distributions or margins.

Resolving the assumptions

A1 was: purchase cost is available at order-line grain. This is the query that decides whether the project as scoped is possible.

A1 · does line-level cost exist, and how complete is it?
-- Question: what share of FY25 revenue has a usable line-level cost?
-- Not "does the column exist" — does it have VALUES, for the rows I need.

select
    date_trunc('month', ol.order_date)              as month,
    count(*)                                        as lines,
    count(ol.unit_cost)                             as lines_with_cost,
    round(100.0 * count(ol.unit_cost) / count(*), 1) as pct_lines_costed,
    -- weight by money, because completeness by row count is misleading:
    -- the missing 3% of rows can be 20% of revenue
    round(100.0 * sum(case when ol.unit_cost is not null
                           then ol.net_amount else 0 end)
                / nullif(sum(ol.net_amount), 0), 1)  as pct_revenue_costed,
    count(case when ol.unit_cost = 0 then 1 end)     as zero_cost_lines
from marts.fct_order_line ol
where ol.order_date >= date '2024-01-01'
group by 1
order by 1;

The result at MedixDirect: 97.2% of lines and 91.6% of revenue carried a cost — but the gap concentrated in drop-ship lines, where cost arrives on the wholesaler's invoice days later, and in 8,900 lines with unit_cost = 0, which are not free products but missing data. So A1 is partially true, which is the most common outcome and the one no tutorial prepares you for. The fallback: use line cost where present, fall back to the product's weighted average purchase price for the month, and report both the coverage and the difference the fallback makes.

The two-number habit

Notice that the query reports completeness twice — by row count and by revenue. Do this everywhere. Row-count completeness is what an engineer cares about; revenue-weighted completeness is what the business cares about, and they routinely differ by an order of magnitude because the broken rows are the unusual, expensive ones. “97% of rows are fine” has ended more careers than “we're missing 8% of revenue”.

Traps

Trap · assuming the grain

The mistake: treating a table called orders as one row per order.

Why it is deadly: it may be one row per order version, per status change, per market, or per line. Join it to anything and you silently multiply money. The result looks plausible — revenue is simply 1.3× too high — and plausible wrong numbers are the dangerous kind.

The fix: never trust a table name. Run the grain test on every table before you use it: select k, count(*) from t group by k having count(*) > 1 limit 20 on the columns you believe are the key. If it returns rows, your assumed grain is wrong. Do this in the first five minutes, every time, forever.

Trap · the join that multiplies

The mistake: joining a per-order table to a per-shipment table and summing revenue.

Why it is deadly: fan-out. Every order with two shipments contributes its revenue twice. At MedixDirect, cold-chain orders split — so the fan-out would have concentrated precisely in the segment under investigation, inflating exactly the numbers that mattered.

The fix: after every join, check the row count and the sum of the money column against the pre-join values. If either changed and you did not intend it, stop. Make this a reflex: join, then count, then sum. Aggregate the many-side to the grain of the one-side before joining, not after.

Trap · “the column exists” ≠ “the column is populated”

The mistake: seeing unit_cost in the schema and marking assumption A1 as verified.

Why it is deadly: schemas describe intent; data describes reality. A column can exist, be documented, and be 40% null for exactly the segment you care about. You find out during the analysis, when the numbers make no sense and you have no time.

The fix: verification is always a query with a count, and always weighted by money as well as rows. And check for the fake-not-null cases: zeros, empty strings, 'UNKNOWN', 1970-01-01, 9999-12-31. Databases are full of nulls wearing a disguise.

Fatal · querying data you were not authorised to use

The mistake: you have technical read access to prescription-level detail because access was granted broadly, so you join it in to enrich the analysis.

Why it is deadly: in a pharmacy this is special-category health data under GDPR Article 9. Technical access is not legal authorisation, and “the permission was granted” is not a defence anybody accepts. This is the one mistake in this document that can end an employment rather than a project, and it is genuinely easy to make with good intentions.

The fix: work from a written purpose. Your charter says what the analysis needs; anything beyond it requires asking. If enriching with restricted data would genuinely improve the answer, raise it with the data protection officer as a question, in writing, and let them decide. Deliberately not requesting access — and documenting that you did not — is a professional act, and it reads extremely well when someone reviews the project later.

Task 05 · 35 minutes

You need FY25 revenue and cost at order-line grain, with freight attributed correctly. Write the skeleton of that query, handling the shipment fan-out. Then state the three checks you would run on its output before using it for anything.

Show the solution

The core move is to aggregate the many-side down to the join grain before joining. Freight lives per shipment; you want it per line; so you compute each shipment's freight, spread it across the lines in that shipment by weight, and only then join to revenue. Never the other way round.

with clean_orders as (
    -- exclude non-real rows ONCE, in one place, so every downstream
    -- number uses the same universe. If this filter lives in five
    -- queries it will differ in five queries.
    select o.order_id, o.order_date, o.market, o.channel, o.customer_id
    from   storefront."order" o
    where  o.order_date >= date '2024-01-01'
      and  o.status not in ('CANCELLED', 'FRAUD_BLOCKED')
      and  o.channel <> 'INTERNAL'
      and  coalesce(o.is_test, false) = false
      and  o.customer_id not in (1, 2, 3, 99999)
),

-- STEP 1: freight per shipment, spread over that shipment's lines by
-- weight share. Done at shipment grain, so no fan-out is possible.
freight_per_line as (
    select
        sl.order_id,
        sl.order_line_id,
        sum(s.freight_cost
            * sl.line_weight_g
            / nullif(sum(sl.line_weight_g) over (partition by s.shipment_id), 0)
        ) as freight_alloc,
        count(distinct s.shipment_id) over (partition by sl.order_id)
            as shipments_in_order       -- keep this: it IS driver 3
    from wms.shipment_line sl
    join wms.shipment s using (shipment_id)
    group by sl.order_id, sl.order_line_id, s.shipment_id,
             s.freight_cost, sl.line_weight_g
),

-- STEP 2: cost, with the documented fallback for missing line cost
line_cost as (
    select
        ol.order_line_id,
        ol.quantity * coalesce(
            ol.unit_cost,                    -- preferred: actual line cost
            pp.wavg_purchase_price           -- fallback: monthly weighted avg
        ) as product_cost,
        (ol.unit_cost is null) as cost_is_fallback   -- ← carry the flag through,
                                                     --   so you can quantify the
                                                     --   fallback's impact later
    from storefront.order_item ol
    left join erp.purchase_price_monthly pp
           on pp.product_id = ol.product_id
          and pp.month      = date_trunc('month', ol.order_date)
)

select
    co.order_date, co.market, p.line_type, p.atc_level2,
    ol.order_line_id,
    ol.net_amount            as net_revenue,   -- after discount, excl. VAT
    lc.product_cost,
    coalesce(f.freight_alloc, 0) as freight,
    lc.cost_is_fallback,
    f.shipments_in_order
from        clean_orders co
join        storefront.order_item ol using (order_id)
join        marts.dim_product     p  on p.product_id = ol.product_id
                                    and p.is_current                  -- SCD2!
left join   line_cost             lc using (order_line_id)
left join   freight_per_line      f  using (order_line_id);

The three checks — before this output is used for anything

  1. Row count and revenue sum, before and after every join. Count order_item rows for FY25 on its own, then count the final result. They must be equal. If the final count is higher, a join fanned out — almost certainly dim_product, if you forgot is_current on a type-2 dimension, which multiplies every line by the number of times that product's attributes ever changed. That single missing predicate is the most common serious bug in analyst SQL.
  2. Reconcile revenue to finance. Sum net_revenue by month and market, and compare to finance.gl_monthly. Agree a tolerance first (0.1% is reasonable). Any month outside it is investigated before you proceed — not explained away. This is the gate for Stage 04.
  3. Quantify the fallback and the nulls. What share of revenue used cost_is_fallback = true, and how much does margin differ between fallback and non-fallback lines? If fallback lines show materially different margin, your fallback is biased and needs disclosing. Also check how much revenue has freight = 0 because no shipment row matched — those are unshipped or digital lines, and you need to know which.

One more thing to notice. shipments_in_order is carried through even though nothing in the brief asked for it. It was added because Stage 03 discovered the grain mismatch, and it is the column that later isolates the −0.55pp cold-chain freight driver. Discovery work pays off in the analysis, but only if you carry the discovery forward into the data you build.

The profiling battery

Run this on every table you rely on. It is mechanical, it takes twenty minutes per table, and it finds things every single time.

The eight-question profiling battery
1  VOLUME BY TIME
   select date_trunc('month', d), count(*), sum(amount) from t group by 1
   → look for: step changes (a migration), gaps (a failed load), a ramp
     (a system being adopted), a cliff at the end (incomplete current month
     — the single most common cause of a "collapse" in the last data point)

2  GRAIN / DUPLICATES
   select key_cols, count(*) from t group by key_cols having count(*) > 1
   → must return zero rows, or your grain assumption is wrong

3  NULLS, BY ROW AND BY MONEY
   count(col) / count(*)  AND  sum(amount where col is not null)/sum(amount)
   → the two rarely agree; the money one is what matters

4  DISGUISED NULLS
   select col, count(*) from t group by 1 order by 2 desc limit 30
   → 0, '', 'UNKNOWN', 'N/A', '-', 1970-01-01, 9999-12-31, 'test', 'xxx'

5  CARDINALITY AND CATEGORY DRIFT
   distinct counts per month for every categorical column
   → a status value that appears in June and not before means the
     application changed, and your historical comparison just broke

6  RANGE AND SIGN
   min, max, and the 1st/50th/99th percentile of every numeric column
   → negative quantities (returns booked as negative lines?), zero prices,
     a €480,000 order line, dates in the future

7  REFERENTIAL INTEGRITY
   left join to each dimension, count the misses
   → orphan product_ids are usually deleted or unmigrated products, and
     they are silently dropped by inner joins, taking revenue with them

8  RECONCILIATION TO AN INDEPENDENT SOURCE
   your revenue by month vs finance.gl_monthly
   → the only check that validates the whole chain at once

Task 06 · 30 minutes

You plot monthly gross margin % for 24 months. Everything is smooth except: March 2025 spikes up by 4 points, December 2024 dips by 3, and the last month on the chart (January 2026, today is the 16th) has collapsed to 4%. Before investigating, list the most likely cause of each — and say which one is not a data problem at all.

Show the solution

January 2026 · collapsed to 4% — not a finding, an artefact

It is the 16th. You are looking at half a month, and the halves are not alike: costs and revenue arrive on different clocks. Drop-ship purchase invoices land days after the sale, so early-month revenue is booked with missing or fallback cost, while returns and write-offs from December are still being processed against January. Incomplete periods almost always distort margin more than revenue, because margin is a ratio of two series with different lags.

Fix: exclude the incomplete period from the chart, or plot it in a distinct broken style with the label “month to date, 16 Jan, cost incomplete”. Then put the data cut-off on the chart footer permanently. Never let an incomplete period sit unmarked on a chart that leaves your screen — someone will screenshot it, and you will spend a week denying a crisis.

March 2025 · spike up 4 points — the replatform

Question 15 in the scoping interview already told you: the promotions engine was replatformed in March. The most likely mechanism is that automatic discounts stopped being recorded for some period — either written to a new table your query does not read, or booked as a separate transaction type. Revenue then appears undiscounted, so margin jumps. A margin improvement that coincides exactly with a system migration is a data event until proven otherwise.

Two other candidates worth checking: a one-off supplier credit booked in March, and a stock revaluation. Both would show as a cost-side step rather than a revenue-side one, so decompose the spike into its numerator and denominator before you go hunting — which side moved? is always the first question about a ratio.

December 2024 · dip of 3 points — genuine, and structural

This is the one that is not a data problem. December is a real seasonal margin trough for a pharmacy, for several compounding reasons: Christmas gifting shifts mix toward low-margin cosmetics and infant formula; cold-and-flu season drives high-volume, price-competitive OTC; carrier surcharges raise freight; and year-end stock write-offs on short-dated goods are often booked in December. Check whether December 2023 dipped by a similar amount — if it did, this is seasonality and belongs in the “not controllable, do not investigate” pile.

The general lesson

Three anomalies, three different classes: one is an artefact of an incomplete period, one is a system change, one is real seasonality. None of them is a business problem, and an analyst who "investigated the December collapse" would have burned two days. Before investigating any anomaly, ask three questions in order: is the period complete? did a system change on that date? did the same thing happen last year? Those three questions dispose of the majority of anomalies for free.

Trap · averaging a percentage

The mistake: computing margin % per category and then averaging those percentages to get the total.

Why it is deadly: it silently weights a €4,000 category equally with a €40M one. At MedixDirect the true group margin was 16.8% and the average of category margins was 24.1% — a seven-point error that looks entirely plausible on a slide. Nobody spots it by eye, and the same bug hides inside pivot tables, BI measures written as an aggregation over a calculated column, and AVG(rate) in SQL.

The fix: always sum the numerator and denominator separately, then divide. In Power BI and similar tools this means a measure like DIVIDE(SUM(profit), SUM(revenue)) — never a column of ratios that is then aggregated. Put this rule in the metric spec, because the person who breaks it next will not be you.

Trap · silently changing a definition

The mistake: improving a definition — moving inbound freight into cost, say — and republishing without announcing it.

Why it is deadly: every historical number moves. Someone compares your new deck to last quarter's, sees a 0.5pp difference that nobody can explain, and the whole reporting stack loses credibility. Trust in numbers is lost through unexplained changes far more often than through errors.

The fix: version the definition, state the restatement impact numerically, name who approved it, and announce it before the number appears anywhere. “v1.2 moves inbound freight into cost to match the P&L. This lowers historical margin by 0.4–0.6pp. Approved by Reuter. Prior figures remain valid under v1.1.” Three sentences buys total forgiveness.

Task 07 · 40 minutes

The commercial team's dashboard says conversion rate is 4.1%. Marketing's says 2.6%. Both were built by competent people from the same warehouse. Find at least six mechanisms that could produce this gap, then write the numerator and denominator lines of the metric spec that settles it for a pharmacy specifically.

Show the solution

A conversion rate is a fraction, so there are only two places to look — but each has several independent choices, and the choices multiply.

Denominator mechanisms

  1. Sessions vs users vs visits. One user with four sessions is either 1 or 4 in the denominator. Sessions are more numerous, so a session denominator gives the lower rate — this alone can account for most of a 4.1 vs 2.6 gap.
  2. Bots and crawlers. Filtered in one, not the other. Pharmacy sites are heavily scraped by price comparison engines, so this is a large effect here, not a rounding one.
  3. Consent. Untracked sessions from users who refused analytics cookies are absent from GA4 but their orders still exist in the storefront. Mixing a GA4 denominator with a storefront numerator inflates the rate — and the inflation changes whenever the consent banner changes.
  4. App and channel scope. Native app sessions, or paid-only vs all traffic. Marketing habitually measures their own channels; commercial measures everything.

Numerator mechanisms

  1. Order placed vs order paid vs order shipped. Placed is the largest. Payment failures and fraud blocks sit between placed and paid.
  2. Cancelled orders included or not.
  3. Attribution window. An order counts in the session that produced it, or any order within 30 days of the visit. A 30-day window credits orders to sessions that did not contain them, so the rate goes up and can even exceed reality.

The pharmacy-specific mechanism, and it is the important one

Prescription orders are not conversions in the same sense. An Rx order is placed, then a pharmacist may refuse or substitute it (domain fact 2). And a large share of Rx traffic arrives with intent already fixed by a doctor — the customer is not choosing, they are collecting. Blending Rx and OTC into one conversion rate mixes a browsing funnel with a fulfilment queue. The two rates move for unrelated reasons, and any optimisation driven by the blend is aimed at an average of two different businesses.

Start with a hypothesis tree, not a query

A margin ratio can only move for a small number of structural reasons, and enumerating them before querying is what stops you from wandering. The tree is exhaustive by construction: a ratio changes because the numerator changed, the denominator changed, or the composition of the things being averaged changed.

Why the tree matters more than the queries

An analyst without a tree runs twenty queries, finds three interesting things, and presents them. The audience's first question is “is that all of it?” and there is no answer. An analyst with a tree can say: “these five branches are exhaustive; I tested all of them; three are material, one offsets, and 0.39 points remain unattributed.” That sentence is the difference between an interesting analysis and a decision-grade one.

The core technique: separating mix from rate

This is the single most useful piece of arithmetic in commercial analysis, and it recurs everywhere — margin by category, cost per unit by plant, salary by grade, conversion by channel. Learn it once and you will use it for the rest of your career.

A blended rate is a weighted average: R = Σ sᵢ · rᵢ, where sᵢ is group i's share of the denominator and rᵢ is its own rate. So the change in the blend splits exactly into two parts:

The decomposition identity
MIX EFFECT   = Σ (s₂₅ᵢ − s₂₄ᵢ) × r₂₄ᵢ
               "we sold a different mix of things, at last year's rates"

RATE EFFECT  = Σ s₂₅ᵢ × (r₂₅ᵢ − r₂₄ᵢ)
               "the things themselves earned differently, at this year's mix"

MIX + RATE   = Σ s₂₅ᵢ r₂₅ᵢ − Σ s₂₄ᵢ r₂₄ᵢ   =  R₂₅ − R₂₄      ← EXACT.

The identity is exact, with no residual — that is the point. Expand it:
   Σ s₂₅r₂₄ − Σ s₂₄r₂₄ + Σ s₂₅r₂₅ − Σ s₂₅r₂₄  =  Σ s₂₅r₂₅ − Σ s₂₄r₂₄  ✓

⚠ The split is not unique. Using s₂₄ in the rate term and r₂₅ in the mix
  term is equally valid arithmetic and gives slightly different numbers,
  with a cross-term left over. Pick one convention, WRITE IT ON THE SLIDE,
  and use it consistently. Auditors ask about this; being unable to answer
  is worse than the choice itself.

Following the mix upstream

Mix is never the root cause. Something caused the mix to move, and that is where the recommendation lives. At MedixDirect: paid search had been optimised against revenue and cost-per-acquisition targets during FY25. Marketing hit every target they were given. The target was wrong, not the execution. That is the recommendation: change the objective from revenue and CPA to gross profit per acquisition. It is a one-line change with an €11M swing behind it, and no analysis of cost-per-click would ever have found it.

Trap · allocating cost at the wrong grain

The mistake: computing freight per order by dividing total freight by order count.

Why it is deadly: it produces the right total and the wrong structure. You would see freight per order up 14% and attribute it to carrier rates, which rose 3% and are “outside our control”, and the analysis would stop there. The real driver — basket composition, which is very much controllable through range and bundling decisions — would never appear. Aggregates preserve totals and destroy mechanisms.

The fix: allocate every cost at the grain at which it is incurred, then roll up. Freight is incurred per shipment. Payment fees are incurred per transaction. Picking labour is incurred per line. If you allocate at the grain you happen to be reporting at, you will always find rates and never find mix.

Statistics: when it matters here, and when it does not

Notice that nothing above used a p-value, and that is correct. A common junior instinct is to attach significance tests to everything. Two rules:

You do not need inference on a census

You have every order for both years. The margin fell 2.40 points — that is not an estimate with sampling error, it is the population value. Asking whether it is “significant” is a category error. What you do need is materiality (is €11.5M worth acting on?) and stability (is this a trend or one strange quarter?). Plot the monthly series and let the reader see the shape.

You do need it when you generalise or compare samples

“Cold-chain customers have a higher repeat rate” is a claim about a sample of customers being used to predict future ones — that needs a confidence interval. So does any A/B test, any survey, and any statement about a subgroup small enough that a handful of orders moves it. Rule of thumb: if a segment has fewer than a few hundred observations, put an interval on it or do not quote a rate for it at all.

Trap · the confident subgroup

The mistake: reporting that the Dutch app channel's margin collapsed 9 points.

Why it is deadly: it is 340 orders. One €12,000 cold-chain order with a data error moves that number by four points. You will be asked to explain a “collapse” that is three orders, and the investigation will consume a week of someone's time.

The fix: set a minimum cell size before you slice — put it in the metric spec — and suppress or interval-bound anything below it. When a small segment genuinely looks alarming, look at the individual rows. With 340 orders you can read them, and reading them is faster and more conclusive than any test.

The causality line

Three of the four findings are near-arithmetic: the promotion defect, the freight allocation and the write-offs are identities, not correlations. You can trace each to specific rows. That is why they are safe to state plainly.

The mix driver is different. The claim “paid-search bidding caused the mix shift” is causal, and you did not run an experiment. What you have is: the mix shift is concentrated in paid-search-acquired orders; it begins when the bidding change was implemented; the mechanism is documented and legally forced (Rx cannot be advertised); and no alternative explanation you tested accounts for it. That is strong circumstantial evidence, not proof — and the honest phrasing matters, because the difference between the two sentences below is the difference between a finding that survives challenge and one that does not.

Do not write

“The change in paid-search bidding caused a 1.16pp margin decline.”

Stated as proven. One counter-example — “our competitor saw the same shift and they didn't change bidding” — and the whole deck is in question.

Write

“The mix shift is concentrated in paid-search-acquired orders (78% of it), begins in the month the bidding objective changed, and has a documented mechanism. We have not run a controlled test. A four-week holdout on generic head terms would confirm it and costs an estimated €140k in forgone revenue.”

Stated as evidence, with its strength, its limit, and the price of certainty. Unattackable — and it hands the stakeholder a decision about how sure they want to be.

Fatal · stopping at the first finding that fits the brief

The mistake: you find the promotion stacking bug on day two. It is €2M, it is a clear villain, and the CFO asked “what to stop doing”. You write it up and stop.

Why it is deadly: it is 17% of the problem. The board commits to a recovery plan worth €2M against an €11.5M hole, and next year the margin falls again. You will be asked why you did not find the rest, and “I found something and stopped looking” has no defence. The bug was also the easiest thing to find, which is exactly why it is unlikely to be the biggest.

The fix: the hypothesis tree, and the agreed threshold. You do not stop when you find something; you stop when the named drivers clear 80% of the movement, or when you can show that the remainder is not economically worth pursuing. Confirmation bias in analysis feels exactly like success — the moment a finding matches what the stakeholder already suspected is the moment to slow down, not speed up.

Task 08 · 45 minutes · the hardest task here

The Commercial Director reads your draft and replies: “The mix analysis is misleading. Rx grew because e-prescription adoption exploded across the whole market — that is a structural industry shift, nothing to do with our bidding. You are blaming marketing for a tailwind we were right to catch.”

He is partly right. Write the analysis that separates the two, state what result would prove him right, and say what you would put on the slide either way.

Show the solution

First: he is raising a real confounder, and he is doing it before the meeting because you pre-briefed him. That is the pre-brief working exactly as intended. The correct internal response is gratitude, then work.

Two mechanisms could produce the same observed mix shift, and they imply opposite actions:

  • H1 · Market-driven. E-prescription adoption grew, so every pharmacy's Rx share grew. MedixDirect caught a wave. Correct action: none on marketing — instead, accept structurally lower blended margin, and defend the P&L through cost to serve and Rx-specific purchasing.
  • H2 · Acquisition-driven. Our bidding change pulled in disproportionately Rx-skewed and formula-skewed traffic. Correct action: change the bidding objective.

The test: does the shift appear where H1 cannot reach?

H1 predicts the shift appears everywhere — every channel, every cohort, including customers acquired years ago. H2 predicts it is concentrated in newly acquired paid-search customers and largely absent among existing direct customers. That is a falsifiable difference, and it needs no external data.

-- Hold the customer cohort fixed. If existing customers' Rx share
-- moved as much as new paid-search customers', H1 wins.
select
    c.acquisition_channel,
    c.acquisition_year,
    date_trunc('year', ol.order_date)                as yr,
    count(distinct ol.order_id)                      as orders,
    sum(case when p.line_type = 'RX' then ol.net_amount else 0 end)
        / nullif(sum(ol.net_amount), 0)              as rx_share,
    sum(ol.net_amount - ol.product_cost - ol.freight)
        / nullif(sum(ol.net_amount), 0)              as gm_pct
from      analysis.fct_margin_line ol
join      marts.dim_product  p on p.product_id = ol.product_id and p.is_current
join      marts.dim_customer c on c.customer_id = ol.customer_id
where     ol.order_date >= date '2024-01-01'
group by  1, 2, 3
having    count(distinct ol.order_id) >= 500     -- min cell size
order by  1, 2, 3;

Three more checks, in order of value:

  1. Same-cohort comparison. Customers acquired in 2023 or earlier, FY24 vs FY25 Rx share. This holds acquisition constant entirely — any shift here is market or behaviour, not acquisition.
  2. Channel decomposition of the mix effect. Re-run the mix decomposition with channel as the grouping dimension. This tells you what share of the −1.16pp sits in paid search specifically. (The answer was 78%.)
  3. External benchmark. National e-prescription volumes are published by the German statutory system, and the pharmacy association publishes channel totals. If national Rx e-script volume grew 40% and our Rx revenue grew 24%, we underperformed the wave, and H1 is not merely partly right — it is the dominant story. Get this number; it is free and it settles the argument with something neither of you owns.

The single most valuable test you can write

Automate the reconciliation. A test that compares your revenue to the general ledger every morning, with a 0.1% tolerance, catches upstream breakage before anyone sees a wrong number. Most analytics teams test for nulls and uniqueness — which catch engineering faults — and never test against an independent source, which is the only thing that catches business faults. When someone silently adds a new order status, or a currency appears, or a source starts double-loading, this is the test that fires.

If you do build a dashboard

Information design, not decoration. Four rules that survive contact with real stakeholders:

  1. Answer the question in the top-left. Not a filter bar, not a logo, not a bank of twelve KPI tiles. One sentence or one number that answers why the page exists. People read a dashboard for three seconds before deciding whether to engage.
  2. One page, one decision. If the page serves two decisions it will serve neither. Tabs are fine; a single page with forty visuals is not.
  3. Every number carries its definition and its “as at”. A tooltip or a footnote linking to the metric spec. Without it you will re-litigate the definition every month with a new person.
  4. Encode state in shape, not only in colour. A margin below plan should be a filled bar or a labelled chip, not only red text — colour alone fails for colour-blind readers and in printed decks, and decks get printed.

Then the negatives, which matter more:

Do not put on a dashboard

Metrics nobody can act on. Ratios with tiny denominators. Anything requiring a paragraph of caveats to interpret — put that in a memo, where the caveat can sit next to the number and cannot be cropped out of a screenshot.

Do not skip row-level security

If a dashboard shows margin by category, work out who may see it before it goes live. In a pharmacy the harder case is patient data: any view reaching prescription detail needs a documented access model, and “it's only internal” is not one.

Trap · the type-2 dimension join

The mistake: joining fct_margin_line to dim_product without is_current or a validity-date predicate.

Why it is deadly: a slowly-changing type-2 dimension holds one row per version of each product. Every line silently multiplies by the number of times that product's attributes ever changed — a repackaging, a category reclassification, a price-band change. Revenue inflates by a factor that varies by category, so the relative picture is corrupted, which is far worse than a uniform error. And it is invisible: the totals just look higher than you expected, and everyone assumes the filter is wrong.

The fix: decide the semantics explicitly. For “what did we sell it as at the time”, join on order_date between valid_from and valid_to. For “how do we classify it now”, join on is_current. Both are right for different questions; neither is right by default. Then add the row-count test so the pipeline catches it if someone removes the predicate.

Why this stage exists

Because you are now the least objective person in the building about this analysis. You have spent nine days with it, you believe the answer, and belief makes errors invisible. Every experienced analyst has presented a number that was wrong in a way they had looked directly at, several times, without seeing.

The remedy is structural, not attitudinal. You cannot decide to be more careful. You can run a procedure that does not depend on your care.

The four checks that catch almost everything

  1. Re-derive the headline a completely different way. Not re-running the query — a different route to the same number. The margin decomposition was built bottom-up from order lines; so derive it top-down from the general ledger: total revenue, total cost of goods, total freight, straight from finance's monthly figures. If the two agree within tolerance, the chance of a systematic error in your pipeline drops enormously. If they disagree, you have found something before the CFO did. This single check is worth more than the other three combined.
  2. Check the boundaries. Errors hide at edges. Run the analysis with the period shifted by one month and confirm the pattern is not an artefact of where you cut. Then check: does the first day of the period appear? The last? What about 29 February 2024 — a leap day inside your comparison, which breaks naive day-count normalisations? What happens at the DST changeovers in March and October if any logic uses hours? Does the fiscal calendar match the calendar you used?
  3. Recount everything at every join. Walk back through the pipeline confirming row counts and money sums before and after each join. Then check the totals still reconcile — it is common to fix one thing at the end and silently break the reconciliation you did on day four. Re-run the reconciliation last, always.
  4. Have a peer try to break it. Not “review it” — that produces “looks good”. Give them a specific brief: “Here is the headline and here are the four drivers. Your job is to find the reason this is wrong. Ten minutes. Start with the join between shipments and lines.” Adversarial framing produces findings; polite review produces nothing.

Trap · percentage points versus percent

The mistake: writing “margin fell 2.4%” when it fell 2.4 percentage points, from 19.2% to 16.8%.

Why it is deadly: a 2.4% relative fall from 19.2% would be a decline to 18.7% — worth €2.2M, not €11.5M. The two readings differ by a factor of five, and finance people notice instantly. It is a small error that signals carelessness about exactly the thing you were hired to be careful about.

The fix: use “pp” or “percentage points” for differences between percentages, always, and audit every label before delivery. The QA checklist above found three. Three is normal.

Fatal · discovering an error after the decision

The mistake: finding a real error two days after the board committed to a plan based on your number, and hoping it does not matter.

Why it is deadly: it always comes out, and the delay is what destroys trust — not the error. An analyst who says “I found a mistake, here is the corrected number and what it changes” on the same day keeps their reputation and often enhances it. One who is found out six weeks later does not recover.

The fix: two things. Prevent it with the independent derivation above. And decide in advance, before you ever need it, that your disclosure rule is same day, in writing, to the decision owner, with the corrected number and the impact. Having decided the rule while calm is what lets you follow it when you are not.

Task 10 · 30 minutes

It is 21 January. The deck goes to the CFO tomorrow. You discover that int_freight_per_line divides by the sum of line weights within a shipment — and 3.1% of shipment lines have line_weight_g = 0, so those shipments' freight is allocated entirely to their non-zero-weight lines. What do you do, in what order?

Show the solution

Do not start by fixing it. Start by sizing it. The order matters, because with one day left the wrong first move is a rewrite you cannot finish.

1 · Size the impact before touching the code — 20 minutes

Is this material to any conclusion? Three numbers to get:

  • What share of total freight sits in shipments containing a zero-weight line? (Not what share of lines — freight is the money.)
  • Does the total allocated freight still equal SUM(shipment.freight_cost)? If the divisor is only ever the non-zero subtotal, the total is preserved and this is purely a distribution error within shipments, which is much less serious.
  • Are zero-weight lines random or systematic? Check what they are. If they are digital items, services, or leaflets, they legitimately have no weight and excluding them from freight allocation is correct behaviour, not a bug. If they are real products with missing master data, it is a bug.

At MedixDirect: 82% of zero-weight lines were prescription service fees and delivery-note lines with no physical weight, and 18% were genuine missing master data on 340 products. Total freight was preserved exactly. So the defect is a within-shipment misallocation affecting about 0.6% of freight — around €14k against a €2.64M driver.

2 · Test whether any conclusion moves — 15 minutes

Re-run the freight driver with the zero-weight lines given a fallback weight (the product's average weight, or an equal share). If driver 3 moves from −0.55pp to −0.55pp or −0.54pp, no conclusion changes and you have your answer. If it moved to −0.40pp, you stop the deck.

3 · Then decide, and the decision is not “fix it”

It moved by 0.01pp. So:

  • Do not rewrite the allocation the night before delivery. Changing a pipeline under time pressure without a full re-run of QA is how a 0.01pp problem becomes a 0.5pp problem. The risk of the fix exceeds the risk of the flaw.
  • Disclose it in the limitations section, in one line: “Freight is allocated within a shipment by line weight; 340 products have missing weights and receive no allocation. Impact on the freight driver: below 0.01pp.”
  • Tell your reviewer now, not after. Tomas signed the QA; he needs to know what he signed.
  • Log it as follow-up work with the 340 product IDs, and send those to master data. That is a real fix, worth doing, next week.

The general rule

A discovered flaw has two sizes: its size in the data and its size in the conclusion. Most flaws are large in the data and irrelevant to the conclusion, and the professional skill is telling the two apart quickly and calmly. Size it, test whether the conclusion moves, disclose it either way, and only rewrite if the conclusion moved. What is never acceptable is knowing and not saying — the disclosure line costs you nothing and is the entire difference between a limitation and a cover-up.

Answer first. Always answer first.

Analysts narrate chronologically — here is what I did, here is what I found, therefore here is the answer — because that is how the work happened and because the method feels like the value. Executives read top-down: answer, then implication, then evidence, then method. Delivering chronologically means your conclusion arrives at minute eighteen, by which time the room has formed its own view and is arguing with it.

Invert it. The first sentence is the answer.

The appendix rule

Everything else — the reconciliation ladder, the SQL, the methodology, the segment tables, the limitations — goes in an appendix that you do not present. It exists so that when someone asks “how did you handle returns?”, you turn to slide 14 and answer in ten seconds. The appendix is not padding; it is the reason the main deck can be three slides. Analysts who put the appendix content in the deck do so out of anxiety that their work will not be visible. It is visible in the confidence with which you answer questions.

The meeting

Three things to do before it, and they matter more than the deck.

  1. Pre-brief anyone who will be implicated, one-on-one, at least a day early. The Commercial Director saw the mix finding on the 21st, alone, with time to react. He pushed back (Task 08), the analysis improved, and by the group meeting he was explaining the finding rather than resisting it. Ambushing someone with a finding about their function in front of their peers guarantees the meeting becomes about the finding's validity rather than about what to do. This is not politics as manipulation — it is giving people the same chance to think that you had.
  2. Send the memo the evening before. Some people need to read; some need to discuss. Serve both.
  3. Decide the one thing you need out of the meeting. Here: agreement to fix the promotion defect immediately, and agreement to a paid-search objective review. Everything else is bonus. Meetings without a target outcome drift into interesting conversation and end with “great, send that round”.

Defending a number

You will be challenged, and challenges come in four kinds. Diagnosing which one you are facing determines the correct response, and answering the wrong kind is how analysts lose rooms they should have won.

ChallengeWhat it sounds likeThe response
Genuine technical doubt “Does that include returns?” Answer precisely and briefly. This is the easy one, and the appendix exists for it. If you don't know, say “I don't know, I'll confirm today” — never guess. One guess that turns out wrong costs more than ten admissions.
Different definition “That's not what I see. My report says margin improved.” Do not defend. Assume both are right and find the definitional difference out loud: “That sounds like product margin before freight — which did improve, by 0.4 points. Mine includes freight, which is the P&L definition. Both are real; here's the bridge.” This converts a confrontation into a shared discovery, and it is why Stage 05 exists.
Motivated resistance “I don't think that data is reliable enough to draw conclusions from.” Do not argue about reliability in general — it is unwinnable and it is not really the objection. Make it specific: “Which part would you want tested? I can check that today.” A vague objection either becomes a specific, testable one (good — you now have a task) or it evaporates in front of the room. Stay entirely neutral in tone; you are offering help, not calling a bluff.
Consequence resistance “Even if that's true, we can't change the paid-search strategy mid-year.” This is not a challenge to your analysis — it is a business constraint, and it is not yours to resolve. Accept it and re-aim: “That's fair, and it's a decision for you. What the analysis says is that the current objective costs about €2M a year in gross profit. If the strategy can't change until FY26, then the plan should assume this cost continues, and the board number should reflect that.” Never fight for a recommendation. Fight for the number being understood.
The one sentence worth memorising

When you do not know: “I don't know — I'll find out and come back to you today.” Then do, the same day. Analysts believe not knowing damages credibility. The opposite is true: an analyst who never says it is one whose confident answers cannot be distinguished from their guesses, and everyone eventually works that out. Saying it once, and following through fast, makes everything else you say more believable.

Then close it properly

Handover

One page in the repo: what the question was, which models answer it, how to re-run it, the known limitations, and who owns the metric now. Written for the person who inherits it in eight months — which may well be you, having forgotten everything.

Task 11 · 25 minutes

Mid-presentation, the CFO interrupts slide 1: “Stop. Your mix number is 1.16 points. Our strategy consultants said last year that mix was worth 0.4 points. Which of you is wrong?” The room is quiet. Write what you say.

Show the solution

Note first what you must not do: not defend, not disparage the consultants, not concede, and not speculate about their method. All four are available and all four are wrong.

The reflex to build instead: a difference between two numbers is a question, not a contest. Almost always the two are measuring different things over different periods with different groupings, and saying so calmly — while conceding nothing about your own number — is the whole answer.

"Possibly neither. A mix effect isn't a single quantity — it depends on
three choices, and if any of them differ the numbers differ legitimately.

First, the grouping. I split into four groups: Rx, OTC, parapharmacy, and
infant formula separately. If they grouped into two — Rx and non-Rx — most
of what I'm calling mix becomes invisible, because the shift toward infant
formula happens inside non-Rx. Finer grouping always finds more mix; that
alone could account for most of the gap.

Second, the period. Mine is calendar 2025 versus 2024. Theirs would have
been an earlier window, and the paid-search change I'm pointing at was
implemented during 2025 — so a study finishing in 2024 would correctly find
a smaller effect. It hadn't happened yet.

Third, the margin definition. Mine includes outbound freight, which is the
P&L definition and is why my total is 2.40 points. If theirs was product
margin before freight, the mix effect on that basis is smaller.

What I can tell you with confidence is that mix and rate sum exactly to the
2.40 points in your flash report — that's on slide 11 — so whatever the
grouping, half of the movement you asked about is composition rather than

If it's useful I'll reconcile to their number specifically. I'd need their
methodology note, and it's about half a day. Do you want that before the
board, or is the reconciliation to the flash report enough?"

Why this works

  • It opens with “possibly neither.” Not “they're wrong”, not “let me check”. It reframes instantly from conflict to explanation, and it costs you nothing.
  • Three concrete mechanisms, in descending order of likely size. Specificity is what makes it credible. Vague answers about methodology sound like excuses; naming grouping granularity as the probable cause sounds like command of the subject.
  • It gives the consultants a legitimate reason to be right. The change happened after their study. You have now made it impossible for the disagreement to be about anyone's competence.
  • It anchors on what is unarguable. Your decomposition sums exactly to the CFO's own reported number. That is the strongest ground available and it is why Stage 04 mattered.
  • It ends by handing her a decision with a price. Half a day. She said no, as people almost always do — but being offered the choice is what closes the topic.

The transferable move: when two numbers disagree, the answer is never which is right. It is “here are the three choices that would make them differ, and here is which one I think it is.” Have those three ready for every headline you present, before you present it. This question is asked in some form in most delivery meetings.

Proof it transfers

Three side quests

The same ten stages, compressed, on three completely different questions. What changes is which stage carries the difficulty — and knowing that in advance is most of what experience buys you.

The flagship was a decomposition problem, and its hard stage was 04 — reconciliation. These three are a counterfactual problem, a longitudinal problem, and a regulated problem. Each one's difficulty lives somewhere else entirely.

Quest A · “How much did stock-outs cost us?”

StageWhat is different here
00 · IntakeThe supply chain planner asked, from Task 01. The decision: how much extra safety stock to fund, which is a working-capital trade-off. Note that the question is asked to win an argument for budget — so it will be read adversarially by finance. Scope it knowing that.
01 · ScopingThe trap is the phrase “cost us”. Lost gross profit? Lost revenue? Lifetime value of customers who left? Each is 3–10× the previous one. Pin it: lost gross profit on the specific lines, plus a separately reported estimate of downstream churn. Never blend a hard number with a soft one.
02 · DomainTwo pharmacy-specific facts change everything. Generic substitution — if the branded product is out of stock, the pharmacist may legally dispense an equivalent generic, so the sale is not lost at all, and margin may even improve. And drop-ship means “out of stock” in the warehouse is not out of stock to the customer, only slower.
03–04 · DataThis is the hard part, and it is a data-existence problem. The counterfactual — what a customer would have bought — is not recorded anywhere. You need availability history, and most systems only store current stock. If nobody snapshotted stock daily, the question is unanswerable for the past. Finding this out on day one is the whole job. Fallbacks: the WMS pick-fail log, out-of-stock page-view events, backorder records, or the substitution log.
05 · MetricDefine lost_units explicitly as an estimate with a stated method, and say which of the four fates a stock-out had: substituted, backordered, drop-shipped, or abandoned. Only the fourth is a lost sale.
06 · AnalysisEstimate expected demand during the out-of-stock window from a comparable in-stock window for the same SKU — same weekday mix, adjusted for the category's trend that week so you do not attribute a market-wide dip to your stock-out. Then subtract what was substituted. Report a range, not a number. Any single figure here is false precision.
07–08 · Build & QAThe QA check that matters: does your method produce a large “loss” for SKUs that were out of stock because demand collapsed and nobody reordered? That is reverse causation and it will inflate your answer. Test it by checking whether lost-sales estimates correlate with pre-stock-out demand trend.
09 · DeliveryLead with the range and the substitution rate. “Gross stock-out exposure was €3.1–4.4M; 61% was recovered through generic substitution or drop-ship, so true lost gross profit was €0.9–1.3M.” That second sentence is what makes the number survive finance — and it is a smaller number than the planner hoped for, which you should tell them before the meeting.
The transferable lesson

Any question of the form “what did we lose?” is a counterfactual, and counterfactuals are not in your data by construction. The professional move is not to find a cleverer query — it is to say so early, name your estimation method, report a range, and separate the recovered portion from the truly lost portion. The same shape appears in churn cost, lost bids, abandoned baskets and downtime cost, in every industry.

Quest B · “Are patients staying with us?”

StageWhat is different here
00–01 · Intake & scopeAsked by the Head of Digital, who wants to justify a repeat-refill reminder feature. The decision is a product investment. The scoping trap: “retention” for a pharmacy is not one thing, because a person who buys sunscreen once a year is not churned.
02 · DomainThe crucial split: chronic versus acute. A patient on blood-pressure medication should reorder every 90 days, predictably, for years — and if they don't, that is a real, measurable, actionable churn signal. Someone who bought a cold remedy in February is not churned in March; they are simply not ill. Analysing them together produces a meaningless blended curve. Use the ATC therapeutic class to split the base.
03–04 · DataStraightforward, with one landmine: is the customer identity stable? Guest checkout, multiple emails per household, and one account ordering for a whole family all break the unit of analysis. Household-level ordering is especially common in a pharmacy — check whether one account's orders show two different chronic regimens.
05 · MetricFor chronic: on_time_refill_rate — share of expected refills that arrived within the expected window plus a grace period, where the window comes from the pack size and daily dose. For acute: repeat purchase within 12 months. Two metrics, two owners. Never one number.
06 · AnalysisThis is where the difficulty lives: censoring. A patient who joined in November cannot yet have missed a 90-day refill, so including them in a “refill rate” drags it up. This is right-censoring, and it is the single most common error in retention analysis. Use cohort tables where each cohort is only measured over months it has actually lived, and never compare cohorts at different ages. Read the diagonal, not the last column.
07–08 · Build & QAThe QA check: does your cohort table's most recent cohort look dramatically better or worse? It almost always does, and it is almost always censoring or an incomplete period. Also check for survivorship — customers deleted under GDPR erasure requests vanish from the base retroactively, which silently improves history.
09 · DeliveryOne chart: two retention curves, chronic and acute, on the same axes. The gap is the entire argument for the feature, and it needs no explanation. Then the honest limit: a reminder feature can address forgetting, but not the patient who switched pharmacy or whose prescription ended — and you cannot distinguish those in the data. Say so.
Fatal · the health-data join in Quest B

The mistake: segmenting patients by therapeutic class — diabetes, oncology, mental health — and pushing those segments into the marketing platform for targeted refill reminders.

Why it is deadly: you have inferred a person's medical condition and exported it to a marketing system. That is special-category health data processing under GDPR Article 9, almost certainly without a lawful basis, and it is the kind of thing that produces a regulatory investigation and press coverage. It is also easy to do accidentally: the segmentation is analytically excellent, the query runs, and the marketing platform accepts the upload.

The fix: do the analysis at aggregate level, and stop there. If a refill-reminder product is genuinely wanted, it is built inside the pharmacy system under a healthcare lawful basis with explicit patient consent — not by exporting condition segments to a marketing tool. Bring the DPO in at scoping, Stage 01, not after you have built it. And for the analysis itself, apply a minimum cell size and never publish a therapeutic-class breakdown fine enough to identify anyone.

Quest C · “Which patients received batch L4471A?”

StageWhat is different here
00 · IntakeThe Qualified Person calls at 16:40 on a Friday. A manufacturer has issued a recall on one batch. This is not an analytics request. It is a regulatory obligation with a legal clock, and your role changes from analyst to operator. Do not scope it, do not estimate it, do not put it in the queue. Do it, and confirm who is accountable.
01 · ScopingThe only scoping question is: what does the QP need, in what format, by when, and who verifies it before it is used to contact patients? Everything else is noise. Ask whether the recall is Class I (immediate risk to life) — that changes the hour, not the day.
02 · DomainBatch traceability is a legal requirement precisely so this query is possible (domain fact 5). The chain is wms.shipment_line → batch/lot → order line → order → customer. If your warehouse layer has aggregated away the batch, the company has a compliance problem far larger than any analysis.
03–04 · DataAccuracy requirements invert here. For the margin analysis, 97% coverage was fine. Here, a missed patient is a potential patient-safety event and a reportable failure. You need completeness, not precision, and when in doubt you must over-include — an extra patient contacted is an inconvenience; a missed one is a harm. Every other instinct in this playbook says be precise. This one says be exhaustive.
05 · MetricThere is no metric. There is a list, and the only quality measure is whether anyone is missing from it.
06 · AnalysisThere is no analysis. Resist entirely the urge to add value — no summary of affected demographics, no chart of geographic spread, nothing. Produce the list. Adding analysis to a safety task delays it and risks introducing a filter that excludes someone.
07–08 · Build & QAQA is everything and it runs the opposite way to normal: did I exclude anyone? Check for guest checkouts with no account, orders where the batch field is null (include them if the product and date window match — over-include), returns and re-dispenses, and anyone who received the product as part of a substitution. Have a second person independently re-derive the list and compare row counts. Do not skip this because it is late on a Friday.
09 · DeliveryHand the list to the QP, in the format they asked for, with a written statement of exactly what it includes, what it might miss, and the query used. Then stop. You do not contact patients. You do not decide the recall's scope. Log it.
The recall query · optimised for completeness, not elegance
-- Batch L4471A recall. Objective: MISS NOBODY.
-- Deliberately over-inclusive. Every OR below is a decision to include
-- someone who might not be affected, rather than risk excluding someone
-- who is. Reviewed with the QP before use.

select distinct
    o.order_id,
    o.order_date,
    c.customer_id,
    c.full_name,
    c.phone,
    c.email,
    o.delivery_postcode,
    sl.batch_code,
    sl.quantity,
    case
      when sl.batch_code = 'L4471A'            then 'CONFIRMED batch'
      when sl.batch_code is null               then 'UNKNOWN batch - include'
      else                                          'other batch in window'
    end as inclusion_reason
from        wms.shipment_line sl
join        wms.shipment      s  using (shipment_id)
join        storefront."order" o on o.order_id = s.order_id
left join   marts.dim_customer c on c.customer_id = o.customer_id
where       sl.product_id = 447122                    -- the recalled product
  and (
        sl.batch_code = 'L4471A'                      -- the batch itself
     or sl.batch_code is null                         -- unknown → INCLUDE
     or s.despatch_date between date '2025-11-03'      -- the full window in
                            and date '2026-01-12'     -- which that batch
      )                                               -- could have shipped
  -- NOTE: no status filter, no test-order filter, no is_current filter.
  -- Every exclusion is a chance to miss a patient. Cancelled orders are
  -- included because a cancellation after despatch still means the product
  -- reached the patient.
order by o.order_date;

-- Second, independent count for verification by a colleague:
--   expected units shipped from batch L4471A per goods-receipt = 4,210
--   units accounted for by the query above                    = ?
--   any gap must be explained BEFORE the list is used.

Every habit this playbook has taught you is deliberately suspended here: no exclusion of test rows, no is_current, no status filter, nulls included rather than investigated. That is not sloppiness — it is the correct response to a task where the cost of a false negative is incomparably higher than the cost of a false positive. Recognising when the usual rules invert is the most senior judgement in this document.

Fatal · treating a pharmacovigilance signal as a finding

The mistake: while analysing support tickets for a service-quality project, your text analysis surfaces forty messages describing symptoms after taking a product. You note it as an interesting finding for the report.

Why it is deadly: a suspected adverse drug reaction that reaches the company creates a legal reporting obligation on a defined clock (domain fact 10). By putting it in a report due next Thursday, you have started that clock and not told anyone. This is a regulatory breach, and “I didn't know that was my job” is not a defence the company can offer a regulator.

The fix: know before you start that this exists, and know who the Qualified Person is — their name and phone number, in your notes, before you ever touch support text. If anything you see could be an adverse reaction, you forward it that day, in full, and you say that you are not qualified to judge whether it qualifies. Over-reporting is expected and costless. Under-reporting is not. Every regulated industry has an equivalent trip-wire — financial services has suspicious activity, safety-critical engineering has incident reporting — and finding out what yours is belongs in Stage 02, on your first project.

Show the solution

What to avoid

Twenty-six failures, grouped by the stage that produces them. Each is written the same way: the mistake, why it is deadly, the fix. Most of them are things a competent, hard-working analyst does — that is precisely why they are worth naming.

Scoping failures

Six integrative tasks

Task 13 · 30 minutes

Your CEO forwards a competitor's press release: “our online pharmacy grew orders 34% last year.” He writes: “We grew 11%. Explain.” Design the analysis. What can you actually conclude, and what should you refuse to conclude?

Show the solution

The honest answer starts with a boundary: you cannot explain a competitor's number, because you have none of their data. Saying that clearly, first, is the professional move — and then giving him something genuinely useful is what stops it sounding like a refusal.

What you cannot conclude, and must say so

  • Whether their 34% is comparable at all. Orders, not revenue. Which markets? Did they acquire someone? Are they annualising a partial year? Press releases select the flattering metric — that is their purpose.
  • Why they grew. Any story you construct is speculation dressed as analysis, and if the CEO acts on it you own the outcome.

What you can do, in three parts

  1. Make the comparison honest. Restate our growth on their metric — order count, not revenue — for the same geography and period. If we grew orders 19% and revenue 11% because average order value fell, that is already an important finding, and it is entirely about us.
  2. Anchor both to the market. Find the published channel growth rate — the national pharmacy association, the e-prescription statistics, the regulator. If the online channel grew 28%, then they beat the market and we lost share; if it grew 9%, we both won and their number includes something structural. This is the analysis that answers the CEO's real question, and it costs half a day.
  3. Decompose our own 11%. New customers vs returning, category, channel, market. Growth decomposition uses exactly the mix-versus-rate arithmetic from Stage 06. This is the part that leads to action, because it is the only part we control.

The reply

I can't explain their number — we have none of their data, and a press
release picks its metric. But I can answer the question underneath it:
are we losing share, and where is our own growth coming from.

On their metric — orders, not revenue — we grew 19%, not 11%. The gap is
average order value, which fell 6%. That is worth understanding on its own.

Published channel growth for online pharmacy was ~28% last year. So on
orders we grew below the market: we lost share, and their 34% is roughly
market-plus-6, not market-plus-25.

Where our growth came from, and what it cost us, by Thursday. One thing I
already know: much of our order growth was Rx, which is why revenue grew
slower than orders and margin fell 2.4 points — that's the analysis Elke
has.

The transferable move: when asked about something outside your data, name the boundary in one sentence, then re-aim at the version of the question your data can answer, and connect it to work already in flight. Never construct a story about a competitor. The last paragraph also does something valuable — it links this request to the margin project, so the CEO learns the two are one story rather than two.

Task 14 · 25 minutes

You inherit a dashboard from an analyst who left. It is used weekly by twelve people. You discover its “active customers” measure counts anyone with an order in the last 90 days — including cancelled orders, and including the internal staff channel. Correcting it drops the headline number by 9%. What do you do?

Show the solution

Two instincts, both wrong. Fix it silently and twelve people find their number dropped 9% overnight with no explanation — you have destroyed trust in a report you inherited. Leave it because it is not your fault and you are now knowingly publishing a wrong number, which is worse than the original author's honest mistake.

The sequence

  1. Quantify precisely, and split the causes. How much of the 9% is cancelled orders and how much is the staff channel? They are different arguments: cancelled orders are clearly an error, while “should staff count as customers?” is a legitimate definitional question someone may have decided deliberately.
  2. Check whether it was deliberate. Read the repo history, the old tickets, the dashboard's own documentation. The departed analyst may have had a reason, and inheriting a decision is different from inheriting a bug. If you cannot tell, say so.
  3. Find out what the number is used for. Twelve weekly users means decisions are attached. If it feeds a board KPI or anyone's bonus, the correction has consequences well beyond accuracy, and it needs the owner's decision, not yours.
  4. Take it to the business owner with the fix and the impact, not the problem. “Active customers is overstated by 9%: 6pp is cancelled orders, which is a bug, and 3pp is the staff channel, which may have been intentional. Here's the corrected series back to 2023 so the trend is unbroken. I'd like to publish the correction on the 1st with a note. The trend is unaffected — only the level moves.”
  5. Restate history, do not just fix forward. A metric that changes level mid-series is worse than either version. Recompute the whole history on the new definition and publish both series once, side by side.
  6. Announce it before anyone notices. One paragraph to all twelve users: what changed, why, the size, the date, and that the trend is unaffected. Then version the metric spec — v1.1, with the restatement impact and the approver.

The sentence that makes this easy

“The trend is unaffected; only the level moves.” Almost every metric correction has this property, and it is what most users actually care about. Leading with it turns an alarming announcement into an administrative one.

And the part people forget: this is a gift, not a burden. Finding and correcting an inherited error, handled this way, is one of the fastest ways to build a reputation for reliability — provided you announce it rather than being found out. Fix it in week one of owning the dashboard, before the number is “yours”.

Task 15 · 30 minutes

Marketing wants to know whether a new homepage layout increased conversion. They launched it to 100% of traffic three weeks ago, with no holdout. Conversion is up 7% versus the previous three weeks. Write your assessment.

Show the solution

The honest headline: this cannot be answered with confidence, and the reason is a decision made before the launch, not a limitation of the data. Say that once, without blame, then extract what value remains.

Everything else that changed in three weeks

A before/after comparison attributes to the layout every other difference between the two periods:

  • Seasonality. This is a pharmacy. If the comparison spans the start of cold and flu season or the pollen season, conversion rises on its own — high-intent, single-item purchases. A 7% lift is entirely within what seasonality delivers.
  • Marketing spend and mix. If paid spend rose, traffic composition changed. Brand traffic converts several times better than generic.
  • Other releases. Almost certainly something else shipped in three weeks — checkout, payment methods, delivery promise, an app release.
  • Novelty. Returning users behave differently with a new layout for a few weeks in either direction.
  • Consent and tracking. If the banner or tagging changed with the release, the denominator changed and the “lift” is measurement.

What you can still do — four things, in order of value

  1. Segment by new versus returning. A layout change should affect new visitors most. If the lift is entirely in returning users, suspect novelty or a mix shift.
  2. Hold traffic mix constant. Recompute conversion within each channel, then re-blend at the old channel mix — the mix-versus-rate decomposition again. If the whole lift is mix, the layout did nothing.
  3. Compare against last year's same weeks. Crude, but it removes the seasonal component that a three-weeks-prior comparison cannot.
  4. Find a natural control. Did the layout ship to all three markets simultaneously? If Netherlands got it two weeks later, you have a staggered rollout, and that is a genuine quasi-experiment — the best evidence available here, and it is free.

The assessment

Conversion is up 7% since launch. I can't attribute that to the layout,
and no analysis of this data will be able to — without a holdout, the
layout's effect and everything else that changed in three weeks are the
same number.

What I can say:
· 4 of the 7 points are traffic mix. Paid brand share rose over the
  period, and brand traffic converts ~2.4× generic. At last period's
  channel mix, the lift is 3%, not 7%.
· The remaining 3% is concentrated in new visitors, which is at least
  consistent with a layout effect.
· NL received the layout on 14 Feb, two weeks after DE and FR. That
  stagger is the strongest evidence we have. I can use it as a natural
  control: 3 days' work, and it gives a defensible estimate.

For the next release: a 10% holdout for two weeks costs almost nothing
and turns this from a debate into a number. I'll write the design if
that's useful.

Note the last paragraph. The lasting value of this task is not the estimate — it is making the next release measurable. Deliver that as a concrete, cheap offer rather than a lecture about experimental design, and it usually gets accepted.

Task 16 · 20 minutes

Six weeks after your margin delivery, the CFO emails: “Margin is still falling. I thought we fixed this.” The promotion defect was fixed on 29 January. Write your response plan.

Show the solution

Do not reply immediately, and do not reply defensively. You have work to do first, and it is about two hours. But acknowledge within the hour: “Looking now, back to you today.”

The four things to establish, in this order

  1. Is it actually still falling? Check for the artefacts first: is she looking at an incomplete month? At a metric restated by the v1.2 definition change? At blended margin including something out of scope? A meaningful share of “it's still falling” emails are looking at a partial period.
  2. Did the fix work? You committed to verifying this on 5 February — so you already know, and that is the point of the verification date. Confirm zero lines above 30% discount since 29 January, and quantify the recovery: if the run-rate improved by €165k/month, the fix worked exactly as predicted.
  3. Was the fix ever going to be enough? This is the crux. The promotion defect was 0.42 of 2.40 points — 17%. The largest driver was mix, and D2 (the paid-search objective change) was a Q1 pilot with a review on 3 March that has not concluded yet. So margin continuing to fall is exactly what the analysis predicted. You said this in January; she has forgotten, which is normal.
  4. Is there a new driver? Re-run the decomposition on the last six weeks. It takes an hour now, because you built the model layer in Stage 07. Check specifically whether the residual grew — that is where an unnoticed fifth driver would appear.

The reply

The promotion fix worked — zero lines above 30% discount since 29 Jan,
recovering about €165k a month, in line with the €2.0M/yr estimate.

But that was 17% of the problem. Half the decline was product mix, and the
paid-search objective change (D2) is still a pilot — the review is 3 March.
Until that lands, mix continues to cost us roughly €460k a month. So margin
still falling is what the January analysis predicted; the recovery plan was
€5.6-6.8M against an €11.5M decline, phased over FY26.

Two things worth flagging from re-running the decomposition this morning:
· No new driver. The residual is stable at 0.4pp.
· February's figure you're looking at is a partial month — cost data lags
  about 8 days on drop-ship, so mid-month margin always reads low. Full
  February will be available on the 9th.

If the pace of recovery isn't acceptable, the lever is D3 — the cold-chain
surcharge, which Vogel was to bring on 12 Feb and hasn't. That's another
€0.9M and it's the one decision still open.

Three things this reply does. It reports the verification you promised, which is why the verification date existed. It reframes without defensiveness — “this is what the analysis predicted” is a factual statement, and it lands only because the phasing was written down in January. And it ends by naming the open decision and who owns it, which converts a complaint into a next step. Note that nowhere does it say “I told you so”, and nowhere does it apologise for something that is working as designed.

Task 17 · 25 minutes

You have three days on a new project and discover on day one that the key table has no history — it is overwritten nightly, so the question (“how did our assortment change over the year?”) is unanswerable for the past. What do you do with the remaining two and a half days?

Show the solution

The wrong answer is to spend two days constructing a heroic reconstruction from adjacent tables and deliver something with error bars wider than the effect. The second wrong answer is to report “not possible” and hand back the time.

Hour one: tell the stakeholder, today

Not tomorrow. A day-one discovery reported on day one is competence; the same discovery reported on day three is a failure. One paragraph: what is missing, why the question as asked cannot be answered for the past, and the three options below with their costs.

Then offer three options and let them choose

  1. Start the clock (½ day). Build a nightly snapshot of the table now. Cheap, permanent, and in twelve months this question becomes trivially answerable. Do this regardless of which option they pick — it is the highest-value half-day available, it prevents the same conversation next year, and nobody will ever ask you to justify it.
  2. Reconstruct from event traces (1½ days, uncertain). Assortment history may be recoverable from what left footprints: order lines tell you what was sold, goods-receipts what was bought, price-change logs and product-created dates give partial timing. This yields a reconstruction of sellable-and-selling assortment, not listed assortment — a different quantity. Viable only if the stakeholder accepts that substitution, stated plainly.
  3. Answer the adjacent question that is answerable (1 day). Often the best option. If the real decision is “are we carrying too much long-tail range?”, then current assortment with twelve months of sales history answers it — SKUs listed now with zero or trivial sales. No history needed, and it likely serves the decision better than the question as asked.

Take option 3 to them as a recommendation, not as a menu. Menus push the decision back onto someone with less context than you.

The reply

Day-one problem: dim_assortment is overwritten nightly, so there is no
history. "How did assortment change over the year" can't be answered for
the past by anyone, with any method.

What I'd do with the three days instead:

· Half a day, starting today: snapshot the table nightly from now on. This
  question becomes answerable in future, and I'd want to do this whatever
  we decide.

· Two days on the question I think you're actually asking. If the decision
  is whether we carry too much long-tail range, I can answer that from
  current assortment plus 12 months of sales — how many listed SKUs sold
  fewer than N units, what they cost us in stock and in write-offs. No
  history required.

If you specifically need the historical trend, I can attempt a partial
reconstruction from order lines and goods receipts, but it would measure
what we *sold*, not what we *listed*, and I wouldn't put it in front of
anyone senior. I'd rather do the two-day version.

The transferable lesson: an unanswerable question almost always sits next to an answerable one that serves the same decision. Finding it requires knowing the decision — which is why Stage 00 asks for it. And when data does not exist, creating it from today is a real deliverable, permanently valuable, and almost always the cheapest thing on the list.

Task 18 · 40 minutes · the exit exam

You start a new job on Monday as the first data analyst at a mid-size online retailer. There is no warehouse, no dbt, one Postgres replica, forty abandoned dashboards, and a queue of thirty-one requests. Write your first thirty days.

Show the solution

There is no single right answer, but there is a clear structure, and a clear set of ways to get it wrong. The temptations are: start clearing the queue (you will never stop, and you will encode wrong definitions into thirty-one answers), or start building infrastructure (three months of invisible work and no credibility to spend when you need it).

Week 1 · Learn, and ship nothing durable

  • The reading list: last two board decks, monthly management accounts, the finance definition of revenue and margin, dashboard usage stats, six months of tickets.
  • Eight people, four questions each, answers written down and attributed.
  • The one-page map: where each fact is born, which copy is authoritative, and the contradictions between them.
  • One trivial thing shipped end to end, purely to time the access process.
  • No dashboards. Anything you build this week encodes definitions you do not yet understand.

Week 2 · Triage the queue, publicly

  • Every one of the thirty-one requests gets a verdict: take, answer now, reduce, decline. Expect roughly: 8 already answerable from something that exists, 6 are the same question in different words, 9 have no decision attached, 5 are real, 3 belong to another team.
  • Publish the triage. This is the single highest-leverage political act of your first month: it makes your queue visible, shows you are not ignoring anyone, and establishes that requests get assessed rather than absorbed. It also surfaces the duplicate cluster, which is usually one metric definition nobody agrees on.
  • Answer the eight easy ones immediately. Fast visible wins buy you the room to say no later.

Week 3 · One real project, done properly

  • Pick the highest-value take — ideally one whose sponsor is senior and whose data is tractable. Run all ten stages on it, visibly, including the charter and the metric spec.
  • The point is not the answer. It is to demonstrate the process once, so that the charter, the metric spec and the decision log become normal rather than novel. Establish these in month one or you will never establish them.
  • Reconcile something to the general ledger. It will not match. That discovery is your first genuinely valuable finding, and it is the argument for everything you want to build next.

Week 4 · Build the smallest durable thing, and say what you found

  • Not a warehouse. A metrics schema of five or six certified views on the replica, with the definitions documented and the exclusions in one place. This is 80% of the value of a warehouse for 2% of the effort, and it is defensible on day 30.
  • Write the reconciliation test and schedule it. Your first automated guard.
  • Delete or archive the forty dashboards — after checking usage, and after telling their owners. Anything with zero views in ninety days goes. This is real work: every abandoned dashboard is a wrong number waiting to be quoted.
  • A one-page memo to your manager: what I found, what I fixed, what I recommend, what I need. Include the estate map, the triage summary, and the ledger discrepancy. This document is what gets you the headcount, the tooling budget, or the mandate.

What you deliberately do not do in thirty days

Do not propose a warehouse migration, do not pick a BI tool, do not hire, and do not attempt a metrics layer covering the whole business. You do not yet know enough, and a plan proposed in week two will be visibly naive by week ten — which costs you the credibility you will need when you propose the real one in month three.

The principle underneath all of it

In month one, credibility is the scarce resource, not time. Everything above is sequenced to spend time acquiring credibility, and to avoid spending credibility you have not yet earned. Fast visible answers earn it; a published triage protects it; one project run properly demonstrates what you are for; and the ledger discrepancy is the evidence that makes your month-three proposal impossible to refuse.

Glossary

Analytical terms, then pharmacy ones. Defined the way a colleague would explain them, not the way a textbook would.

Analysis and data

GrainWhat one row of a table means. “One order line” or “one shipment” or “one product per month”. The first thing to establish about any table and the most common source of silent error.
Fan-outWhat happens when you join a one-row-per-X table to a one-row-per-Y table where each X has several Ys: X's values are duplicated. If X carries money, the money multiplies.
System of recordWhere a fact is created and is authoritative. Everything downstream is a copy, and copies lag, filter and drift.
ReconciliationComparing your number to an independent source that nobody in the argument controls — usually the general ledger. The only check that validates a whole pipeline at once.
Mix effectHow much a blended rate moved because the composition changed, holding each group's own rate constant. Its partner is the rate effect: how much moved because the groups' own rates changed.
Simpson's paradoxEvery subgroup moves one way and the total moves the other, because the weights shifted. Not a curiosity — the most common way a performance conversation goes wrong.
Percentage point (pp)The unit for a difference between two percentages. 19.2% to 16.8% is 2.4pp, which is a 12.5% relative decline. Confusing the two is a factor-of-five error.
CensoringNot having observed something yet because not enough time has passed. A customer who joined last month cannot have missed a 90-day refill. Including them drags retention up and is the leading error in cohort analysis.
Survivorship biasMeasuring only the things that are still present. Deactivated products vanish from the product table, so historic availability improves by deletion.
RestatementA historical number changing after the fact, because late data arrived — returns, invoices, claims. Means every figure needs an “as at” date.
SCD type 2A dimension table holding one row per version of an entity, with validity dates. Powerful and dangerous: join without a date or is_current predicate and every fact multiplies.
CounterfactualWhat would have happened otherwise. Never in your data by construction, which is why “what did we lose?” questions need estimation methods and ranges, not queries.
Minimum cell sizeThe smallest denominator for which you will quote a rate. Protects against nonsense from small segments, and against identifying individuals.
Certified metricA metric with a written spec, a named business owner, a version history and a reconciliation target. As opposed to an exploratory number, which is fine to compute and not fine to publish.
Semantic layerOne place where metric definitions live, so every tool computes them the same way. Whether it is dbt metrics, a BI model, or a folder of certified views matters far less than that it exists once.

Pharmacy and pharmaceutical e-commerce

RxPrescription-only medicine. Requires a valid prescription and pharmacist validation. Price regulated in several EU markets, cannot be advertised to the public, low and fixed margin.
OTCOver-the-counter medicine, sold without prescription. Freely priced, promotion-sensitive, strongly seasonal.
ParapharmacyEverything else a pharmacy sells: cosmetics, supplements, infant formula, medical devices. Behaves like ordinary retail, with infant formula as the near-zero-margin exception.
E-prescriptionA prescription issued and transmitted electronically. Its adoption is the structural force reshaping online pharmacy volumes and mix.
PZN / CIP / EANNational and international product identifier codes (Germany, France, and the retail barcode). The same molecule has different codes per market, and repackaging changes the code without changing the product.
ATCAnatomical Therapeutic Chemical classification — the international therapeutic hierarchy. The only sane way to compare products across markets, and the basis for splitting chronic from acute.
Batch / lotA production run, identified so that every unit can be traced to a customer. Legally required for recalls, and the reason inventory is not fungible.
Cold chainProducts requiring continuous 2–8 °C storage and transport. Ship alone in validated packaging at many times normal parcel cost, so one such line splits an order into two shipments.
GDPGood Distribution Practice — the rules governing storage, transport, temperature control and supplier licensing. Why “just source it cheaper” often is not available.
PharmacovigilanceMonitoring and reporting of adverse drug reactions. A suspected reaction reaching the company creates a legal reporting obligation on a defined clock.
Qualified PersonThe named individual legally accountable for pharmaceutical quality and release decisions. In a recall, they are who you deliver to and who decides scope.
Generic substitutionDispensing an equivalent generic in place of the prescribed brand, where law permits. Means a branded stock-out is often not a lost sale — and may improve margin.
Co-paymentThe part of a reimbursed prescription the patient pays. The rest comes from an insurer, later, and may be rejected — so the buyer is not the payer.
Drop-shipShipping direct from a wholesaler when own stock is short. Means “out of stock” in the warehouse is not out of stock to the customer, only slower and more expensive.
Article 9 dataGDPR's special categories, including health data. Prescription detail falls here. Technical access is not legal authorisation, and this distinction is not the analyst's to resolve alone.

One last thing

Of the ten stages, exactly two involve writing queries. That ratio is not an accident of how this document was written — it is the job. The analysts who become indispensable are not the ones with the best SQL; they are the ones who work out what was really being asked, who prove their number before defending it, who know which questions their data cannot answer, and who can stand in a room and make a finding land without anyone having to lose.

All of that is learnable, and none of it is taught. Start with the twenty questions and the one-screen checklist. Everything else in here is commentary on those two pages.
