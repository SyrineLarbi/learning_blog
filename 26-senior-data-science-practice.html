The single most useful heuristic in this document

A suspiciously good result is a bug report, not a celebration. 0.99 AUC on a human-behaviour problem means you have leaked something — every time. The most damaging failures in data science announce themselves as success, which is exactly why they survive to production.

Part I

Before modelling

Topic 01

Problem framing

What a senior knows
  • "Predict attrition" is not a problem statement. "Give a manager a ranked list of five people to talk to this month" is — and it dictates the metric, the threshold, the interface and the definition of done.
  • The cost of a false positive and a false negative are almost never equal, and the ratio between them is the threshold decision. That is a business input, not a technical one.
  • If a rule works, use the rule. Auditable, instant, free, explainable — and often nearly as accurate. Reaching for a model first is the most common framing failure.
  • Ask what happens to the prediction. A model whose output nobody acts on has changed nothing, whatever its metrics say.
  • Establish the baseline before anything else. "83% accurate" is meaningless if 95% of employees stay.
Anti-patterns
  • Starting from the available data rather than the decision — building what the dataset allows instead of what anyone needs.
  • Accepting a stakeholder's proposed solution as the requirement. Half of all requests are a solution wearing a requirement's costume.
  • No baseline, so there is nothing to compare against and no way to claim value.
  • Choosing accuracy on an imbalanced problem, which rewards predicting the majority class.
  • Solving the interesting problem when the actual bottleneck was that nobody can find last year's reviews.
The kickoff checklist
  • What decision changes, and who makes it?
  • What happens today, without this?
  • Which error costs more, and roughly by how much?
  • What is the baseline, and what would "good enough to act on" be?
  • Would a rule or a report solve 80% of this?
  • What does the person see, and what do they do next?
Before you write any code

You can state the problem, the decision, the baseline, the metric and the cost asymmetry in five sentences — and someone who is not an engineer has agreed with them.

Topic 02 · the one that ends projects

Data hygiene and leakage

What a senior knows
  • Leakage is information in training that will not exist at prediction time. It produces excellent validation scores and a model that fails in production — and because the symptom is success, nobody investigates.
  • The test: would this value have existed, with this value, at the moment I need the prediction? Applied to every feature, explicitly.
  • Split first, transform second. A scaler, an imputation median or an encoder fitted on all the data has leaked the test set into training.
  • Time-ordered data is never split randomly — that is predicting the past from the future.
  • Missingness has a mechanism. If the unhappiest employees skip the survey, imputing their satisfaction biases the result, and the fact that it is missing is itself information.
Anti-patterns
  • The classic in HR data: exit_interview_completed, handover_uploaded, access_revoked_date as features. Perfect predictors, all known only after the decision.
  • Group leakage — the same employee in train and test, so the model memorises the person.
  • An aggregate feature ("team attrition rate") computed over the whole period, including the outcome of the row you are predicting.
  • Target encoding computed outside the training folds.
  • Filling missing values with the mean and never recording that they were missing.
The patterns

A pipeline object that holds preprocessing and model together, fitted as one thing and applied as one thing. It makes contamination structurally difficult rather than merely discouraged.

A was_missing flag for every imputed column. An imputed value that looks measured is worse than a null, because nobody downstream can tell — and the flag is often predictive in its own right.

Validate on a genuinely later time period before trusting anything.

Before you trust a score
  • Did I split before fitting any transformation?
  • For each feature: known at prediction time?
  • Does one feature dominate importance absurdly? That is usually leakage, not a discovery.
  • Are duplicates removed before splitting?
Topic 03

Experiment design

What a senior knows
  • Significant, material and stable are three different questions that people collapse into one. With enough data almost everything is significant; only some of it is worth acting on, and less of it is still there next month.
  • Decide what you will test before looking. Testing twenty things guarantees roughly one false positive at the conventional threshold.
  • Effect size is the number a decision can use. "Significant at p < 0.01" is not actionable; "three percentage points, about eleven people, roughly £40,000 of replacement cost" is.
  • Regression to the mean manufactures fake wins. Target the worst-performing teams, intervene, measure again — they improve, and so would they have without you.
  • A holdout group matters even when it feels unnecessary, because without one you cannot claim the effect and someone will ask you to.
Anti-patterns
  • Slicing until something looks dramatic, in cells of nine people.
  • Stopping a test as soon as it reaches significance — which biases the result upward.
  • Reporting a p-value as the probability the effect is real, which it is not.
  • Claiming causation from observational data because the correlation is strong.
  • No control group, then attributing an improvement to the intervention.
The patterns

Write the analysis plan first — the metric, the split, the duration, the stopping rule, the subgroups you will look at. Then run it.

Always name the four alternatives to causation: reverse direction, a confounder, selection in the data, chance. Being able to rule them out — or admit you cannot — is the whole of causal honesty.

Report the range, not the point. "0.81 ± 0.02" is a result; "0.81" alone may be luck.

Before you report a finding
  • How big is the sample in the smallest cell I am quoting?
  • Did I decide this comparison before or after seeing the data?
  • Have I stated the effect size in units the business uses?
Topic 04

Feature engineering discipline

What a senior knows
  • Better features beat a better model, almost always. Swapping random forest for gradient boosting might buy a point; a feature that encodes something the model could not otherwise see can buy ten.
  • Ratios and differences carry more than raw values. Salary relative to the department median encodes "underpaid for peers"; salary alone does not.
  • Trees do not need scaling; distance-based methods and networks do. Which is why you will not see scaling in an XGBoost pipeline, and its absence is not an oversight.
  • Fewer features is often better — drop near-constants, drop one of any highly correlated pair, drop anything you cannot explain.
  • In this domain, some features are excluded deliberately: protected attributes and their proxies — postcode, school, working pattern, career gaps.
Anti-patterns
  • Generating hundreds of automated features nobody can interpret, then being unable to explain a prediction.
  • Encoding categories as 1..50, inventing an ordering the model will happily use.
  • One-hot encoding a 400-value column and wondering why training slowed down.
  • Computing a feature differently in the notebook and in the serving code — training/serving skew, which degrades production quality for no visible reason.
  • Binning a variable, throwing away information, because a chart looked nicer.
The patterns

Write the feature logic once, in one module, and call it from both training and serving. That is 95% of what a feature store buys, for none of the cost.

Aggregate features exclude the row itself, or you have leaked the target.

Domain knowledge is the highest-yield source of features — one conversation with an HR business partner about why people actually leave beats a week of automated generation.

Before you add a feature
  • Known at prediction time?
  • Can I explain what it means to a stakeholder?
  • Is it computed identically in training and serving?
  • Is it a proxy for something I deliberately excluded?
Part II

Modelling

Topic 05

Modelling discipline

What a senior knows
  • Start with logistic regression. It is the baseline and sometimes the answer — fast, calibrated, and its coefficients can be explained to a person whose job is affected.
  • On tabular data, gradient boosting wins. Not deep learning. It trains in seconds, tells you which features mattered, and beats neural networks on this shape of problem consistently.
  • Bagging cuts variance; boosting cuts bias. Being able to say which one you need, and why, is the difference between choosing and guessing.
  • The train–test gap is the diagnosis. Both low means underfitting; train high and test much lower means overfitting; test higher than train is suspicious and usually a leak.
  • Interpretability can be the correct engineering choice even at slightly lower accuracy, when the decision affects someone's employment.
Anti-patterns
  • Reaching for deep learning on a few thousand tabular rows — slower, worse, and less explainable.
  • Tuning hyperparameters against the test set, then reporting that score as if it were held out.
  • Two hundred tuning trials selected on one validation split, which finds the configuration that suits that split.
  • Stacking five models for half a point of AUC that nobody can maintain or explain.
  • No early stopping on a boosted model, so it overfits quietly.
The patterns

Baseline → logistic regression → gradient boosting → stop. The gap between the second and third tells you whether extra complexity buys anything at all.

Cross-validation for the spread, not just the mean. 0.81 ± 0.15 means your single number was luck.

Keep a test set the tuning never touched, and treat the tuned score as optimistic until it agrees.

Before you call a model done
  • Does it beat the baseline, and by how much in business terms?
  • How many times have I looked at the test set?
  • Can I explain a single prediction to the person it concerns?
Topic 06

Evaluation and statistics

What a senior knows
  • Never accuracy on imbalanced data. Predict "stays" for everyone and score 95% while being useless.
  • PR-AUC, not ROC-AUC, when the positive class is rare — ROC looks flatteringly high because true negatives are abundant.
  • The precision/recall trade is a business decision, not a technical one. Which error costs more sets the threshold, and leaving it at 0.5 is not neutral — it is an unexamined choice.
  • Calibration matters whenever a human reads the number. If the model says 0.7, do 70% of those people actually leave?
  • The base-rate trap: a model catching 90% of leavers and wrongly flagging 10% of stayers, on a 5% base rate, is right about 32% of the people it flags. Nothing is wrong with it — the rare event does that.
Anti-patterns
  • Reporting F1 alone, which hides which side you are failing on.
  • Quoting R² without context, or being pleased when it rises after adding features.
  • Comparing models on different splits, or on a metric that was not agreed in advance.
  • Presenting a probability as a certainty — "will leave" rather than "worth a conversation".
  • Evaluating only in aggregate, so a model that works well for one group and badly for another looks fine.
The patterns

Always report against the baseline, in one table, on one split, with the metric named in advance.

Evaluate per subgroup, always. An overall 0.72 can be 0.75 for one group and 0.51 for another, and the single number will never tell you.

Translate the metric into consequences: "of the ten people it flags, about three would have left" is a sentence a manager can act on.

Before you present a number
  • Is this metric right for the base rate?
  • What is it compared against?
  • Have I checked it per subgroup?
  • If a human sees the probability, is it calibrated?
Part III

Production

Topic 07

MLOps essentials

What a senior knows
  • An ML system can fail without failing. Nothing errors, latency is fine, the dashboard is green — and the predictions have become worthless because the world moved. No ordinary monitoring catches that.
  • Behaviour comes from code and data, so it can break with nobody changing anything.
  • Reproducibility requires the data version, not just the commit. Without a snapshot identifier, "why did the metrics change?" is unanswerable.
  • Save the pipeline, not the model — the preprocessing is part of the model — along with library versions, feature order and the metrics it achieved.
  • Rolling back a model must be as fast as rolling back code.
Anti-patterns
  • A model artefact with no record of which code, data or parameters produced it.
  • Preprocessing reimplemented in the serving code, so it drifts from training.
  • No gate on registration, so a run that completed replaces a better model.
  • Retraining on a schedule without validating the input data first — a broken upstream load silently produces a model trained on garbage, and every step reports success.
  • A notebook as the deployment artefact.
The patterns

Three separate pipelines: code CI, training (with an automatic gate — do not register a model that does not beat the incumbent on the held-out set), and deployment. Training and deploying are different decisions with different risk.

Validate the data before training: schema unchanged, row count in range, no column suddenly 90% null, distributions roughly where they were.

Shadow mode — the new model predicts, its output is logged and unused. Real traffic, zero risk, and the most under-used deployment option there is.

Before a model serves anyone
  • Can I reproduce this exact model from recorded inputs?
  • Is the preprocessing the same object in training and serving?
  • Which version is live, and what do I roll back to?
Topic 08

LLM and RAG practice

What a senior knows
  • The deterministic system computes; the model narrates. Calculate the number where it is reproducible and auditable, and let the model write the sentence around it — then the worst hallucination is a clumsy sentence rather than a wrong figure.
  • Hallucination is structural. The model produces plausible continuations with no truth flag and no reliable "I don't know". You cannot prompt it away; you engineer around it.
  • Most bad output is missing context, not bad wording. The right context is the biggest lever by a wide margin; rewording is the smallest.
  • A schema is a hallucination control — an enum of seven values cannot become an eighth. It removes a failure mode rather than reducing it.
  • When RAG is wrong there are four possible causes — coverage, recall, precision, faithfulness — and you must be able to tell them apart. Without that split, teams tune chunk size for weeks when the document was never ingested.
  • Prompt injection is defended by the trust boundary, in code, never by an instruction in the prompt.
Anti-patterns
  • Asking the model to do arithmetic, or to produce a number that will be read as fact.
  • Free-text output where a schema was possible, so nothing downstream can be validated.
  • Building RAG before checking whether the corpus fits in a cached prompt.
  • Chunks with no document title or section heading — "…must be approved by HR" is useless without knowing which policy.
  • Fine-tuning to add facts. Facts in weights are uncitable, stale on change and unverifiable.
  • No eval set, so nobody dares touch the prompt and a superstition stays frozen in production.
The patterns

Twenty graded examples in a file, run on every prompt, model or retrieval change. A day's work, and it is the difference between engineering and vibes.

Assert the citations: if the model cites chunk ids, check each was actually in the context. A dozen lines that convert an invisible failure into a caught one.

Read the fully rendered prompt when output is bad — the actual bytes, every variable interpolated. A surprising share of prompt bugs are visible immediately.

Before an AI feature ships
  • Does any number in the output come from the model rather than from a computation?
  • Is the output shape constrained?
  • Is there an eval set, and does it run automatically?
  • Could a hostile document in the context cause an action the user could not perform themselves?
Topic 09

Monitoring in production

What a senior knows
  • Four layers, and most teams monitor only the first: the service, the inputs, the outputs, and the outcomes.
  • Data drift is detectable immediately and without labels — which makes it the most valuable monitor you can add. A reorganisation changes every department code and the model degrades silently.
  • Concept drift is only visible once outcomes arrive, which for attrition is months later. So accuracy is always measured in arrears, and input drift is your only early warning.
  • Prediction distribution shifts are a cheap alarm: suddenly 40% high-risk where it was 8% means something upstream changed.
  • A model that changes behaviour poisons its own training data. Managers act on flags, so future data reflects the interventions — and the model appears to get worse precisely when it is working.
Anti-patterns
  • Monitoring only latency and error rate, so a silently wrong model looks perfectly healthy.
  • Retraining automatically on data shaped by the model's own predictions, with no holdout that was not acted on.
  • No alert on the prediction distribution, so a broken feature pipeline shows up in a quarterly review.
  • No owner and no review date — launched, celebrated, unowned, quietly wrong six months later.
The patterns

Log every prediction with its model version and its inputs, so "why did this score change?" is answerable — and someone will ask.

Keep a holdout that is never acted upon, or record which predictions were acted on, so you can still measure the model rather than your own interventions.

An owner and a review date for every deployed model. A model nobody owns is a liability with a good reputation.

Before you call it live
  • Would I notice input drift within a week?
  • Can I answer "which model produced this prediction"?
  • Who owns this, and when is it next reviewed?

Topic 10

The mistakes list, by area

Problem framing

Starting from the data · no baseline · accepting the proposed solution · no cost asymmetry · building a model where a rule works.

Data hygiene & leakage

Post-outcome features · fitting transforms before splitting · random splits on time data · group leakage · imputing without a flag.

Modelling

Deep learning on small tabular data · tuning on the test set · no early stopping · ensembles nobody can maintain.

Evaluation

Accuracy on imbalanced data · ROC-AUC on a rare class · F1 alone · no subgroup breakdown · threshold left at 0.5.

Code quality

Logic only in notebooks · cells run out of order · no functions · hardcoded paths · outputs committed with real data in them.

MLOps

No data version · preprocessing duplicated in serving · no registration gate · no rollback · a notebook as the artefact.

LLM / RAG

Model-produced numbers · free text where a schema fits · RAG before checking the context window · chunks without titles · no eval set.

Ethics & fairness

Proxies left in · no subgroup evaluation · no explanation · no human override · aggregates below a safe cell size.

Communication

Leading with method · quoting metrics instead of consequences · no recommendation · hiding uncertainty · no written record.

Mindset

Celebrating a suspiciously good score · optimising the metric instead of the decision · finishing the notebook and calling it done.

Topic 11

Communication and stakeholders

What a senior knows
  • Answer first. The conclusion goes at the top. Engineers write chronologically — context, method, findings, conclusion — which is the order they discovered it in and the wrong order to read it in.
  • Nobody outside the team cares about the model metric. "PR-AUC improved to 0.71" answers no business question; "of the ten people it flags, about three would have left" does.
  • State the uncertainty yourself. It is more credible than having it found for you, and it is how you are trusted with the next decision.
  • Give a recommendation. Presenting options with no recommendation is abdication dressed as neutrality — you have the most information in the room.
  • The override rate is your best feedback signal. Consistently high overrides mean the model is wrong or the interface is not trusted, and nobody will say so in a survey.
Anti-patterns
  • Presenting the journey — data cleaning, the models tried, the tuning — instead of the answer.
  • A confusion matrix on a slide for a non-technical audience.
  • False certainty to end a conversation quickly. "I don't know yet, I'll know by Wednesday" is a complete answer.
  • Overclaiming causation because the stakeholder wants a cause.
  • No written record, so the finding is re-litigated in three months.
The memo structure

Bottom line · context · findings · risks · what I propose · what I need answered. Six headings. A busy reader can act correctly after the first one, and the last one turns a report into a decision.

One chart, annotated. A line on the chart saying "reorganisation" where the step change is explains more than any amount of styling — and it travels with the screenshot, which is how the chart will actually be seen.

Before you send it
  • Can someone read only the first paragraph and act correctly?
  • Is every number in the units the business uses?
  • Have I said what I recommend, and what I am not claiming?

The operating principles

The whole document, compressed to eleven lines
①  ⭐ FRAME THE DECISION, NOT THE PREDICTION. What changes, who
   acts, and which error costs more.
②  ESTABLISH THE BASELINE FIRST. A metric without one is a
   number without a meaning.
③  IF A RULE WORKS, USE THE RULE.
④  SPLIT FIRST, TRANSFORM SECOND. Always.
⑤  ⭐ FOR EVERY FEATURE: WOULD THIS HAVE BEEN KNOWN AT
   PREDICTION TIME?
⑥  A SUSPICIOUSLY GOOD RESULT IS A BUG REPORT.
⑦  MATCH THE METRIC TO THE BASE RATE, AND EVALUATE PER
   SUBGROUP.
⑧  THE THRESHOLD IS A BUSINESS DECISION. Do not leave it at
   the default and call that neutral.
⑨  THE DETERMINISTIC SYSTEM COMPUTES; THE MODEL NARRATES.
⑩  ⭐ VERSION THE DATA, NOT JUST THE CODE — or nothing is
   reproducible.
⑪  ANSWER FIRST, IN THEIR UNITS, WITH A RECOMMENDATION.

The default stack

LayerThe sensible default, and why
LanguagePython. The ecosystem is the reason, and it is a sufficient one.
Data handlingpandas for exploration; a columnar format (Parquet) for storage, because CSV is untyped and uncompressed.
Tabular modelsscikit-learn for baselines and pipelines; XGBoost or a peer for the real model.
PipelinesA scikit-learn Pipeline object — preprocessing and model as one fitted artefact. The structural defence against leakage.
TrackingAny experiment tracker, from day one. It solves "which run produced this?" for almost no commitment.
ServingFastAPI with the pipeline loaded once at startup — and inference in a plain def, not an async def, because it is CPU-bound.
LLM accessA provider API behind an adapter, so switching is one file. Plus an eval set, which is not optional.
Batch over real-timeWherever the prediction is not needed at the moment of the request. Cheaper, simpler, easier to audit.
