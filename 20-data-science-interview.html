Interview preparation · 6 of 6 · data science

They can teach you a library. They cannot teach you to notice the leak.

Eight rounds of the ML and data science loop, every question graded weak / good / senior — with the follow-up you will actually get, what the interviewer is really scoring, and the honest wording for what you have not done.

Rounds · 8 Graded · weak / good / senior Focus · framing, leakage, validation, metrics Companion · an AI engineering loop file
How to use this

Answer out loud before opening the reveal. The gap between your answer and the senior tier is your study list, and it disappears if you read the answer first — you will believe you knew it.

The honest gap row is not a consolation prize. It is wording that lets you decline a claim cleanly, and it scores better than a bluff that collapses one follow-up later.

What they are actually scoring

Data science loops are unusually good at finding people who can fit a model and cannot frame a problem. That is what most of these rounds are for.

Round 01 · the highest-signal round

The case round

A vague business problem and forty-five minutes. They are watching whether you reach for an algorithm or for a definition.

The six questions — run them out loud, in this order
1  WHAT DECISION does this change, and who makes it?
2  WHAT IS THE UNIT of prediction? (the grain)
3  WHAT EXACTLY IS THE TARGET? every clause is a decision
4  WHEN is it predicted, and WHAT IS KNOWABLE THEN?
     ⭐ this one question prevents most leakage
5  WHAT IS THE BASELINE? (the current rule / majority / naive)
6  WHAT DOES EACH ERROR COST? → this sets the metric AND the
     threshold, not convention

⭐ SPEND FIVE MINUTES HERE. Candidates who go straight to "I'd use
  XGBoost" have answered a question nobody asked and have nothing
  left to say for forty minutes.

B1.1Case · the classic prompt

"We're losing customers. Can you build something to help?"

Reveal — answer out loud first
Weak

"I'd build a churn model — gradient boosting on customer features, evaluate with AUC." Every part is defensible and it skips the entire round. You have chosen a target, a unit, a metric and an intervention silently, and at least two of them are probably wrong.

Good

Questions before methods:

  • What would we do with the answer? A retention offer? A call? Changing onboarding? Each implies a different model — and if the answer is "we'd like to understand it", that is analysis, not a model.
  • What counts as churn here? Cancelled subscription, no purchase in 90 days, downgrade? Contractual and non-contractual churn are different problems.
  • How many can we act on? "200 a month" turns the metric into precision@200 and makes recall at 5,000 irrelevant.
  • What does the current process do? The baseline.
  • What does a wasted offer cost vs a lost customer? The threshold.
Senior

The same, plus the reframing that changes the project:

"The question underneath 'who will churn' is usually 'how do we keep more customers', and those need different work. A perfect churn predictor that surfaces people leaving because their company was acquired is accurate and useless — nothing can be done. So what I'd actually want to build is at risk and saveable, which means excluding involuntary churn from the target, or modelling the two separately."

"And I'd flag early that ranking who will churn is prediction, but 'will the offer stop them' is causal. The model can prioritise who to contact; it cannot tell us the offer works. If we want that, the cheapest version is holding out a random slice of the contacted group — a few weeks, and it converts an assumption into a number."

Then the honest close: "and before any of it I'd want to know whether the data supports it — specifically whether we have history rather than only current state, because 'what was true 90 days before they left' is unanswerable from a table that gets overwritten."

Follow-up

"Say we can only contact 200 a month. Does that change the model?" → it changes the metric and the whole evaluation. Precision@200 becomes the number; AUC becomes almost irrelevant because performance in the tail of the ranking does not affect anything you will do. It may also change the target — you now care about ranking the top slice correctly, not about calibrated probability across the whole base.

Scored on

Whether you ask before modelling, and whether "saveable" occurs to you. That reframing is the strongest single move available in this round.

Round 02

Statistics

B2.1Statistics · the definition test

"What is a p-value?"

Reveal
Weak

"The probability that the result happened by chance" or "the probability the null hypothesis is true". Both are wrong, both are extremely common, and this question exists specifically to catch them.

Good

"The probability of observing data at least as extreme as what we saw, assuming the null hypothesis is true."

"So it is a statement about the data under an assumption — not about the probability that the assumption holds. A p of 0.03 does not mean a 97% chance the effect is real."

Senior

The definition, plus what you do with it:

"And on its own it is not very useful. With a large enough sample, trivial differences become significant — a 0.02 percentage point conversion difference across ten million sessions is significant and worthless. So I'd always report the effect size and a confidence interval alongside it, because those answer 'is it big enough to act on', which is the actual question."

"I'd also be careful about where a p-value applies at all. On a full census — every order last year — there is no sampling error, so testing whether the difference is 'significant' is a category error. There the questions are materiality and stability: is it big enough to matter, and does it persist."

And the CI, stated correctly: "a 95% interval means that if we repeated the procedure many times, 95% of the intervals would contain the true value. It's a property of the procedure, not a 95% probability about this particular interval."

Follow-up

"You test 20 segments and one comes back significant. What do you conclude?" → probably nothing. At α = 0.05 you expect one false positive in 20 by chance. Correct for multiple comparisons — Bonferroni is strict, Benjamini-Hochberg FDR is usually the better choice for exploratory work — and treat the finding as a hypothesis to test on fresh data rather than a result.

Scored on

Precision on the definition, and whether you volunteer effect size unprompted. This is a screening question — getting it wrong is disproportionately costly.

B2.2Statistics · the applied one

"We selected our 20 worst-performing stores for a turnaround programme. Six months later 15 of them improved. Did it work?"

Reveal
Weak

"Yes — 75% improved, that's a strong result." It is the answer the business wants and it is unsupported. This question is testing one specific concept and rewarding the answer that misses it.

Good

"We can't tell, because of regression to the mean. The 20 worst stores were worst partly because of real problems and partly because of a bad run — a broken freezer, local roadworks, an unusual month. The bad-luck component doesn't persist, so most of them would have improved with no intervention at all."

"To answer it we'd need a comparison group of similar stores that were not selected."

Senior

Plus the design point and what you can salvage:

"The deeper problem is the design: selecting on an extreme value and then measuring change on that same value guarantees an apparent effect. It is not a flaw in the analysis, it is baked into how the group was chosen — so no amount of careful statistics rescues it after the fact."

"What I can do now: compare the 20 against their own long-run average rather than against the extreme month that selected them — that removes most of the regression effect. Or find stores that were similarly bad in the same period but weren't selected, if the selection had any arbitrary component, and use them as a control. Neither is as good as randomisation, and I'd say so."

"And for next time: if we're running this again, randomise within the bottom quartile. Half get the programme, half don't. It costs us treating some poor stores, and it converts this from an argument into a number."

Follow-up

"Where else does this bite?" → praising after a peak seems to hurt and criticising after a trough seems to help; a "high risk" segment looks better next month with no action; the best salesperson this quarter usually does worse next quarter. Any time you select on an extreme and re-measure, expect it.

Scored on

Whether you name regression to the mean at all. It is one of a small number of concepts that separate people who have analysed real interventions from people who have not.

Round 03 · where the traps are

Modelling & validation

B3.1Validation · the trap question

"Your model gets 0.97 AUC on the test set. What do you do?"

Reveal
Weak

"Ship it — that's excellent." The wrong answer, and the question is designed to see whether you say it. On a real business problem 0.97 is not a triumph, it is a symptom.

Good

"I'd be suspicious. On a real business problem that usually means leakage. I'd check:

  • Feature importances — is one feature dominating? That is the usual signature.
  • For each top feature: would this value exist, with this value, at prediction time?
  • Was anything fitted before the split — a scaler, an encoder, target encoding?
  • Is the split right for the data — temporal? grouped by entity?
  • Duplicate rows landing on both sides.
Senior

Same instinct, plus the specific hunt and the wider point:

"The fastest diagnostic is to drop the top feature and re-run. If AUC collapses to something plausible, that feature is the leak and I go and find out what it actually is. Often it turns out to be something recorded after the outcome — a cancellation reason, a status field that gets overwritten, an aggregate computed over the whole history rather than as-at."

"And I'd check the target definition itself. A surprisingly common case is that a feature is a near-restatement of the target — days_since_last_login in a model where churn is defined as inactivity. That is not a leaky feature so much as a circular definition, and it needs fixing in the spec, not the pipeline."

"The general habit: a suspiciously good score is an alarm, not a success." Distrusting your own good result is the most valuable instinct in modelling, and it is the one thing that separates people who have shipped a model that failed in production from people who haven't yet.

Follow-up

"How would you prevent it structurally?" → build features as-at: pick a cut-off per row and compute every feature using only data before it. Fit every transformer inside the CV fold using a pipeline object so it is impossible to do otherwise. And write the prediction-time question into the feature review, so it gets asked of every new feature rather than remembered.

Scored on

Whether suspicion is your first reaction. Enthusiasm here is the failing answer, and the interviewer is watching for exactly that.

B3.2Validation · the design question

"You have a table of customer-months and the model will run monthly to score every active customer. How do you split for validation?"

Reveal
Weak

"Stratified k-fold, random." Two problems at once: it trains on the future, and the same customer appears on both sides so the model memorises individuals. Both inflate the score, and neither is visible in the number.

Good

"Split by time, because deployment predicts forward — train on months up to T, test on T+1, rolling forward. And I'd check whether the same customer appearing in both training and test is a problem, which it usually is."

Senior

"It needs both, and that is the point of the question:

Time split because the model runs forward — a random split lets it learn from months that hadn't happened yet, which is not a situation that will ever occur again.

Grouping by customer because otherwise the same person's January is in training and their March is in test. The model then learns that customer rather than the pattern, and it will look much better than it performs on customers it has never seen.

"So: train on customers' history up to a cut-off, test on a later period — and if I also care about performance on brand-new customers, hold out a set of customers entirely, because those are two different questions with two different answers."

And I'd match the split to the actual deployment: if it scores every active customer monthly, the backtest should be a rolling origin — score month T+1, then T+2 — because one split tells you about one moment and the business runs every month.

Follow-up

"What if you also want to know how it does in a new country you're launching in?" → hold out that dimension too: train on existing countries, test on a held-out one. A random split massively overstates generalisation to a genuinely new population, and this is the same reasoning applied to a third axis.

Scored on

Whether you spot that it needs both. Naming one is the common answer; naming both, and tying it to how the model will actually be used, is the distinguishing one.

Honest gap

If you have only done random splits: "I've mostly worked with random and stratified splits on i.i.d. data. I know the rule is that the split should resemble deployment, so here I'd want time plus grouping — but I haven't built a rolling-origin backtest in production."

Round 04

Metrics

B4.1Metrics · the arithmetic one

"Your fraud model is 99% accurate. Is that good?"

Reveal
Weak

"Yes, that's very good." If 1% of transactions are fraudulent, a model that predicts "never fraud" is 99% accurate and catches nothing. This is the accuracy paradox and the question exists to test it.

Good

"It depends entirely on the base rate, and on imbalanced data accuracy is close to meaningless. If fraud is 1% of transactions, predicting 'never' scores 99%."

"I'd want precision and recall on the fraud class, and PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC — ROC-AUC looks optimistic on heavy imbalance because the huge negative class keeps the false-positive rate small."

Senior

Plus the arithmetic and the money:

"And I'd want the confusion matrix, because the counts tell the story that rates hide. Say 1% base rate, 90% sensitivity, 90% specificity, on 100,000 transactions: we'd catch 900 of 1,000 frauds and also flag 9,900 legitimate ones. Precision is 900 out of 10,800 — about 8%. So more than nine in ten alerts are false, and that is structural when the event is rare, not a fixable model flaw."

"Which means the real question is the operating point. How many reviews can the team do a day? What does a missed fraud cost versus a wasted review? That ratio sets the threshold, and then I'd report it in money: at this threshold we flag N a day, catch €X, waste Y reviews."

"And I'd check calibration if the score feeds an automated decision — if 0.7 doesn't mean 70%, any expected-value rule built on it is wrong even when the ranking is fine."

Follow-up

"How would you improve precision without losing recall?" → better features rather than a different threshold, since the threshold only trades one for the other. Then: a second-stage model on the flagged set, or routing by confidence — auto-approve the clearly-fine, auto-block the clearly-bad, and send the middle band to humans, which is where the review capacity should go.

Scored on

Whether you can do the base-rate arithmetic out loud. Being able to produce the precision figure from the confusion counts, quickly, is a strong signal.

Round 05

Experimentation

B5.1Experiments · the process question

"We ran an A/B test for three days, saw p = 0.04, and shipped. What's wrong with that?"

Reveal
Weak

"Three days isn't long enough." True, incomplete, and it misses the more serious problem — which is about when they looked, not only how long.

Good

Several things:

  • Peeking. If they checked repeatedly and stopped when p crossed 0.05, the real false-positive rate is far above 5% — with enough looks a null test crosses the threshold almost surely at some point.
  • No full business cycle. Three days misses the weekday/weekend pattern. A week minimum.
  • Novelty effect. Returning users react to change itself; early days can reverse.
  • Was the sample size computed in advance? If not, the stopping rule was the p-value, which is the peeking problem restated.
Senior

Plus what should have happened and what to check now:

"The root cause is that the design wasn't fixed in advance. Sample size from the minimum effect worth detecting, the baseline rate, α and power; duration at least one full cycle; one primary metric; guardrails; and a stopping rule. All decided before launch. Then p = 0.04 at the pre-planned endpoint means something."

"Before I'd trust anything, I'd check for sample ratio mismatch. If we expected 50/50 and got 52/48, assignment is broken and the result is void regardless of the p-value. It is the single best health check on an experiment and it takes a minute."

"And I'd ask what the effect size was, not just the p-value. Significant and tiny is common and usually not worth shipping — especially against a guardrail that moved."

"If they genuinely need to monitor continuously, that is fine — but it needs sequential testing or always-valid confidence sequences, not repeated fixed-horizon tests."

Follow-up

"What if you can't randomise?" → say so plainly and use a quasi-experimental design with its assumption stated: difference-in-differences (needs parallel pre-trends — check them), regression discontinuity (needs a genuine arbitrary cut-off), or a synthetic control. What you must not do is a before/after comparison presented as causal — that attributes everything else that happened in the period, including seasonality, to your change.

Scored on

Peeking and SRM. Naming SRM unprompted is a strong marker of someone who has actually run experiments rather than read about them.

Round 06 · where candidates are thinnest

Production & MLOps

B6.1Production · the real failure

"Your model scored 0.86 offline. In production it behaves much worse. Why?"

Reveal
Weak

"The data must have drifted." Possible, and unlikely to be the answer on day one of deployment — drift takes time. Reaching for it immediately usually means you have not deployed a model.

Good

Ranked by likelihood:

  1. Leakage — the offline score was never real. Check first.
  2. Training/serving skew — features computed differently in the two paths.
  3. The validation split didn't match deployment — random split on temporal data, or the same entity on both sides.
  4. Population shift — production traffic differs from the training sample.
  5. Drift — real, but usually later.
Senior

Same list, plus the diagnostic that settles it:

"The fastest way to tell these apart is to score the same rows through both paths and diff the feature vectors. If the features differ, it is skew and I have my answer in an hour. If they are identical and the predictions are still worse, it is the split design or leakage — and both of those mean the offline number was never real."

"Skew is the one I'd bet on, and it is structural. It happens because the notebook computes features one way and the serving code re-implements them. The fix is not care, it is architecture: share the transformation code, serialise the whole pipeline as one artefact rather than the model alone, or use a feature store so both read one definition."

"And it's why I'd want to log the feature vector with every prediction. Without it you cannot debug a bad prediction, detect drift, or reproduce anything — and this exact investigation becomes guesswork."

Follow-up

"How would you have caught it before users did?" → shadow deployment: run it alongside the current approach without acting on the output, and compare. Plus monitoring input distributions from day one — those drift visibly and immediately, whereas performance monitoring needs labels that may be months away.

Scored on

Whether skew is high on your list and whether you can name the structural fix. Most candidates have never deployed a model and it shows in this round.

Honest gap

"I haven't run a model in production at scale — my deployments have been batch scoring on a schedule. So I know skew and drift as concepts and the monitoring I'd want, but I haven't debugged a live degradation." Clean, and it still shows you know where the failures are.

Round 07

SQL & coding

Usually a screen rather than a deep round. The failure modes are predictable.

What they askWhat they are watching for
Aggregation with a joinFan-out. Joining one-to-many and summing a money column double-counts. Say "join, then count, then sum" out loud — checking row counts before and after is the habit they want to see.
Window functionsRunning totals, rank within group, previous value. ROW_NUMBER vs RANK vs DENSE_RANK — know the difference in ties.
Cohort or retention queryWhether you compare cohorts at equal age rather than equal calendar date. The newest cohort always looks worst otherwise.
"Find the duplicates"Group by the assumed key, count, having > 1. Also whether you ask what the grain is supposed to be.
A pandas transformationCorrectness over cleverness. Chained indexing warnings, silent type coercion, and whether you check the shape after a merge.
"Optimise this query"Read the plan before guessing. Look at estimated vs actual rows first — a large divergence means the planner's input was wrong, not the query.
The habit that scores in any coding round

Narrate intent, not keystrokes. "I'm going to aggregate the line items to order grain first, so the join can't fan out" tells them how you think. Silence is scored as being lost, even when you are solving it perfectly — the interviewer's only instrument is what you say.

Round 08

Behavioural & the offer

QuestionWhat a strong answer contains
"Tell me about a model that didn't work."A real one with the mechanism and what you changed systemically. Never "nothing failed" — that is heard as "I shipped nothing" or "I wasn't watching".
"A stakeholder wants to act on a correlation."State it as a consequence, not a correction. "That's a predictor rather than a driver — if we want to know whether acting on it works, the cheapest test is a holdout, and it's three weeks."
"How do you explain a model to non-technical people?"Consequence first, mechanism on request, and the ask at the end. Lead with what it means for their decision, not with the method.
"You have two weeks and messy data. What do you do?"Baseline first, profile the data, write the spec, and be prepared to report that the data cannot answer the question. That last option is the one that shows judgement.
"When have you recommended not building a model?"An increasingly common question. Have a real answer — a rule that sufficed, data that didn't exist, or a decision nobody was going to make.

The questions to ask them

  1. "What happens to a model after it's built here?" Reveals whether deployment and monitoring exist, or whether models go into decks. The single most predictive question about what the job is actually like.
  2. "How do you decide what to work on?" Whether there is a prioritisation process or a queue of requests.
  3. "What's the data infrastructure like — is there a warehouse, a feature store?" Tells you how much of your first year is plumbing.
  4. "Who do data scientists work with day to day?" Embedded in product teams, or a central group taking tickets. Very different jobs.
On the salary question

Ask for their range first — normal, expected, and in many jurisdictions required. If pushed to go first, give a researched range anchored to the market rather than to your current salary, which is not relevant information and which you are not obliged to volunteer.

Tactics under pressure

The seven sentences
1  UNCLEAR QUESTION
   "Let me check I've got this — you're asking about X, in the case
    where Y?"

2  NEED THINKING TIME
   "Give me twenty seconds to think about that."

3  GENUINELY STUCK
   "I'm stuck on X. I've considered A, which fails because…, and B,
    which works but assumes Y. Which would you rather I take?"

4  WRONG MID-SENTENCE
   "Wait — that's not right. If the split is random the same customer
    is on both sides, so that number is inflated."
   ← catching your own error scores HIGHER than never making it

5  DON'T KNOW
   "I don't know. I've used X but never gone below the API. If I had
    to reason about it I'd expect Y because… — but that's inference.
    I'd check before relying on it."

6  PUSHED ON A WEAK CLAIM
   "I should be precise. What I've done is A. What I haven't done is
    B, and I'd be learning there."

7  A NUMBER YOU CAN'T DEFEND
   "I'd want to check how that was measured before I stand behind it."
   ← always available, always safe, and better than a guess

The schedule

DayDo this
1The six framing questions out loud until automatic, then run B1.1 against a timer. This round carries the most weight.
2Statistics: the p-value definition word-perfect, confidence intervals stated correctly, regression to the mean, multiple comparisons.
3Leakage and split design. Write out the six leakage types and the four split types from memory.
4Metrics: work the base-rate confusion matrix by hand three times until the arithmetic is fast.
5Experiments and production. Rehearse SRM and training/serving skew — both are markers of real experience.
6SQL: one fan-out question, one window function, one cohort query. Then the seven sentences.
7Nothing technical. Re-read one project of your own and prepare the honest version of what you would do differently.
The day before
  • You can run the six framing questions without notes.
  • You can define a p-value and a confidence interval precisely, and say what each is not.
  • Your reflex on a great score is suspicion, not celebration.
  • You can name the six leakage types and the prediction-time question.
  • You can design a split from how the model will be deployed, including the both-axes case.
  • You can produce precision from a confusion matrix out loud.
  • You can name SRM, peeking, and regression to the mean.
  • You have something honest to say about production, even if it is a bounded claim.
  • You say "predictor, not driver" without being asked.
  • You have four questions for them, starting with what happens to a model after it is built.
