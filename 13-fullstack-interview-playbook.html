Full-stack interview preparation · built from your CV

They will not stop at the first answer. They descend until they find your floor.

A technical interview is not a quiz. It is a search — the interviewer asks, you answer, and then they follow up on your own answer, three or four levels down, until you say something you cannot support. That depth is your score. This playbook is built to raise the level at which you run out, and to make running out survivable.

Most interview preparation is a pile of questions and answers. That is the part you already have: this repository holds 82,348 lines of it across interview/, docs/interviews/ and docs/interview-prep/, and roughly 608 questions. Knowledge is not your constraint.

Two things are. The first is that a memorised answer collapses at the second follow-up, because the follow-up is generated from what you just said and cannot be pre-read. The only defence is having a method underneath the answer — a way of thinking that produces the next sentence even when you have never seen the question. That is Part 1, and it is the largest part of this document because it is the part nothing else here teaches.

The second is that your CV makes about forty distinct technical promises, and an interviewer picks which to call in. Some of those promises you can defend to the floor of the stack. Some you cannot, and you need to know which — in advance, calmly, with a sentence ready. That is Part 2.

How to use this

Read Part 1 first and slowly, even though it contains no framework trivia. Each of the seventeen sections is a procedure you can run out loud in a room. If you only get through Part 1, you will interview noticeably better.

Then do Part 2 with your CV open. Rate every claim honestly. The output is a short list of things to study and a shorter list of things to stop volunteering.

Then drill Part 3. Every question is hidden behind a reveal — answer out loud first, then compare against three tiers. The gap between your answer and the senior tier is your study list. Reading the answer first teaches you nothing; you will believe you knew it.

On versions, because it matters in this stack. Everything here is written against Next.js 16, NestJS 11, Node 22, PostgreSQL 16, Prisma 7, React 19 — the versions in your own projects. Next.js in particular has changed caching defaults between major versions, so where a default is version-dependent this document names the version rather than stating it flatly. Do the same in an interview: "as of 15 the default changed to…" is a senior sentence, and confidently reciting a default from a two-year-old blog post is a specific way to lose a Next.js interview. Before any interview, check the versions in their stack and say so out loud.

Before anything else

What they are actually scoring

Not "did they know it". Five dimensions, and the one that decides your level is never the one candidates prepare for.

DimensionWhat a high score looks likeHow they test it
Depth of knowledge You can go one level below the API you use. Not "I use Prisma" but "Prisma generates this SQL, and here is why that produced N+1". The follow-up ladder. They keep asking "why" until you stop. The level at which you stop is the score.
Reasoning under uncertainty Given a problem you have never seen, you produce a structured approach out loud instead of a guess or a silence. A deliberately underspecified problem. They are watching whether you ask questions before solving.
Judgement and trade-offs You choose, name what you gave up, and say what would change your mind. This is the single strongest senior signal. "Why did you use X instead of Y?" and "when would that be the wrong choice?"
Ownership You talk about what happened after you shipped — the bug, the rollback, the monitoring, the thing you would do differently. "What broke?" "How did you know it worked?" "What would you change now?"
Communication They can follow you without effort. You define a term before using it. You say "I don't know" cleanly and it costs nothing. The whole interview. It is scored continuously and mentioned last.

The follow-up ladder, and why it exists

Understand this mechanism and half of interview anxiety dissolves. An interviewer is not trying to find out whether you know one fact. They are trying to locate the boundary of your understanding, and the cheapest way to do that is to take your own answer and ask about it. Every answer you give becomes the next question.

It is the *consequence* of the answer given at L2. You cannot pre-read it, because it is generated from your own words. The only thing that gets you to L4 is understanding the mechanism, not the API. Notice also: reaching L3 and saying "I have not had to solve that — my instinct would be a short token lifetime plus a revocation list, but I would want to check the cost" scores BETTER than a confident wrong L3. The ladder is measuring your floor, and an honest floor is a floor. A bluffed floor is a collapse.
The single most useful reframe

You are not trying to answer every question. You are trying to make the level at which you run out be high, and to run out gracefully. Those are two different skills, and the second one is entirely learnable in an afternoon — it is the HONEST GAP row that appears throughout Part 3.

Competency 01 · runs in every round

Clarify before solving

The interviewer's problem statement is deliberately incomplete. Starting to solve it is the most common way strong candidates get marked down in the first two minutes.

When someone says "design an endpoint that returns a team's leave requests", they have left out: how many teams, how many requests, who is allowed to see it, whether it needs to be real-time, what happens to deleted users, and whether "team" means direct reports or the whole subtree. Every one of those changes the answer. If you start typing, you have chosen answers to all six silently, and the interviewer now knows you would do the same on their codebase.

The procedure

  1. Restate the problem in your own words, in one sentence. "So — an authenticated manager needs to see leave requests for people in their team, and I should assume this is a web UI." This costs eight seconds and catches misunderstandings before they cost twenty minutes. It also visibly buys you thinking time, legitimately.
  2. Ask about scale. How many rows, how many users, how often. You are not being pedantic — the answer decides whether this is a simple query or a paginated, indexed, cached one. "Is this ten teams or ten thousand?" is a question a senior asks and a junior does not.
  3. Ask who the actors are and what they may see. This is where authorisation lives, and forgetting it is the single most common design omission. "Can a manager see a peer's team? Can HR see everyone? Is there an audit requirement?"
  4. Ask about the edges you can already see. Deleted users, users who changed team mid-request, requests spanning a year boundary. Naming two or three specific edge cases early is a strong signal — it shows you are modelling reality rather than the happy path.
  5. State your assumptions out loud and write them down. "I'm going to assume single region, read-heavy, and that eventual consistency is fine for the list view. Tell me if any of those are wrong." Now if an assumption was wrong, it is a correction rather than a failure — and you invited it.
  6. Confirm what "done" means before starting. "So a working endpoint with the right shape, and we'll talk about scaling after — or do you want the scaling thinking up front?" This is the question that stops you optimising for forty minutes while they wanted working code.
The time budget

Two to four minutes of clarifying on a forty-five-minute problem. Not more — clarifying indefinitely is its own failure mode and reads as stalling. The tell that you are done: you can state the problem and its constraints in three sentences and nothing important is unspecified.

Trap · asking questions you do not use

The mistake: asking the six questions because a guide told you to, then designing as if the answers had been different.

Why it is deadly: interviewers notice immediately, and it converts a good signal into a bad one — now you look like someone performing seniority rather than having it. If you asked about scale and they said ten thousand teams, your design must show it.

The fix: after each answer, say what it changed. "Ten thousand teams — then I will not load the subtree in memory, I will do that with a recursive CTE and paginate." The answer must visibly move the design.

Drill 01Reasoning · clarify

"Build me a feature that lets an employee export their own data." Before designing anything: what do you ask, and what does each answer change?

Reveal — answer out loud first
Weak

Asking nothing and describing a CSV download endpoint. It is probably not wrong, and it demonstrates none of the five scored dimensions. You have also walked past the fact that "export their own data" in an HR system is very likely a GDPR subject access request, which is a legal obligation with a deadline and a defined scope — a completely different feature from a convenience download.

Good

Six questions, each with its consequence named:

  • Is this a convenience feature or a GDPR/subject-access obligation? → decides scope, retention, format and whether legal signs it off. This is the highest-value question in the set.
  • What counts as "their data"? → profile only, or leave, payroll, performance reviews, exit interview? Performance reviews contain other people's words about them, which is a genuinely hard call and not mine to make.
  • How large, and synchronous or not? → a 2KB profile is a request/response; five years across eight tables plus S3 documents is a background job with an email.
  • What format? → CSV for humans, JSON for portability. GDPR portability leans machine-readable.
  • Who else can trigger it? → self only, or HR on someone's behalf? The second needs authorisation and audit.
  • Is the export itself auditable? → in an HR system, almost certainly yes: actor, subject, timestamp, what was included.
Senior

Everything above, plus the three things that make it a real feature rather than an endpoint:

The download link is the security hole. A file containing one employee's complete HR record must not sit on a guessable URL. So: pre-signed S3 URL, short expiry, single use if possible, and the link goes to the verified account email rather than being returned in the response. I would raise this before writing any code, because retrofitting it is worse.

Size is unbounded, so the naive version is a time bomb. I would build it as a job from the start — enqueue, generate, notify — not because it is slow today but because the synchronous version fails silently for exactly the long-tenured employees who are most likely to ask. That is a place where doing it properly first is cheaper than doing it twice.

I would ask what happens on request two. If someone triggers it five times, do we generate five exports? Idempotency and rate limiting on a feature that reads everything about a person is not optional.

And I would flag one thing I cannot decide: whether reviewer comments in a performance review are the subject's data or the reviewer's. That is a legal and policy question, and the answer changes the schema I query. I would want it answered before building rather than guessing.

Follow-up

"You said background job. How does the user know it's ready?" → email with the pre-signed link, plus a status the UI can poll or a socket event. Then the real follow-up: "what if the job fails halfway?" → the job must be idempotent and restartable, the partial file must never be exposed, and a failure must surface to the user rather than silently disappearing — which is exactly the reliability thinking in competency 09.

Scored on

Whether you ask before building; whether you spotted the legal framing; and whether you noticed that the artefact this feature produces is a single file containing everything about one person. The security instinct is worth more here than the implementation.

Honest gap

If you have not shipped a subject-access flow: "I haven't built a formal SAR flow — the exports I've shipped were operational rather than legal. So I'd want the requirements from whoever owns data protection before designing, because the scope and the deadline are theirs to set, not mine." That answer is stronger than guessing at GDPR specifics, and it is exactly the judgement they want.

Competency 02 · live coding, take-home

Decomposition

How to start a problem you have never seen without freezing, and how to make progress visible while you do it.

The freeze happens because you are trying to find the good solution before writing anything. That is the wrong order and it is also unnecessary: interviewers are watching the path, and a working slow solution that you then improve out loud scores higher than a clever one that arrives silently.

The procedure

  1. Solve the smallest version by hand, on paper, with three inputs. Not code — an example. If the problem is "group leave requests into overlapping periods", take three requests and write the answer manually. You cannot code what you cannot do by hand, and doing it by hand reveals the algorithm most of the time.
  2. Say the naive approach out loud, and name its cost. "The obvious version is nested loops comparing every pair — that's O(n²), and for a team of thirty it is completely fine. Do you want me to write that first, or go straight to the sorted approach?" You have now demonstrated that you see the better solution without spending twenty minutes on it, and you have handed the interviewer a decision they usually enjoy making.
  3. Write the working version. Ugly is fine. Name things properly anyway — naming is free and it is scored. Do not handle edge cases yet; write a comment where each one goes: // TODO empty input, // TODO same-day requests. Visible TODOs prove you saw them.
  4. Run it in your head on your three hand-worked inputs. Out loud. This is the step candidates skip and it is where most bugs get caught. "Input one: two requests, no overlap — returns two groups. Correct."
  5. Then improve, in the order: correctness → clarity → speed. Announce which you are doing. "That's correct now; let me handle the empty case and the boundary, then I'll talk about making it O(n log n)."
  6. Stop and ask before a big refactor. "I could restructure this to be cleaner — is that worth the time, or would you rather see the edge cases handled?" Time is the scarce resource and they know it better than you do.

Fatal · coding in silence

The mistake: thinking hard, typing nothing, for two minutes. Or typing steadily and saying nothing for ten.

Why it is deadly: the interviewer's only instrument is what you say. Silence is not neutral — it is scored as "could not structure an approach", because they cannot distinguish deep thought from being lost. You can be solving it perfectly and still fail the round.

The fix: narrate at the level of intent, not keystrokes. Not "now I type a for loop" — that is noise. Say "I'm going to sort by start date, because then an overlap can only be with the previous element, which turns this into one pass." If you need genuine silence, ask for it: "give me twenty seconds to think this through" — that sentence buys you real quiet and costs nothing.

Competency 03 · coding and design

Cost reasoning

Big-O is the first thing taught and rarely the thing that matters. In a web application the cost is almost never CPU — and knowing that is a level marker.

Your 50-second page is the perfect illustration and you should use it. Nothing about that page was CPU-bound. The fix was not a better algorithm; it was fewer and cheaper trips to the database, less data crossing the wire, and an index so the database did less work per trip. Candidates who only reason in Big-O cannot explain why that page was slow.

The hierarchy of cost, in a typical web request

OperationRough orderWhat it means in practice
CPU work in memorynanosecondsLooping over a thousand objects is free. Stop optimising it.
Reading from local memory / cache hit~100 nsEffectively free.
SSD read~100 µsA thousand times slower than memory. This is why an index matters — it turns a scan of a million rows into a handful of page reads.
Database round trip, same datacentre~0.5–2 msThe number that dominates most web apps. 200 of these in a loop is 400 ms of pure waiting, and it is invisible in any CPU profile.
Cross-region network call~50–150 msOne of these blows a latency budget on its own. Two in sequence is a user-visible pause.
Cold serverless function start~100 ms–1 sRelevant to your Vercel and Neon setup, and a good thing to raise unprompted.
The sentence that demonstrates this

"O(n²) in memory over a few hundred items is fine — it's microseconds. O(n) where each n is a database round trip is the thing that kills you, because that's the N+1, and it's linear in round trips rather than linear in CPU. So when I look at a slow endpoint I count queries before I count loops."

That is roughly the whole of this competency, and it will move you up a level in most conversations because it demonstrates that you have debugged something real rather than only studied.

The four questions to ask about any slow thing

  1. How many round trips? Queries per request, HTTP calls per render, sequential awaits that could be parallel. Count them; do not estimate.
  2. How much data is crossing the wire? Over-fetched columns, missing pagination, a JSON payload with an embedded base64 file. Your CV explicitly says you "trimmed over-fetched columns" — that is this question, and it is worth saying that way.
  3. How much work does each trip cause? A sequential scan versus an index seek. Is there an index, does it match the predicate, and is the column order right for it.
  4. Is it doing the work at the wrong time? Computed per request when it could be cached, computed on read when it could be computed on write, done synchronously when the user does not need to wait. Moving work in time is often cheaper than making it faster.

Drill 02Reasoning · cost

An endpoint returns 200 employees with their manager's name and their department name. It takes 1.4 seconds. Nothing is CPU-bound. Give me every plausible cause, ranked, and how you would confirm each in under a minute.

Reveal — answer out loud first
Weak

"Probably needs an index." It might, but this is a guess dressed as an answer, and it skips the step that separates engineers from people who have read about performance: finding out. It also misses the far more likely cause.

Good

Ranked by how often it is actually the cause:

  1. N+1 queries. One query for the employees, then one per employee for the manager and one for the department — 401 round trips. At 3 ms each that is 1.2 s, which matches the symptom almost exactly. Confirm: turn on query logging or Prisma's log: ['query'] and count the lines for one request. If there are ~400, you are done in thirty seconds.
  2. Missing index on the join columns. If it is genuinely one query, check whether manager_id and department_id are indexed. Confirm: EXPLAIN ANALYZE and look for a sequential scan on the joined table.
  3. Over-fetching. SELECT * pulling a large text or JSON column nobody displays. Confirm: look at the response size and the selected columns.
  4. Sequential awaits. Two or three independent async calls awaited in series instead of with Promise.all. Confirm: read the handler.
  5. No pagination. 200 today, 5,000 next year — not the current cause but the same bug waiting.
Senior

Same list, plus the discipline around it:

I would not guess at all — I would look at the query count first, because it is the cheapest measurement available and it discriminates between cause 1 and everything else immediately. One log line versus four hundred is not a subtle signal. Ordering your diagnostics by cost-to-check rather than by likelihood is the actual skill.

The fix for the N+1 has three options and they are not equivalent. An ORM include — one query with joins, but it multiplies rows and can over-fetch on a one-to-many. Two queries and stitch in application code — often the fastest, and better when the child set is large. Or a dataloader-style batch. For 200 employees joining to a small department table I would use the join; for 200 employees each with 50 leave requests I would use two queries, because the join returns 10,000 rows to build 200 objects.

And I would check the p95, not this one measurement. 1.4 s might be the median or might be the one slow request; if there is a cold connection or a pool limit involved, the distribution matters more than the number. That is competency 07.

Follow-up

"You said the join multiplies rows. Show me." → 200 employees × 50 leave requests = 10,000 rows over the wire, each repeating all the employee columns, to construct 200 objects. That is the classic case where include is slower than two queries despite being one round trip — the round trip is cheap and the payload is not.

Scored on

Whether you measure before guessing; whether N+1 is the first thing you reach for (it should be); and whether you know that the fix has trade-offs rather than being a single correct answer.

Honest gap

None needed — this is your strongest territory. Your CV says you diagnosed a 50-second page down to 6 seconds by tracing the query, adding indexes, removing redundant joins and trimming columns. That is four of the five causes above, found in production. Say so.

Competency 04 · the system-design round

The architecture method

Eight steps, in this order, every time. The order is the content — most candidates fail this round by drawing boxes before knowing what the system must do.

A system-design round is forty-five minutes and one sentence of prompt: "design an HR analytics platform". Candidates who have no method draw a browser, an API and a database in the first two minutes and then have forty-three minutes of nothing. Candidates with a method spend forty-five minutes visibly making decisions.

The procedure

  1. Functional requirements — what must it do? Get to a numbered list of three to five things, agreed out loud, and explicitly park the rest. "So: HR uploads employee data, the system scores attrition risk, managers see their team's risk, and someone can generate a report. I'm going to leave recruitment and payroll out — tell me if that's wrong." Parking scope is a senior move; trying to design everything is how you run out of time.
  2. Non-functional requirements — what must be true of it? Latency, scale, freshness, availability, compliance. Ask, do not assume: "how many employees — a thousand or a million? Does the risk score need to be live, or is nightly fine?" Nightly versus live is the single biggest architectural fork in most data products, and asking it early saves you from designing the wrong system.
  3. Estimate the scale, roughly, out loud. Not for precision — to size the problem. "10,000 employees, one scoring run a night, 40 fields each. That's 400,000 values a night. That is nothing; it fits in memory on a laptop. So this is not a big-data problem, it is a correctness and access-control problem." That conclusion changes everything downstream and it takes twenty seconds of arithmetic. Getting this wrong in the other direction — designing Kafka for 10,000 rows — is a classic over-engineering flag.
  4. The data model, before any boxes. Entities, relationships, and the grain of the central table. This is where the real thinking is, and doing it fourth rather than last is what separates the rounds that go well. For HR: is the fact table one row per employee, or one row per employee per month? The second lets you answer "what was headcount last March" and the first does not. Competency 08 is entirely this.
  5. Now the components, and the data flow between them. Draw it. Say what each box owns and — more importantly — what it does not own. "The Nest API owns authorisation and the employee data; the Python service owns scoring and knows nothing about who is asking."
  6. The API contract for the one or two most important operations. Method, path, request, response, error cases. Concrete beats abstract: writing out one endpoint's actual shape demonstrates more than describing five.
  7. Failure modes — go through each arrow and ask what happens when it fails. This step is where most candidates simply stop, and it is the highest-scoring part of the round. "If the scoring service is down when the nightly job runs, do we retry, skip, or serve yesterday's score with a staleness flag? I'd serve yesterday's with a flag, because a missing risk score breaks the manager's page and a slightly stale one does not."
  8. What breaks at 10×, and what you would do about it — but not yet. Name the first bottleneck and the change you would make, then say you would not make it now. "At 100,000 employees the nightly scoring stops fitting in one run, and I'd partition by department and parallelise. I wouldn't build that now — it's a day's work later and it costs nothing to defer." Knowing the limit and choosing not to pre-build it is exactly the judgement they are scoring.

Trap · designing for a scale nobody asked for

The mistake: reaching for Kafka, sharding, microservices and a read replica for a system serving a thousand employees.

Why it is deadly: it is read as inexperience, not ambition. Anyone who has operated infrastructure knows what each of those costs to run, and proposing them unprompted says you have only read about them. It is one of the most reliable ways to be marked down as "would over-engineer our codebase".

The fix: step 3 exists to prevent this. Do the arithmetic out loud, reach the conclusion "this is small", and then say the sentence that gets you the credit anyway: "a thousand employees is a Postgres table and a nightly cron. Trade-off articulation

Senior

Everything above, and then the part that actually answers the question:

Competency 06 · the debugging round, and your best story

Debugging by hypothesis

The difference between an engineer and someone who fixes things by changing them until they work. Six steps, and the sixth is the one nobody does.

This competency matters more for you than for most candidates, because your single strongest résumé line is a debugging story — the 50-second page. An interviewer will ask you to walk through it, and the temptation is to describe the fix. The fix is the least interesting part. They are asking how you found it, because that is the transferable skill.

The procedure

  1. Reproduce it, reliably. If you cannot make it happen on demand, you cannot know when you have fixed it. Note exactly what reproduces it: which user, which team size, which date range. Often the reproduction conditions are the diagnosis — "it's only slow for managers with more than fifty reports" tells you it is data-volume-dependent before you have looked at anything.
  2. Measure, and measure the right layer. Do not guess where the time goes; find out. Is it the browser, the network, the API handler, or the database? One measurement at each boundary localises the problem in minutes. The commonest waste of a day is optimising the layer the time is not in.
  3. Form a hypothesis that is falsifiable. Not "the query is slow" — that is a restatement. "The query does a sequential scan on leave_request because employee_id has no index" is a hypothesis, and EXPLAIN either confirms it or kills it in one command.
  4. Bisect. Halve the search space with each test. Remove joins one at a time. Run the query alone in a client to take the application out. Comment out half the page. If it started recently, bisect the commits. Bisection is how you find things in logarithmic time instead of linear guessing.
  5. Fix the cause, and know whether you did. An index is a cause fix. A cache in front of the slow query is a symptom fix — sometimes correct, but you must know which one you are choosing and say so.
  6. Verify with the same measurement you started with, and say the number. This is the step candidates skip and interviewers listen for. "It was 50 seconds; after the index and the join removal it was 6." You have a before, an after, and the same instrument for both. Without this step you do not know whether you fixed it or whether the cache was warm.
Your 50s → 6s story, told as method rather than outcome

   removed some joins and got it to 6."

  REPRODUCE   "First I found it was not slow for everyone. It was slow

Competency 07 · deep dive, ownership

Performance thinking

Three ideas: measure before you change anything, the average lies, and know the difference between premature optimisation and negligence.

The average lies, and this is how to say so

If a page's mean load time is 900 ms, that number tells you almost nothing. It could be everyone experiencing 900 ms, or 90% of users at 200 ms and 10% at 7 seconds. Those are different problems with different fixes, and only one of them is losing you users.

MeanAlmost nothing on its own; one 30-second outlier drags it. Sensitive to exactly the values you care about.Capacity planning, cost. Rarely for user experience.

The sentence that scores

"I'd want the p95 rather than the average, and I'd want it segmented by team size — because in an HR tool the slow requests aren't random, they're the biggest teams, and those are exactly the users whose opinion matters most. An average hides that completely."

Note what that does: it takes a generic statistics point and makes it domain-specific. That combination is what "senior" sounds like.

Premature optimisation versus negligence

The quotation is over-used and has made a generation of engineers afraid to think about performance at all. The useful distinction:

Premature — do not do this

Optimising a code path before you know it is hot. Caching before measuring. Denormalising a schema on a hunch. Reaching for a queue when the operation takes 40 ms. Each adds permanent complexity to buy speed you cannot demonstrate you need.

Negligence — this is not "premature"

Writing an N+1 into a list endpoint. Shipping a query with no index on the predicate. No pagination on a collection that grows without bound. Loading an entire table into memory. These are not optimisations you deferred — they are known defects, and calling their prevention "premature optimisation" is a misuse of the phrase.

The dividing line is whether the cost grows with data. If it does, handle it now, because it will fail in production on the largest account and be expensive to retrofit. If it does not, leave it alone until measured. That one rule resolves most of these arguments, and stating it is a strong answer to "how do you decide when to optimise?"

Competency 08 · design and SQL rounds

Data modelling

The model decides which questions are answerable. Get it wrong and no amount of application code recovers — the answer is simply not in the database.

This is the highest-leverage thinking in any data-backed system and it is under-practised because it feels like it belongs to a database course. It does not: it is a product decision wearing a schema.

The procedure

  1. Write down the questions the system must answer, before the tables. Actual sentences. "Which of my reports have leave pending?" "What was headcount on 1 March?" "Who approved this request and when?" The schema exists to serve this list.
  2. For each question, ask whether the data to answer it is being recorded. This is the step that catches the fatal errors. "What was headcount last March" is unanswerable if you store a current employee row and overwrite it — no query recovers a value you never wrote down. Storing tenure_months instead of hire_date is the same class of mistake: it is a derived value that silently destroys the ability to ask time-based questions.
  3. Decide the grain of every fact table explicitly, and say it out loud. "One row per leave request" or "one row per employee per month". The grain determines what you can aggregate and what you can compare over time; getting it wrong is not a slow query, it is an impossible one.
  4. Model the relationships honestly, including the ones people wish were simpler. Can an employee have two managers? Can a team have no manager? Does a department have history? Systems break at exactly the relationships someone assumed were one-to-one.
  5. Then think about access patterns and indexes. Not before — the model comes first, and access patterns tell you what to index, not what to store.
  6. Ask what happens to history. When someone changes department, do we need to know they used to be elsewhere? If yes, you need a temporal design — validity dates or a snapshot table — and deciding that after launch is a migration nobody enjoys.

The same shape, plus four things that come from having been burned:

Store the actor's role at the time of the action, not a foreign key to their current role. Competency 09 · design and ownership rounds

Failure and reliability

One question, asked of every arrow in your diagram and every external call in your code: what happens when this fails? Most candidates never ask it once.

This is the cheapest way to sound senior in a design round, because the bar is low — the majority of candidates design only the happy path. Every network call, every third-party API, every queue and every database write can fail, be slow, or succeed in a way you never hear about.

The four failure modes of any remote call

ModeWhat happensWhat you must do about it
Fast failureConnection refused, 500. You find out immediately.The easy case. Decide: retry, fail the request, or degrade.
Slow successIt works, in 30 seconds. Your user is gone and your thread was held.Always set a timeout. A call with no timeout is a call that can hang forever, and this is the most common omission in real code.
Ambiguous failureYou time out, but the other side did process it. You do not know.Idempotency. If the caller retries, the operation must be safe to repeat — which means an idempotency key, not hope.

Idempotency, in the form that actually gets asked

The question is usually concrete: "your report-generation endpoint is called twice because the user double-clicked. What happens?"

The answer has a shape. Make the operation identifiable — the client sends a key, or you derive one from the meaningful inputs (user, report type, parameters, period). Record it before doing the work, with a unique constraint, so the second insert fails rather than starting a second job. Return the same result for the same key — the second call gets the first call's job ID, not an error and not a duplicate. The unique constraint is doing the real work here: enforcing it in application code by checking first is a race, and saying that unprompted is the senior half of the answer.

Retries, and the three ways they make things worse

  1. Retrying a non-idempotent operation. You have now charged the card twice. Retries are only safe on operations you have made repeatable — decide which those are before adding retry logic, not after.
  2. Retrying immediately, in a tight loop. The downstream service is struggling and you have just tripled its traffic. Use exponential backoff with jitter — the jitter matters, because without it every client retries in lockstep and you have built a synchronised thundering herd.
  3. Retrying forever. A permanently poisoned message retried infinitely blocks the queue behind it. You need a maximum attempt count and a dead-letter queue — and something that actually looks at the DLQ, because an unmonitored DLQ is just a slower way to lose data.
Trap · "and then it retries" as a complete answer

The mistake: answering every failure question with "it retries".

Why it is deadly: it is the reflex answer, and the follow-up dismantles it in one move — "retry how many times? with what delay? and what if it's still failing? and is the operation safe to repeat?" If "it retries" was the whole thought, you now have three questions and no answers.

The fix: say the whole policy in one breath. "Three attempts, exponential backoff with jitter, then dead-letter. It's safe to retry because the job is keyed on report ID and writes to a deterministic S3 path, so a repeat overwrites rather than duplicating. And the DLQ has an alert on it — otherwise it's just a place failures go to be forgotten." That is four sentences and it closes every follow-up at once.

Everything above, plus the three things that make this a real answer:

The progress state must live in the database, not only in the socket stream. This is the structural flaw in the naive design. If progress exists only as emitted events, then a user who refreshes the page has no idea what is happening, and a crash leaves no trace of how far it got. So: a report_job row with status and current_stage, updated as each stage completes, and the socket becomes a notification that the row changed rather than the source of truth. On reconnect or refresh, the client reads the row. That one change fixes the refresh case, the crash case and the multi-tab case together.

Resumability has a cost and I would not always pay it. Resuming from stage four means every stage's output has to be durable and identifiable. For a report that takes two minutes, restarting from scratch is often the better trade — simpler, and two minutes is tolerable. I would make each stage idempotent so a restart is safe, and only add resumability if the pipeline got long enough that restarting hurt. Distinguishing "safe to retry" from "resumes where it left off" is the actual engineering judgement here.

And the failure the design would not survive: if the LLM summary stage is stage five and the provider is down, retries will not help — it is not transient. That stage needs to degrade rather than fail: deliver the report without the generated summary, flagged, rather than losing five stages of completed work to one optional stage. Distinguishing a stage that must succeed from one that may be skipped is something I would want declared in the pipeline definition rather than implied by the code.

Follow-up

"You said the socket is a notification, not the data. Why does that matter?" → because two sources of truth for the same state will disagree. If the socket carries the progress payload, then the row and the stream can diverge, and the client's view depends on when it connected. If the socket only says "job 41 changed", the client re-reads one authoritative row and every client converges — at the cost of an extra round trip, which for a progress update is nothing.

Scored on

Whether you thought about the user, not just the job. Half of candidates answer only the infrastructure half. And whether you noticed that socket-only progress is unrecoverable state — that observation is the one that marks this as a system you designed rather than assembled.

Honest gap

If your implementation does not in fact persist stage progress: say so, and say what you would change. "In my implementation the progress is emitted over the socket and not persisted, so a refresh loses it — that's a real weakness and the fix is the job row I described." Naming a flaw in your own project and its fix scores far better than describing a design you did not build. Interviewers can tell the difference, and they are specifically listening for it in a deep-dive round.

Competency 10 · design and code-review rounds

Security thinking

Not a checklist of vulnerabilities. Three questions asked of every endpoint, and the one distinction candidates get wrong.

You do not need to be a security engineer. You need to demonstrate that security is part of how you design rather than a review someone else does. Three questions, asked of every endpoint you write, get you most of the way:

  1. Who can call this? Authentication. Is there a guard? Is it on by default, or does each route opt in — because opt-in means the next developer forgets and ships an open endpoint.
  2. What can they see? Authorisation, and specifically row-level authorisation. A manager can call GET /employees/:id — but for which ids? This is where real breaches live, and it is the question most candidates skip entirely.
  3. What can they change, and is it recorded? Mutations need both authorisation and audit. In an HR system, "who changed this salary" is a question someone will eventually ask under pressure.
Fatal · confusing authentication with authorisation

The mistake: "the endpoint is protected — you need a valid token." Then the interviewer passes a valid token belonging to a different employee and reads someone else's record.

Why it is deadly: this is the single most common real-world API vulnerability (broken object-level authorisation), and getting it wrong in an interview for an HR product is especially bad, because the data is sensitive by definition. Authentication answers who are you. Authorisation answers may you do this, to this specific record. A guard that only checks the token has answered the first and skipped the second.

The fix: state both, always, and name where the second is enforced. Your CV says RBAC is enforced "at the query level" in HR Insight AI — that phrase is the correct answer to this question and you should lead with it. "The role check is a guard, but the row filter is in the query — a team manager's employee query is always scoped to their subtree, so there is no code path that returns a row they may not see. Doing it in the guard would mean every new endpoint has to remember; doing it in the data layer means it cannot be forgotten."

Competency 11 · code review, take-home

Testing judgement

Nobody is impressed by "I write tests". They are asking what you test, at which level, and — the real question — what you deliberately do not test.

You have an unusually strong claim here: Playwright and Cucumber suites in production, plus Jest and Supertest. Most mid-level candidates have unit tests and an aspiration. The way to convert that into a level signal is to talk about the boundary rather than the tools.

The procedure — deciding what to test

  1. Ask what breaking would cost. Blast radius decides coverage. A leave-approval flow that feeds payroll gets end-to-end coverage; a tooltip does not. Say this explicitly — it is the answer to "how much coverage should we have?", which has no numeric answer worth giving.
  2. Push tests to the cheapest level that can catch the bug. A validation rule belongs in a unit test, not a browser test. If a Playwright test is the only thing catching a date-parsing bug, that test is in the wrong place — it is slow, flaky and it tells you the page is broken rather than which function is wrong.
  3. Test at the seams, integration-style, for anything involving the database. Mocking the ORM to test a query tests your mock. Your CV pairs Jest with Supertest, which is exactly the right instinct: hit the real handler, with a real database, and assert the response.
  4. Reserve E2E for critical user journeys, and keep the count small. Five to fifteen journeys, not two hundred. E2E suites die of flake and slowness, and a suite people ignore is worse than no suite because it trains the team to ignore red.
  5. Say what you do not test. Getters, framework behaviour, third-party libraries, the exact wording of copy. And do not chase a coverage number — coverage measures lines executed, not assertions made, and it is trivially gamed.
The Cucumber question, and the trap in it

Your CV says Cucumber (BDD). The follow-up is almost always: "who actually reads the Gherkin? Do product people write those scenarios?"

The honest answer is usually no, and saying so is much better than pretending. "In practice the Gherkin was written and read by engineers, so the collaboration benefit BDD is designed for wasn't really there. What it did buy us was readable scenario names in the report when something failed, which made triage faster. If I were choosing again for an engineer-only audience I'd probably use Playwright's own test syntax and skip the Gherkin layer — the value of Cucumber is the shared language, and if nobody outside the team is reading it you're paying the indirection cost for nothing."

That answer demonstrates you understand why the tool exists rather than that you used it, which is the whole point of the question.

Trap · the flaky test answer

The mistake: "we had some flaky tests so we added retries" or "we skipped them".

Why it is deadly: both are the answers of someone who treats flake as weather rather than as a defect. Retrying a flaky test hides a real race condition — frequently a race that also exists in production.

The fix: name the causes and the fixes. Flake in E2E is nearly always one of four things: waiting on time instead of state (fix: assert on the element or the network response, never waitForTimeout), shared state between tests (fix: each test creates its own data, and tests do not depend on order), test-vs-app races — clicking before a handler is attached (fix: Playwright's auto-waiting locators, used properly), and real application races the test happens to expose (fix: the application, not the test). Then the sentence that lands: "a flaky test is either a bad test or a real bug, and it's worth finding out which before adding a retry."

Competency 12 · the code-review round

Code-quality judgement

SOLID recited is a red flag. SOLID applied, without naming it, is a level signal. And the most valuable judgement is knowing when not to abstract.

In a code-review round they hand you a file and watch what you notice, in what order. Strong candidates comment on structure and risk; weaker ones comment on formatting, which a linter already handles.

What to look for, in priority order

  1. Correctness and edge cases. Off-by-one, null handling, the empty list, the error path that swallows the error. Always first.
  2. Security. Missing authorisation, unparameterised query, a secret, mass assignment. Second, because it is the most expensive to find later.
  3. Cost that scales with data. A query in a loop, a missing index, no pagination. See competency 07 — this is negligence rather than premature optimisation.
  4. Coupling and responsibility. Does this function do one thing? Does the controller contain business logic that belongs in a service? Does the service know about HTTP? In Nest specifically: a controller that queries Prisma directly is the structural smell to name.
  5. Naming and readability. Does the name say what it does, including the units and the truthiness — isActive not flag, timeoutMs not timeout.
  6. Tests. Is the risky path covered? Not "is coverage high".
  7. Style. Last, and mostly you should not mention it — say "the linter will get the formatting" and move on. Leading with style is the classic junior review.
The rule of three, and why premature abstraction is worse than duplication
  ONE occurrence      → write it inline.
  TWO occurrences     → duplicate it. Yes, duplicate it.
                        You do not yet know which parts vary.
  THREE occurrences   → now you can see the shape of the variation.
                        Extract it.

  WHY: an abstraction built from two examples encodes a guess about
  what varies. When the third case does not fit, you get a parameter.
  Then a flag. Then a flag that changes behaviour in two places. The
  abstraction is now harder to read than the duplication it replaced,
  AND it couples three call sites that have no real relationship.

  Duplication is cheap and local. A wrong abstraction is expensive
  and global. That asymmetry is the whole argument.

  THE SENTENCE:  "I'd leave this duplicated for now. There are two
  cases and they might diverge — if a third shows up I'll be able to
  see what actually varies and extract the right thing. Extracting
  from two examples usually means I'm guessing."
How to give review feedback — it is being scored too

The code-review round tests collaboration as much as knowledge, because they are predicting what you are like on their pull requests. Three habits: separate must-fix from suggestion ("this one's blocking — it's a missing authz check; the rest are preferences"); ask rather than assert when you might be missing context ("is there a reason this is in the controller? if it's temporary, fine"); and say what is good, briefly and specifically, because a review that is only criticism is a review people dread.

Competency 13 · behavioural, planning

Estimation

"How long would that take?" is a judgement question, not a scheduling question. The answer has a required shape.

Never a single number. A single number is a commitment you cannot support and it hides the thing they are actually assessing: whether you know what you do not know.

  1. Decompose out loud, roughly. "Schema and migration, the endpoint, the UI, the authz, tests, and the export path." Six pieces named is already a better answer than most.
  2. Give a range, not a point. "Three to seven days."
  3. Name what makes it the wide end. This is the part that matters. "The width is the document storage — if we already have the S3 upload path I can reuse it and it's three; if I have to build signed uploads and virus scanning it's seven."
  4. Say when you would know. "I'd know which after half a day looking at the existing upload code, and I'd come back and narrow it."
  5. State what you are excluding. "That's not including design review or the data migration for existing records, if there are any."
The two answers that lose points

"Two days" — a confident point estimate on an underspecified feature signals that you have not thought about the unknowns, or that you will discover them late. Either way it predicts missed dates.

"It's hard to say, it depends on a lot of things" — refusing to estimate reads as an inability to commit. Engineers who cannot estimate cannot be planned around, and that caps the scope they get trusted with.

The range plus the named unknown plus the date you would narrow it is the answer that is both honest and usable. It is also, not coincidentally, exactly how a manager wants to be told.

Competency 14 · behavioural, ownership

Production ownership

The questions after "did you ship it". This is where a 2-year engineer can sound like a 5-year one, because most candidates have never thought about it at all.

The questionWhat a strong answer contains
"How did you know it worked?"A measurement, not a feeling. The before number, the after number, the same instrument. Plus: what you watched for the first week.
"What would you alert on?"Symptoms users feel, not causes: error rate, p95 latency, queue depth and job failure rate, and one business metric (reports completed per hour). Not CPU — CPU is a diagnostic, not an alert, because high CPU with happy users is not an incident.
"What broke?"A real story with a real cause. Never "nothing broke" — that means either you shipped nothing or you were not watching. Interviewers hear it as the latter.
"How did you find out it broke?"The honest hierarchy: an alert fired (best), a colleague noticed, a user reported it (worst, and very common — say so if it is true). Then what you changed so the next one is caught earlier.
"What did you change afterwards?"Both the fix and the systemic change — a test, an alert, a check in CI. A fix with no systemic change means the same class of bug returns.
"Whose fault was it?"A trap, and the answer is about the system. "The deploy went out without the migration because nothing enforced the order — the fix was in CI, not in the person."
Trap · the mistake story where you were not at fault

The mistake: answering "tell me about a mistake" with a story where the real cause was someone else's code, unclear requirements, or bad luck.

Why it is deadly: the question is testing self-awareness and accountability directly. A story where you turn out to be blameless answers neither, and interviewers see the manoeuvre every single day.

The fix: pick something genuinely yours, with a real consequence, and spend most of the answer on what you changed about how you work. The shape: what I did, what it cost, how I found out, what I did in the moment, and the habit I have now that I did not have then. That last clause is the answer. If you are short of material, a performance regression you shipped, a migration you ran without a rollback plan, or a test you skipped because you were sure, are all real, all forgivable, and all demonstrate the point.

Disagreeing with someone senior

They will ask about this, because it predicts whether you will let a bad decision through. The pattern that works has four parts and takes fifteen seconds:

  1. State their position accurately first. "So the plan is to denormalise the department onto the employee row to avoid the join." If you cannot restate it, you are not ready to disagree with it.
  2. Name your concern as a consequence, not as an opinion. Not "I don't like that" — "my worry is that when someone moves department we'd have to update it in two places, and if one fails they're in two departments."
  3. Propose the cheapest way to find out who is right. "Could we check whether the join is actually costing us? If it's two milliseconds, we don't need to decide this."
  4. Commit either way, and say so out loud. "If you'd rather go ahead, I'm fine with it — I'd just want a constraint or a test that catches the divergence." Disagree and commit, stated explicitly, is what makes you safe to disagree with.

Competency 16 · panel, behavioural

Product sense

Whether you build what was asked or what was needed. Cheap to demonstrate and almost nobody does it.

An engineer who asks "why do they want this?" before "how do I build it?" is rarer than they should be, and every hiring manager notices. Three habits:

Ask what the user is trying to do

A request for "an export button on the employee list" might be someone reconciling headcount every month. The export is their workaround, not their goal — and knowing that might mean the right build is a headcount view, which is less work and solves it properly.

Offer the cheaper version

"The full version is about two weeks. There's a version that covers the common case in two days — it wouldn't handle multi-year ranges. Is that enough for now?" Almost always yes. This is the single most valuable sentence an engineer can say to a product manager.

Say what you would cut

When asked to design something, name the feature you would drop and why. It shows you think in trade-offs rather than scope lists, and it is the same muscle as competency 05.

Know who uses it and how often

"That report is generated by four HR people once a month" changes every technical decision — caching, performance budget, whether it needs to be a dashboard at all. Ask it every time.

A1TypeScript · generics

"Write a function that takes an array of objects and a key, and returns an array of that key's values. Type it properly."

Reveal — answer out loud first
Weak
function pluck(items: any[], key: string): any[] {
  return items.map(i => i[key]);
}

It works and it types nothing. Two anys means the caller gets no completion, no error on a misspelt key, and no knowledge of the return type. This is the answer that says you use TypeScript as a linter rather than a type system.

Good
function pluck<T, K extends keyof T>(items: T[], key: K): T[K][] {
  return items.map(i => i[key]);
}

// pluck(employees, 'name')      → string[]
// pluck(employees, 'nmae')      → compile error: not a keyof Employee
// pluck(employees, 'hireDate')  → Date[]

Two type parameters. T is inferred from the array. K extends keyof T is the constraint that makes the key checkable, and it is the whole point of the question. T[K] is an indexed access type — the type of that property — so the return type is exact rather than any.

Senior

Same signature, plus the reasoning behind each choice:

Why K extends keyof T rather than K = keyof T: the constraint is what enables inference to narrow to the literal type 'name' rather than widening to keyof T. Without extends you lose the exact return type — you would get a union of every property type instead of the one you asked for.

Why not one type parameter: writing pluck<T>(items: T[], key: keyof T) compiles and typechecks the key correctly, but the return type collapses to a union of all of T's value types. The second parameter exists purely to carry the specific key through to the return type.

What I would add in real code: nothing, usually. But if the array could be empty and callers were destructuring the result, I would consider whether they want T[K][] or a non-empty guarantee, which TypeScript cannot express without a branded tuple type — at which point I would handle it at the call site instead. Reaching for exotic types to encode a runtime invariant usually costs more than it saves.

Follow-up

"Now make it work for nested keys like 'department.name'." → this is a recursive conditional type with template literal types, and it is genuinely hard. The right answer is to say the shape — "you'd need a recursive type that splits the string on the dot and indexes progressively; the built-in machinery is template literal types plus a conditional" — and then say you would use a library rather than maintain that type by hand. Recognising the mechanism and declining to hand-roll it is the correct senior answer, not a dodge.

Scored on

Whether you reach for a constraint. keyof and extends in the same signature is the specific thing being looked for, and it is the most common TypeScript screen question there is.

Honest gap

Deep conditional and template-literal types I can read but I don't write from scratch often." That is an entirely acceptable position for a full-stack developer and far better than floundering.

A2TypeScript · unknown vs any

"What's the difference between any and unknown, and when would you use each?"

Reveal
Weak

"unknown is a safer any." Correct direction, no mechanism, and it will not survive "safer how?"

Good

any disables type checking in both directions — you can assign anything to it and you can do anything with it. It is a hole in the type system, and it spreads: an any passed into a typed function silently disables checking at that boundary.

unknown accepts any value but permits no operation on it until you narrow it. You must check the type before use, and the compiler enforces that.

const a: any = JSON.parse(s);
a.employee.name.toUpperCase();     // compiles. crashes at runtime.

const u: unknown = JSON.parse(s);
u.employee;                        // compile error - good
if (typeof u === 'object' && u !== null && 'employee' in u) { /* narrowed */ }

So: unknown for anything crossing a trust boundary — JSON.parse, a request body, a third-party response, a catch clause. any for escaping a type you cannot express, deliberately and locally.

Senior

Plus the three things that make this a real answer:

catch is the case everyone meets. With useUnknownInCatchVariables (on under strict), a caught error is unknown, not Error — because JavaScript lets you throw anything. So catch (e) { e.message } is a compile error, and the correct handling is e instanceof Error ? e.message : String(e). Getting this right in an error filter is a real-code detail.

In practice I do not narrow by hand at API boundaries — I validate. unknown plus a Zod schema gives you a runtime check and the inferred static type from one declaration, so the type and the validation cannot drift. That is why z.infer matters: two sources of truth for the shape of a request body is a bug waiting to happen.

And the honest use of any: it is not banned, it is quarantined. If I need it, it goes in one line with a comment saying why, never in a signature — because an any in a public signature leaks into every caller, and that is how a codebase silently stops being typed.

Follow-up

"So is as the same problem?" → largely yes. as asserts without checking; it tells the compiler to stop arguing rather than proving anything. Two legitimate uses: narrowing a value you genuinely know more about than the compiler (a discriminated union after an exhaustive check), and as const, which is a different operation entirely. Everything else — especially as unknown as T — is a runtime bug the compiler has been instructed to ignore.

Scored on

Whether you know the catch case, and whether you connect unknown to runtime validation rather than treating it as a typing curiosity.

A3TypeScript · narrowing

"You have an API that returns either a success with data or a failure with an error message. How do you type that so the compiler forces callers to handle both?"

Reveal
Weak
type Result = { data?: Employee; error?: string };

Optional fields on one object. Both can be present, both can be absent, and the compiler forces nothing — every caller writes if (r.data) and the type system never objects to code that ignores the error. This models four states when there are two.

Good

A discriminated union — a shared literal field the compiler can switch on:

type Result<T> =
  | { ok: true;  data: T }
  | { ok: false; error: string };

if (result.ok) {
  result.data;     // Employee — error is not accessible here
} else {
  result.error;    // string — data is not accessible here
}

The literal true/false is the discriminant. Checking it narrows the whole object, so the illegal states are unrepresentable rather than merely discouraged.

Senior

Plus exhaustiveness, which is the part that pays off later:

type Status = 'PENDING' | 'APPROVED' | 'REJECTED';

function label(s: Status): string {
  switch (s) {
    case 'PENDING':  return 'Awaiting approval';
    case 'APPROVED': return 'Approved';
    case 'REJECTED': return 'Rejected';
    default: {
      const _exhaustive: never = s;   // ← the guard
      throw new Error(`Unhandled status: ${s}`);
    }
  }
}

The never assignment is the whole trick. If someone adds 'CANCELLED' to Status, s is no longer never in the default branch and this file fails to compile. So adding a state to the union produces a compile error at every place that must handle it — which is exactly what you want in a workflow like leave approval, where a forgotten branch is a silent bug in production.

And the design point: I would rather make illegal states unrepresentable than validate against them. Optional-field soup pushes the check to runtime in every caller; a discriminated union does it once, in the type.

Follow-up

"Would you use this for your API responses, instead of throwing?" → a real trade-off worth naming. A Result type makes failure explicit and forces handling, at the cost of threading it through call sites; exceptions are ergonomic and easy to forget. My split: exceptions for genuinely exceptional conditions and across the HTTP boundary, where Nest's filters turn them into responses; a Result type for expected, domain-level failures a caller must branch on — "this leave request overlaps an existing one" is not an exception, it is an outcome.

Scored on

Whether you reach for a discriminated union unprompted, and whether you know the never exhaustiveness pattern. The second one is a strong differentiator because it shows you have maintained a union over time.

A4JavaScript · event loop

"What does this log, and why?" — followed by setTimeout, Promise.resolve().then(), and a synchronous log in one function.

Reveal
Weak

Getting the order right by memory but being unable to say why. The follow-up is always "why is the promise before the timeout?", and "because promises are faster" is wrong — it is not about speed at all.

Good
console.log('1');
setTimeout(() => console.log('2'), 0);
Promise.resolve().then(() => console.log('3'));
console.log('4');

// 1, 4, 3, 2

Synchronous code runs to completion first — 1, 4. Then, before the event loop continues, the microtask queue is drained completely: 3. Then a macrotask (the timer callback): 2. Promise callbacks are microtasks; setTimeout is a macrotask. Microtasks always run first, regardless of the timer being 0.

Senior

Plus the consequence that makes it matter in a Node service:

The microtask queue is drained fully between macrotasks, so a promise chain that keeps scheduling microtasks can starve the event loop — timers never fire, incoming requests are not accepted. It looks like a hang with low CPU, which is a confusing thing to debug.

The practical version of this in a web server: any synchronous CPU work blocks everything, because there is one thread running JavaScript. Hashing, a large JSON.parse, a synchronous crypto call, or generating a PDF in-process will stall every concurrent request — not slow them, stall them. That is directly relevant to my resume builder, which renders PDFs with headless Playwright: the reason that runs out of process is exactly this. If it were in-process and synchronous, one PDF would freeze the server.

And the diagnostic: if latency spikes with no corresponding database time, I would look at event-loop lag before anything else, because a blocked loop and a slow query look identical from the outside and have completely different fixes.

Follow-up

"So how would you do CPU work in Node?" → a worker thread for CPU-bound work in the same process, a separate process or service for anything heavy, or a queue if the user does not need to wait. Which one depends on whether the caller needs the result now — and that is the sync-vs-async trade-off from competency 05.

Scored on

Whether you can get from the trivia to "this is why a single blocking call takes down the whole service". The trivia is table stakes; the consequence is the signal.

B1Next.js · the boundary

"What actually happens when a Server Component renders a Client Component? What goes over the wire?"

Reveal
Weak

"Server Components render on the server and Client Components render on the client." A definition, not a mechanism, and it leaves the interesting question untouched: if the server rendered a tree containing a client component, what did it send, and how does the browser end up with a working interactive component?

Good

The server renders the Server Component tree. Where it meets a Client Component, it does not render it — it emits a reference to that component's chunk plus the serialised props being passed in. Two things are then sent:

  • HTML — so the page paints immediately and is indexable.
  • The RSC payload — a serialised description of the rendered tree, including those client-component references and their props.

The browser paints the HTML, downloads the client components' JavaScript, and hydrates: React attaches to the existing DOM and makes it interactive. On subsequent client-side navigation, only the RSC payload is fetched — no HTML.

The consequence: props crossing the boundary must be serialisable. You cannot pass a function, a class instance or a Date-with-methods expectation into a Client Component.

Senior

Plus the three implications that show you have shipped this:

"Server Component" is a misnomer worth correcting. They run on the server only — they never re-render, they have no state, and their code is never sent to the client. So the real distinction is not where it renders, it is whether the code ships. That is the framing that makes the bundle-size benefit obvious: a Server Component importing a 400KB markdown library adds nothing to the client bundle.

The 'use client' directive marks a boundary, not a file. Everything imported by a Client Component is also client code, transitively. So a single 'use client' high in the tree pulls the whole subtree client-side, and the common mistake is putting it in a layout for one interactive button. The fix is to push the boundary down — keep the layout a Server Component and make the button the client component.

Data flows down through props, and the mistake this invites is real. Because it is so easy to fetch on the server and pass down, it is also easy to pass more than the client needs — and anything passed to a Client Component is in the payload, visible in the browser. Passing a whole employee record to a component that renders the name is both a payload problem and, in an HR app, a data-exposure problem. I select the fields I pass.

Follow-up

"Can a Client Component render a Server Component?" → not by importing one. But you can pass a Server Component as children from a server parent into a client component — the server renders it and the client component just places it. That is the standard pattern for a client-side provider or layout wrapper around server content, and knowing it is the answer to "how do I use a context provider with Server Components?"

Scored on

Whether you say "the code doesn't ship" rather than "it renders on the server", and whether you know the boundary is transitive. Both are the marks of having debugged a bundle rather than read a tutorial.

Honest gap

If you have mostly used Server Components as a data layer without hitting the edges: "I use them mainly to fetch and pass down, and I've hit the serialisation boundary and the transitive 'use client' problem. I haven't worked much with streaming or Suspense boundaries in anger." Precise and fine.

B2Next.js · hydration

"You get 'Hydration failed because the server rendered HTML didn't match the client'. What causes that, and how do you find which element?"

Reveal
Weak

"I'd wrap it in a useEffect so it only renders on the client." That does silence it, and it is the reflex fix that costs you the round — you have converted a bug into a layout shift and admitted you do not know the cause. It is also the wrong fix for most of the real causes.

Good

Hydration mismatch means the DOM React produced on the client differs from the HTML the server sent. Causes, in rough order of how often they are the culprit:

  • Time and dates. new Date().toLocaleString() — the server is UTC, the browser is in the user's timezone. The single most common cause.
  • Randomness. Math.random(), a generated id, anything non-deterministic between the two renders.
  • Browser-only APIs during render. Reading window, localStorage or matchMedia in the render body — undefined on the server, a value on the client.
  • Invalid HTML nesting. A <div> inside a <p>, or a <p> inside a <p>. The browser silently repairs the structure while parsing, so the DOM no longer matches what React expects. This one is invisible in your JSX and infuriating to find.
  • Locale-dependent formatting — number and currency formatting differing by server and client locale.
  • Third-party scripts or extensions mutating the DOM before hydration.

To find it: the console error names the mismatching text or element, and React's dev overlay shows a diff. Then bisect — comment out half the page.

Senior

Plus the framing that makes the fix correct rather than merely quiet:

The right question is "which of these two renders is wrong?", not "how do I suppress this?" For a timezone problem the server render is arguably the wrong one, and the real fix is to render dates deterministically — send an ISO string and format it in a client component, or format server-side in an explicit, fixed timezone. Both are deterministic; the bug was ambiguity about whose clock applies.

The escape hatches, and their honest cost. suppressHydrationWarning is correct for exactly one case: content you know is legitimately different, like a timestamp, on a single element. It suppresses the warning without fixing the mismatch, so it is a scalpel and not a policy. Deferring to useEffect means the content is absent on first paint — you have traded a console error for a real layout shift and lost the server-rendering benefit for that subtree.

And the reason it matters more than a console warning suggests: React may discard the server HTML and re-render the subtree on the client, which costs you the performance benefit you adopted SSR for. It is not cosmetic.

Follow-up

"How would you prevent the whole class of bug?" → make render deterministic by policy: no Date formatting, no randomness and no browser API reads in a render body — those go in effects, or the value is passed in as a prop from a single authoritative source. Then the invalid-nesting family is caught by a linter rule or by validating markup, since it cannot be caught by reasoning.

Scored on

Whether you diagnose or suppress. The invalid-HTML-nesting cause is the differentiator — almost nobody names it, and everybody has eventually lost an afternoon to it.

"Explain Next.js caching in the App Router. What's cached, where, and how do you invalidate it?"

Weak

"Next caches fetches by default, so you use no-store to opt out." This was true in Next 13 and 14 and is not the default in 15+. Reciting a stale default confidently is the specific way to lose a Next.js interview, because it tells the interviewer where your knowledge came from and when.

Senior

Plus the three things that make this the answer they remember:

State the version, because the defaults moved. In 13 and 14, fetch was cached by default and you opted out. That sentence is worth more than any specific default, because it is the only durable answer in a framework that keeps changing this.

Tag-based invalidation is the one worth designing around. Time-based revalidation means serving stale data for up to the window, and path-based means knowing every route affected by a write. Any authenticated page is therefore dynamic, so the Full Route Cache mostly does not apply to an internal HR tool — almost every page is per-user. Knowing that the caching model largely does not help you in an authenticated app is a more useful observation than knowing the API.

Follow-up

"Your dashboard shows stale data after an update. Walk me through debugging it." → identify which cache. Is it stale on a hard refresh (server: Data Cache or Full Route Cache) or only on client navigation (Router Cache)? That single distinction localises it immediately. Then: was the mutation supposed to call revalidateTag/revalidatePath and did not, or is the tag not attached to the fetch it should invalidate? B4Next.js · server actions

"You've implemented leave approval as a Server Action. Is it secure?"

Reveal
Weak

"Yes — it runs on the server, so the code never reaches the client." The premise is right and the conclusion is dangerously wrong. This is the single most important security fact about Server Actions and the reason this question gets asked.

Good

A Server Action is a public HTTP endpoint. Next generates a POST route with an obfuscated identifier, and anyone who can find that identifier — it is in the client bundle — can call it with any arguments. Obscurity is not access control. So:

  • Authenticate inside the action. Every action, every time. Not in the component that renders the button, and not in a layout — those control what is displayed, not what is callable.
  • Authorise inside the action. Is this user the manager of the employee whose request they are approving? Object-level authorisation, per competency 10.
  • Validate the arguments. They come from the client. A Zod schema at the top of the action, not trust in the TypeScript signature — types are erased at runtime.
Senior

Plus the parts people miss:

Hiding the button is not authorisation, and that is the mental slip the API invites. Because the action is imported and called like a function, it feels like an internal call. It is an endpoint. The mental rule I use: if I would put a guard on it as a Nest controller method, it needs the same check inside the action.

Closed-over variables are sent to the client. If the action closes over a variable from the enclosing server component, Next serialises it into the client payload so it can be sent back on invocation — encrypted, but present. So closing over a secret, or over another user's data, is a leak in waiting. Pass what the action needs as an argument and re-fetch what it must trust from the session.

And what I would still not use them for. No idempotency by default — a double-click fires twice, so the action needs a guard or a unique constraint per competency 09. No rate limiting. And error handling is awkward, because a thrown error becomes a generic client message in production. For an approval that writes to payroll I would want the operation behind a real API route with explicit idempotency and audit, and use the action only as the transport.

Follow-up

"So where does the session check come from — is cookies() available in an action?" → yes, actions run on the server and can read cookies and headers, so the session is available. The point is that reading it is your job in every action; nothing does it for you. A shared requireUser() helper called on the first line of each action is the pattern, and a lint rule or code-review habit is what keeps it from being forgotten.

Scored on

Whether "it's a public endpoint" is your first sentence. Everything else follows from it, and candidates who do not say it usually do not know it.

B5React · forms

"Why React Hook Form rather than controlled component state? And where does Zod fit?"

Reveal
Weak

"It's less code." True and not the reason. The reason is a performance characteristic, and knowing it is the difference between having chosen the library and having inherited it.

Good

A controlled form puts every keystroke into React state, so the component — and often its children — re-renders on every character. On a large form that is visibly janky.

React Hook Form keeps inputs uncontrolled, holding values in refs and subscribing only what needs to re-render. Typing in one field does not re-render the form. You get validation, error state and submission handling without the re-render cost.

Zod fits as the schema: one declaration gives the runtime validation (zodResolver) and the static type (z.infer), so the form's type and its validation cannot drift apart.

Senior

Plus the part that matters more than the library choice:

The same Zod schema should validate on the server. Client validation is a user-experience feature — it is trivially bypassed, so it is not a security control. The server must validate independently, and if it validates with the same schema, the two cannot disagree. In a Nest backend that means either sharing the schema through a workspace package — which is exactly what a Turborepo monorepo is for, and why my résumé builder is one — or accepting that the DTO and the client schema are two sources of truth and will drift.

The honest cost of uncontrolled: when you genuinely need to react to every keystroke — a live-filtered list, a dependent field, a character counter — you have to watch that field, which reintroduces the re-render you were avoiding. Scoped to one field it is fine; watching the whole form gives back the entire benefit. That is the trade-off, and it is worth naming rather than presenting the library as free.

Follow-up

"How do you handle a server-side validation error — say the date range overlaps an existing request?" → that check cannot live on the client at all, because it needs data the client does not have. So the action returns a structured field error and the form sets it with setError, which is precisely the Result-type case from A3: an overlapping range is an expected outcome, not an exception.

Scored on

The re-render mechanism, and whether you say client validation is not security. The second is the one that transfers.

C1NestJS · DI

"What is dependency injection actually doing in Nest? Why not just import the service?"

Reveal
Weak

"It makes the code more testable and loosely coupled." Textbook and empty — it is the answer of someone who has read the phrase rather than felt the problem. The follow-up "testable how, specifically?" ends it.

Good

You declare what you need in the constructor; you never construct it. Nest's injector reads the constructor's parameter types, resolves each from the module's provider registry, builds the dependency graph, and instantiates in the right order — once, as a singleton by default.

Why it matters concretely: the class never names a concrete implementation, so in a test you can swap it. overrideProvider(EmailService).useValue(fake) gives you the real controller and service with a fake mailer. Importing directly means the import is hard-wired and you have to reach for module mocking to break it.

It also owns lifecycle: one instance shared, created in dependency order, with onModuleInit hooks run at the right time.

Senior

Plus the mechanism and the honest costs:

How it knows the types. The injector uses metadata TypeScript emits under emitDecoratorMetadata — which is why @Injectable() is required even with no options: the decorator is what triggers metadata emission. It is also why an interface cannot be injected. Interfaces do not exist at runtime, so you inject a class, or a string/symbol token with @Inject('TOKEN') and a custom provider. That is the answer to "how do you depend on an abstraction in Nest?" and most candidates do not have it.

Singleton by default, and that has a real consequence. One instance per application, so a provider holding mutable state shares it across every request — which is a cross-user data bug in an authenticated app, not a performance issue. Request-scoped providers exist for the cases that genuinely need per-request state, and they cost real performance.

The cost I would name: indirection. Finding what actually satisfies a dependency means reading module configuration rather than following an import, which is slower to navigate and harder for a newcomer. For a small service the DI container is overhead. It pays for itself when you have real seams worth swapping — which for me was the mailer and the scoring client.

Follow-up

"Two modules need each other. What happens?" → a circular dependency; Nest cannot resolve the graph and throws at startup. forwardRef() on both sides defers resolution and works. But the real answer is that a cycle is a design signal: usually the shared thing belongs in a third module both depend on, or one direction should be an event rather than a call. I would use forwardRef to unblock myself and treat it as debt.

Scored on

Whether you know why @Injectable() is needed, and that interfaces cannot be injected. Those two facts are the tell that you understand the container rather than the syntax.

C2NestJS · lifecycle

"A request needs the user's role checked, the payload validated, and the response time logged. Which of guard, pipe, interceptor and filter do you use — and in what order do they run?"

Reveal
Weak

Naming the right pieces but guessing the order, or saying "middleware does all of that". The order is the question — it determines whether your validation runs before your authorisation, which is a real design consequence.

Good

Guard for the role check — it answers "may this request proceed", returns a boolean, and throws 403 if not. Pipe for validation and transformation of the payload. Interceptor for the timing, because it wraps the handler and can act both before and after. Filter catches thrown exceptions and shapes the error response.

request
  → middleware            (Express-level; no access to the handler context)
  → GUARDS                (global → controller → route)
  → INTERCEPTORS (pre)    (global → controller → route)
  → PIPES                 (validation/transformation of params)
  → route handler         → service
  → INTERCEPTORS (post)   (in reverse)
  → EXCEPTION FILTERS     (only if something threw)
  → response
Senior

Plus the two consequences of that order that actually come up:

Guards run before pipes, so a guard sees the raw, unvalidated payload. That matters if you want to authorise based on the body — for example, "may this user approve this specific leave request?". The id in the body has not been validated or transformed yet, so the guard is working with untrusted input. My preference is to keep guards on role and identity only, and do object-level authorisation in the service where the entity has been loaded — which is also where I can do it as part of the query rather than as a separate check.

Interceptors wrap the handler, so a post-interceptor sees the return value but not thrown exceptions — those go to the filter. So a timing interceptor that only logs on success silently ignores your slowest requests, which are usually the failures. If I want timing on errors too, the interceptor has to handle the error stream and re-throw, or the logging belongs in the filter as well.

And middleware sits outside all of it with no access to the handler context, which is why ExecutionContext exists — a guard can ask which handler is about to run and read its metadata. That is how @Roles() works: the decorator sets metadata, the guard reads it with the Reflector.

Follow-up

"How does the roles decorator actually get its value to the guard?" → SetMetadata attaches a key to the handler, and the guard reads it via Reflector.getAllAndOverride against the handler and the class — the AndOverride part being how a route-level role overrides a controller-level one. Being able to describe that round trip is the sign you wrote the decorator rather than copied it.

Scored on

The order, and then whether you noticed that guards see unvalidated input. That observation is the senior half.

D1SQL · EXPLAIN

"Here's an EXPLAIN ANALYZE output for a slow query. Walk me through it. What do you look at first?"

Reveal
Weak

"I'd look for a sequential scan." It is a reasonable instinct and an incomplete method — a sequential scan on a small table is correct and fast, and the planner choosing one is often the right decision. Hunting for a keyword is not reading a plan.

Good

Read it inside-out — the innermost nodes execute first — and look at four things:

  1. Actual time, per node, and where it accumulates. The node with the largest gap between its own time and its children's time is where the cost is. Note that times are cumulative and per-loop.
  2. Estimated rows versus actual rows. A large divergence — estimated 50, actual 400,000 — means the planner is working from bad statistics, so every decision it made downstream is suspect. That is often the root cause rather than the plan shape.
  3. Scan types. Seq Scan on a large table with a selective predicate means a missing or unusable index. Index Scan is good. Index Only Scan is better — the index had every column needed, so the table was never touched.
  4. Join strategy and loop count. A Nested Loop with loops=200000 is the plan shape of an N+1 inside the database. A Hash Join on huge inputs may be spilling to disk.

With BUFFERS, I also check shared hits versus reads — reads mean it went to disk rather than cache, which changes whether the problem is the query or the working set size.

Senior

Plus the discipline that makes it a method rather than an inspection:

Row-estimate divergence is the first thing I look at, before the plan shape. Because the plan is a consequence of the estimates. If the planner thought a join would produce 50 rows and it produced 400,000, it picked a nested loop that would have been optimal for 50 — the plan is not wrong, the input to the decision was. The fix there is ANALYZE, or raising the statistics target on a skewed column, and it costs nothing compared to rewriting a query.

I care about the shape being stable, not just fast today. A plan that is fine on 10,000 rows and quadratic on a million is the bug that ships. So I look at whether the cost grows with the data — competency 07's dividing line — and for a query on the critical path I check it against production-sized data rather than a dev database. That is exactly what made the leave-approval query look fine locally and take fifty seconds for a manager with a large team.

And I use EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS), not plain EXPLAIN, with one caveat: plain EXPLAIN only gives estimates, so it cannot show the divergence that matters. ANALYZE actually runs the query — which for an UPDATE or DELETE means wrapping it in a transaction and rolling back, or you have just modified production while diagnosing it.

Follow-up

"You added an index and the planner still doesn't use it. Why?" → several honest possibilities: the predicate is not sargable (a function or a type cast on the column, like WHERE DATE(created_at) = …, which needs an expression index instead); the index's leading column is not in the predicate; the table is small enough that a scan is genuinely cheaper; statistics are stale so selectivity is mis-estimated; or the query returns so much of the table that a scan wins. The type-cast case is the one that bites most often, and it is invisible unless you read the plan.

Scored on

Whether estimated-versus-actual rows is in your answer. That single habit distinguishes people who read plans from people who look for red words.

D2SQL · index design

"You have WHERE status = 'PENDING' AND created_at > $1 ORDER BY created_at DESC. Design the index. Does column order matter?"

Reveal
Weak

"Index both columns" — meaning two separate single-column indexes. Postgres can combine them via a bitmap scan, but a single composite index is substantially better here, and the question is specifically testing whether you know why.

Good
CREATE INDEX idx_leave_status_created
    ON leave_request (status, created_at DESC);

Order matters, and the rule is equality columns before range columns. status is an equality predicate, so putting it first lets the index seek directly to the PENDING block; within that block, entries are ordered by created_at, so the range scan and the ORDER BY are both satisfied by walking the index — no separate sort step.

Reversed, as (created_at, status), the index would have to scan the whole date range and filter each entry by status. Much worse.

A B-tree index also only serves a leftmost prefix: this index helps a query filtering on status alone, but not one filtering on created_at alone.

Senior

Plus three refinements I would actually consider:

Matching the ORDER BY direction is not cosmetic. Declaring the index created_at DESC lets Postgres walk it forward for a DESC sort. It can also scan a ASC index backwards, so for a single column it often does not matter — but for a mixed ORDER BY a ASC, b DESC the index directions must match or you get an explicit sort. That is the case where people get surprised.

If the query is hot and the status set is small, a partial index is better. CREATE INDEX … ON leave_request (created_at DESC) WHERE status = 'PENDING' — smaller, so more of it stays in cache, and it stops indexing the millions of historical approved rows nobody queries this way. In a workflow table where 99% of rows are terminal and queries only ever look at the open ones, this is a large win and it is the kind of thing that shows you have tuned a real table.

And I would ask whether it can be index-only. If the endpoint needs a few more columns, an INCLUDE (employee_id, start_date) lets Postgres answer entirely from the index without touching the heap — which turns a scan plus random heap fetches into one sequential index read. That is the mechanism behind "trimmed over-fetched columns" being a performance fix and not just tidiness.

The cost, which I would name unprompted: every index slows writes and consumes space, and an unused index is pure overhead. So I would check pg_stat_user_indexes for indexes with zero scans before adding a fourth one to a write-heavy table.

Follow-up

"Now the query also filters by employee_id. Same index?" → no. If employee_id is an equality predicate it belongs with the other equality column, before the range: (status, employee_id, created_at DESC). And then the ordering question becomes which equality column is more selective first — though for equality predicates the selectivity ordering matters much less than people think, because both are seeks; what matters is that both precede the range column.

Scored on

Equality-before-range, and the leftmost-prefix rule. Partial and covering indexes are the senior bonus.

D3SQL · transactions

"Name the isolation levels and what each one allows. Then: what's Postgres's default, and what's MySQL's?"

Reveal
Weak

Naming the four levels but not what each permits, or saying "we use SERIALIZABLE to be safe" — which is a real answer only if you also know it means handling serialisation failures with retries, and that it costs throughput.

Good
LevelDirty readNon-repeatable readPhantom read
Read uncommittedallowedallowedallowed
Read committedpreventedallowedallowed

Dirty read: you see another transaction's uncommitted write. Non-repeatable read: you read a row twice in one transaction and get different values. Phantom read: you run the same query twice and get different rows, because someone inserted.

Postgres defaults to read committed. MySQL/InnoDB defaults to repeatable read. That difference is worth knowing precisely because your CV claims both.

Senior

Plus the two things that make this more than a memorised table:

The implementations differ from the standard, in both engines. Postgres never allows dirty reads at all — asking for read uncommitted silently gives you read committed. And Postgres's repeatable read is snapshot isolation, which does prevent phantoms, so it is stronger than the standard requires. InnoDB's repeatable read uses next-key and gap locks, which also prevents phantoms in practice but means your reads can take locks that block other writers — a source of deadlocks people do not expect from a SELECT. So "repeatable read" means meaningfully different things in the two databases your résumé claims.

The anomaly that survives snapshot isolation is write skew, and it is the one that matters. Two transactions each read a set, each check a condition over it, and each write based on what they read — both conditions were true at read time, and both writes together violate the invariant. The classic shape: two people both check "is anyone else on call?", both see the other, both remove themselves, and now nobody is on call. Neither transaction saw a stale row; the combination broke the rule. Repeatable read does not prevent it. Serializable does, at the cost of aborting one transaction with a serialisation failure that you must catch and retry.

And the practical version for a leave system: "does this employee have enough leave balance" is exactly write skew — two overlapping requests each check the balance and each pass. My fix is not to raise the isolation level globally; it is to make the invariant enforceable in one place: a SELECT … FOR UPDATE on the balance row so the second transaction waits, or a database constraint that makes the illegal state impossible. A constraint beats an isolation level because it cannot be forgotten by the next code path.

Follow-up

"If you go serializable, what does your code have to do?" → catch the serialisation failure (Postgres 40001) and retry the whole transaction, with a cap. That means the transaction body must be safe to run twice, which is idempotency again. Candidates who propose serializable and cannot describe the retry loop have not used it.

Scored on

Knowing both defaults, and knowing that write skew exists. Naming write skew is a genuine senior marker.

Honest gap

If you have not tuned isolation deliberately: "I've worked at the defaults — read committed on Postgres, repeatable read on MariaDB — and handled concurrency with row locks and constraints rather than by changing isolation level. I haven't run a serializable workload and dealt with retry storms." Honest, and it still demonstrates the concepts.

D4Prisma · N+1 and pooling

"How do you find and fix an N+1 in Prisma? And what changes when that code runs on Vercel against Neon?"

Reveal
Weak

"Use include." It is one of three fixes and not always the right one, and the second half of the question — serverless connection handling — is the part that separates people who have deployed this stack from people who have run it locally.

Good

Finding it: enable log: ['query'] in development and count the queries for one request. Four hundred lines where you expected two is unmistakable.

Three fixes, and they are not equivalent:

  • include or select with the relation — Prisma resolves it (typically as a second query per relation rather than a join, which is worth knowing) so you get a constant number of queries instead of one per row.
  • Two explicit queries and stitch in application code — fetch the employees, collect the ids, one findMany({ where: { id: { in: ids } } }), then map. Best when the child set is large, because it avoids transferring repeated parent columns.
  • An aggregate or raw query when what you need is a count or a sum — fetching rows to count them in JavaScript is the version of this bug that survives the include fix.

On Vercel and Neon: each serverless function instance creates its own Prisma client and therefore its own connection pool. Under concurrency you exhaust the database's connection limit. The fix is a pooler — Neon's pooled connection endpoint — plus keeping the client as a module-level singleton so warm invocations reuse it rather than opening new connections.

Senior

Plus the parts that come from having been bitten:

The fix I actually prefer is the one that stops it coming back. Fixing an N+1 once is easy; preventing the next one needs a mechanism. An integration test that asserts the query count for the important endpoints turns a regression into a failing build. Without that, the next developer adds a nested include and nobody notices for a quarter.

Nested include is where the trade-off inverts. Including a one-to-many pulls every child row for every parent — two hundred employees each with fifty leave requests is ten thousand rows to build two hundred objects, and half of them are repeated parent columns. On the serverless side, two more details. Follow-up

D5SQL · MariaDB vs Postgres

"Your CV lists both PostgreSQL and MySQL/MariaDB. Name a difference that has actually affected how you wrote something."

Reveal
Weak

"They're both SQL, mostly the same, different syntax for a few things." Technically defensible and it wastes the question. This is an invitation to demonstrate depth on a claim most candidates list without thinking, and a specific answer here is disproportionately valuable.

Good

Pick one and be concrete. The most useful:

InnoDB clusters the table on the primary key; Postgres stores rows in a heap. In InnoDB, the primary key is the row's physical location, and every secondary index stores the primary key as its pointer. So a wide or random primary key — a UUID — makes every secondary index bigger and makes inserts land in random pages, causing page splits and fragmentation. An auto-increment key appends. In Postgres the row location is a tuple id independent of the key, so a UUID primary key is much less costly.

The practical consequence: on MariaDB I would use an auto-increment or time-ordered key as the primary key even if the application exposes a UUID, keeping the UUID as a unique secondary column. On Postgres I would not bother.

Senior

That one, plus two more that shape real code:

Default isolation differs — read committed on Postgres, repeatable read on InnoDB — so identical application code has different concurrency behaviour on the two, and InnoDB's gap locks mean a SELECT in a transaction can block an insert in a way that surprises people coming from Postgres.

Postgres has analytical machinery MariaDB lacks, and it changes how you write reporting queries. generate_series for a date spine, FILTER (WHERE …) on aggregates, and richer index types — partial, expression, GIN. On MariaDB a date spine has to come from a calendar table or a recursive CTE, and conditional aggregation is SUM(CASE WHEN … THEN 1 ELSE 0 END). That is exactly the difference between writing a headcount-by-month query on the two, and it is the kind of thing you only know from having done it.

And the honest framing of my own experience: the production HR platform was MariaDB, and my own projects are Postgres. So my depth on Postgres features is from personal work and my depth on operating a database under real load is from MariaDB — I would not claim both equally.

Follow-up

"So which would you choose for a new service?" → Postgres, and the reason should be specific rather than tribal: richer index types, better analytical SQL, stronger constraint support, and jsonb when a column genuinely needs to be flexible. The condition that would flip it: an existing MySQL estate and a team that operates it well — the operational familiarity usually outweighs the feature gap.

Scored on

Whether you can name one difference precisely. The clustered-index point is the strongest available because it changes a schema decision rather than a syntax choice.

E1Auth · OIDC

"Walk me through what happens when a user clicks 'Log in with Okta'. Every step."

Reveal
Weak

"They get redirected to Okta, log in, and come back with a token." Directionally right and it skips every step where the security actually lives — which is the entire content of the question.

Good

Authorization Code flow with PKCE:

  1. Your app redirects to Okta's /authorize with the client id, redirect URI, requested scopes, a state value, and a PKCE code_challenge.
  2. The user authenticates with Okta. Your application never sees the password — which is most of the point.
  3. Okta redirects back to your redirect_uri with a short-lived authorization code and the state.
  4. You verify state matches what you sent (CSRF protection), then exchange the code at the /token endpoint — server-side — sending the PKCE code_verifier.
  5. Okta returns an ID token (who the user is, a JWT), an access token (what they may do), and usually a refresh token.
  6. You validate the ID token: signature against Okta's published JWKS, issuer, audience, expiry, and nonce. Then you establish your own session.

The code goes through the browser; the token exchange does not.

Senior

Plus the four things that make it a real answer:

Why the code exists at all. The token could be returned directly — that was the implicit flow — but then it travels through the browser URL, into history and referrer headers and logs. The code is a single-use, short-lived voucher that is worthless without the client secret or PKCE verifier, so an intercepted redirect is not an intercepted session. PKCE exists because public clients cannot hold a secret, and it binds the code to the specific client that started the flow.

ID token versus access token is the distinction people blur. The ID token is about authentication — it is for your app, proving who logged in, and it should never be sent to an API as a credential. The access token is about authorisation — it is for the API, and your app should treat it as opaque. Using an ID token as a bearer token is a common and real mistake.

Validation is not optional and is where implementations are actually broken. Verify the signature against the JWKS, cache the keys but honour rotation by key id, and check issuer, audience and expiry. A JWT that you decode without verifying is user-supplied JSON — anyone can mint one with "role": "ADMIN".

And the design decision after the flow: I exchange the token and then establish my own httpOnly session cookie rather than handing the token to the browser. That keeps the token out of reach of any XSS, and it means revocation is mine — deleting the session logs the user out immediately, which a stateless JWT cannot do.

Follow-up

"So where does SAML come in?" → an older XML-based federation protocol that solves the same problem; some enterprise identity providers still require it. OIDC is JSON, built on OAuth 2.0, and much easier to implement. If your résumé's SSO work was OIDC, say so and do not claim SAML depth — the honest boundary is "the integration I built was OIDC; I know SAML is the alternative and I haven't implemented it."

Scored on

The state check, the ID-vs-access token distinction, and signature validation. Missing signature validation is the answer that worries an interviewer most.

E2Auth · the revocation problem

"You revoke someone's manager role. Their JWT is valid for another 55 minutes. What happens, and how do you fix it without hitting the database on every request?"

Reveal
Weak

"They'd lose access on the next request." Wrong, and it is the wrong answer that matters most in this chapter — if the role is a claim inside a signed, self-contained token, nothing checks the database, so they keep manager access until the token expires. In an HR system that is 55 minutes of access to other people's records after revocation.

Good

They keep the old role until expiry. That is the fundamental trade-off of stateless tokens: you avoid a lookup per request by accepting a staleness window.

The options, in increasing cost:

  • Short access-token lifetime — five to fifteen minutes, with a refresh token. Bounds the window without a per-request lookup. Usually enough.
  • A revocation list in Redis, checked per request. Cheap — an in-memory lookup rather than a database query — and it gives immediate revocation for the small set of actually-revoked tokens.
  • Server-side sessions — a lookup per request, immediate revocation, and you have given up statelessness.
  • A token version per user — store an integer on the user, embed it in the token, bump it on any permission change. Requires reading that integer, but it is one small cached lookup rather than a full permission resolution.
Senior

Plus the reframing, which is the actual answer:

The question is what the token is allowed to assert. Putting a role in the token means the token is making an authorisation claim, and authorisation changes. Putting only identity in the token — this is user 4127, signed — means authorisation is resolved at request time from a source that can change, and the revocation problem disappears for roles entirely. The lookup cost is real, but it is a cached read of one user's permissions, which is cheap and is a cache you control and can invalidate on change.

Which I would choose depends on the blast radius of stale authorisation. For most applications, a short token lifetime is the right trade — simple, no per-request state, and a few minutes of staleness is tolerable. For an HR system where the role governs access to salary and performance data, I would not accept a 55-minute window: identity in the token, permissions resolved per request from a cache invalidated on role change.

And this connects to how I enforce it. Because the RBAC in my project is enforced at the query level rather than in a guard, the permission set has to be loaded to build the query anyway — so resolving it per request costs almost nothing extra. That is a case where the enforcement design and the token design fit together, and it is worth saying that it was a consequence rather than a coincidence.

Follow-up

"How do you invalidate that permission cache across several instances?" → the cache lives in Redis rather than in process memory, so all instances share it and a single delete on role change is sufficient. In-process caching would need pub/sub invalidation and would still have a propagation window — which is the same class of problem as the Socket.IO adapter in Chapter F: any per-instance state needs a shared backing store once you run more than one pod.

Scored on

Whether you know the answer is "nothing happens for 55 minutes", and whether you get to "don't put authorisation in the token". That reframe is the senior answer and it is what the question exists to find.

E3Auth · RBAC at the query level

"Your CV says RBAC is enforced 'at the query level'. What does that mean, and why not in a guard?"

Reveal
Weak

Describing a roles guard. That is role-based route protection — it answers "may a team manager call this endpoint" — and it is not what the phrase claims. If this is all you can describe, the CV line is overstated and the interviewer now knows it.

Good

Two distinct layers:

The guard answers "may this role reach this endpoint". A team manager may call GET /employees; a viewer may not call POST /employees.

The query answers "which rows". A team manager calling GET /employees gets their subtree; an HR manager gets everyone; a viewer gets aggregates only. That filter is applied where the data is fetched, not checked after:

// the scope is derived from the actor, and the query cannot be
// built without it
const scope = scopeForActor(actor);   // → { departmentIds } | 'ALL' | 'SELF'
return prisma.employee.findMany({ where: { ...scope, ...filters } });

Why not the guard: a guard would have to know, for every endpoint, which parameter identifies the resource and how to check ownership — and every new endpoint has to remember. Putting the filter in the data-access layer means there is no code path that returns rows the actor may not see.

Senior

Plus the reasoning and the honest limits:

The principle is that the safe thing should be the default, not the disciplined choice. Authorisation implemented as a check is something a developer can forget; implemented as a constraint on how data is reached, it is something they cannot bypass without obviously going around the layer. That is the same argument as the database constraint in D3 — prefer mechanisms that cannot be forgotten to rules that must be remembered.

What it does not solve, and I would say this unprompted. Row scoping does not give you field-level authorisation: a team manager may see that an employee exists but perhaps not their salary. That is a projection concern, and doing it by scope alone means either separate queries per role or filtering fields after fetch — which reintroduces the forgettable check. My approach is a per-role field allowlist applied in the serialisation layer, so both the rows and the columns are decided by the actor.

And the cost: it makes the data layer aware of the actor, which is a real coupling — every repository method needs the actor threaded through it, and it is harder to write a legitimate unscoped query for a background job. My compromise is an explicit system-actor scope for jobs, so bypassing the filter is possible but has to be named, which means it shows up in review rather than happening by omission.

Follow-up

"Postgres has row-level security. Why not use that?" → a good question and a legitimate alternative: RLS moves the guarantee into the database, so even a raw query cannot bypass it. The costs are that it needs the actor set as a session variable per connection — which fights connection pooling — that the policies are invisible from application code, and that debugging "why is this row missing" gets harder. For a single application with one data-access layer I would keep it in the application; for a database queried by several services or by analysts directly, RLS is the stronger choice.

Scored on

Whether you distinguish "which endpoints" from "which rows" — and then whether you volunteer that field-level authorisation is a separate problem. That last point is what makes the CV claim credible rather than aspirational.

F1Realtime · Socket.IO at scale

"Your app runs as three pods in Kubernetes. A worker finishes stage three and emits a progress event. The user sees nothing. Why?"

Reveal
Weak

"Maybe the socket disconnected." Possible, and it is not the structural answer this question is asking for. This is the single most common real bug when a socket application is scaled beyond one instance, and it is entirely predictable.

Good

Socket.IO's default adapter keeps the connection registry in the memory of one process. So a client connected to pod A exists only in pod A's registry. When the worker — or pod B — emits to that client's room, pod B looks in its own memory, finds nobody, and the event goes nowhere. No error; the event is simply delivered to zero recipients.

The fix is a shared adapter: @socket.io/redis-adapter, which publishes emits over Redis pub/sub so every instance forwards to whichever of its own clients belong to the room. Once that is in place, any instance can emit to any client.

Senior

Plus the two adjacent problems that come with it:

Sticky sessions matter for the handshake, depending on transport. Socket.IO can start on HTTP long-polling and upgrade to WebSocket. During polling, several separate HTTP requests belong to one session, so they must reach the same instance — without sticky routing the handshake fails intermittently, which presents as flaky connections rather than an obvious error. Either configure the ingress for session affinity, or force transports: ['websocket'] and accept losing the polling fallback. The Redis adapter solves broadcasting; it does not solve the handshake.

And the deeper design point, which is the answer I would actually lead with: this bug is a symptom of the socket carrying state. If the progress lives in the database and the socket only says "job 41 changed, re-read it", then a missed event costs you a delayed update rather than a lost one — and a client that reconnects, refreshes, or opens a second tab converges on the truth. That is the same conclusion as competency 09: any per-instance in-memory state becomes a bug the moment you run two instances, and the durable fix is to move the state rather than to synchronise the memory.

Follow-up

"How does the worker emit at all — it's a separate process from the API." → it does not emit directly. Either the worker writes progress and publishes to Redis, and the API instances subscribe and forward to their own sockets; or the worker uses a Socket.IO emitter client that writes to the same Redis channel the adapter reads. The first is cleaner because the worker then has no knowledge of transport at all.

Scored on

Whether "in-memory adapter" is your first thought. It is the canonical scaling question for socket applications and the answer is expected to be immediate.

Honest gap

If your project ran as a single instance: "HR Insight AI runs as one instance, so I haven't hit this in production — but the default adapter is in-memory, so it would break as soon as there were two, and the fix is the Redis adapter plus sticky sessions for the handshake." F2Jobs · BullMQ

"A report job fails. Walk me through your retry configuration, and what happens to a job that can never succeed."

Reveal
Weak

"BullMQ retries it automatically." It retries if you configured attempts; the default is one attempt, so an unconfigured job does not retry at all. And "automatically" avoids every decision the question is about.

Good
await queue.add('generate-report', data, {
  jobId: `report:${reportId}`,   // ← idempotency: same id = not re-queued
  attempts: 3,
  backoff: { type: 'exponential', delay: 2000 },  // 2s, 4s, 8s
  removeOnComplete: { age: 3600, count: 1000 },
  removeOnFail: false,           // keep failures for inspection
});

Three attempts with exponential backoff. After the final failure the job moves to the failed set, where it stays — that is the dead-letter equivalent — with its error and stack retained for inspection and manual retry.

A job that can never succeed — a malformed payload, a deleted employee — will burn its attempts and land there. Which is correct, but only useful if something is watching.

Senior

Plus the four decisions that make this production-ready:

Distinguish retryable from terminal failures, and do not retry the terminal ones. A database timeout is transient and worth three attempts. A validation error or a missing referenced record will fail identically three times, so retrying wastes eight seconds and muddies the metrics. I throw an UnrecoverableError for those so BullMQ moves the job to failed immediately rather than retrying a certainty.

The jobId is the idempotency mechanism and it is easy to get wrong. Keying on the report id means a double-click does not create a second job. But it also means that once the job completes and is removed, the same id can be enqueued again — so removeOnComplete and idempotency interact, and if I needed exactly-once semantics over a long window I would record completion in the database rather than relying on the queue's memory of it.

The failed set needs an alert, not just retention. An unmonitored dead-letter queue is a slower way of losing data. So: a metric on failed count and an alert when it grows, per competency 14. Failed jobs nobody looks at were not handled — they were postponed.

And concurrency has to be set against the constraint downstream. Worker concurrency of 50 on a database with 20 connections means most workers block on the pool. The right number comes from whatever the job actually contends for, which for a report pipeline is database connections and — if there is an LLM call in stage five — a provider rate limit. That is what BullMQ's rate limiter is for, rather than trying to express it as concurrency.

Follow-up

"What if the worker is killed mid-job — SIGTERM during a deploy?" → the job is not lost, because BullMQ tracks active jobs and a stalled one is recovered and retried after the stall interval. But that only helps if the job is safe to re-run, which is idempotency again. And the deploy should handle it properly: catch SIGTERM, stop accepting new jobs, let in-flight ones finish within the grace period. Without that, every deploy produces a handful of stalled retries.

Scored on

Whether you separate retryable from terminal failures, and whether the dead-letter set has an alert. Both are things people only do after being burned.

"Mock both — mock Prisma and mock the mailer." Mocking the database is the trap. If you mock the query, you are asserting that your code calls your mock with certain arguments, which tests your understanding of the ORM rather than whether the query is correct. The bugs that actually happen — a wrong join, a missing index, a null constraint, a wrong where — are all invisible to that test.

Good

Mock the email; use a real database.

The mailer is an external side effect with no useful behaviour to verify locally, and sending real mail from a test is wrong. So it is replaced with a fake, and you assert it was called with the right recipient.

The database is not an external system in the same sense — it is where your logic actually lives. Run tests against a real Postgres (a container, or a dedicated test schema), with each test creating its own data and rolling back or truncating afterwards. That is an integration test and it catches the class of bug that matters.

In Nest this is overrideProvider(MailService).useValue(fake) in the testing module while everything else stays real — which is what DI is for, per C1.

Senior

Plus the general rule and its cost:

The rule I use: mock at the boundary of things you do not own, and only when the real thing is slow, non-deterministic, costly or has side effects you cannot undo. A payment provider, an email service, an LLM API — mock those. Your own database, your own modules — do not. Every mock is an assumption about how a dependency behaves, and it goes stale silently: the mock keeps passing after the real API changes, which is the worst possible failure mode because the test is now actively lying to you.

The honest cost of using a real database is slower tests and setup complexity — a container in CI, migrations run before the suite, and test isolation to get right. It is worth it, and the isolation question has a clean answer: wrap each test in a transaction and roll back, which is fast and gives perfect isolation, with the caveat that it does not work if the code under test manages its own transactions.

And for the external ones I would go one step further than a mock: a contract test or a recorded fixture, so at least one test exercises the real shape of the provider's response. Otherwise the mock encodes what I believed the API returned in the month I wrote it.

Follow-up

"So do you write unit tests at all?" → yes, for logic with branches and no I/O: date-range overlap, leave-balance calculation, the risk-band thresholds, permission scope derivation. Those are pure functions and unit tests are exactly right — fast, precise, and they fail pointing at the function. The distinction is not "unit versus integration" as a philosophy; it is that pure logic gets unit tests and anything crossing a boundary gets an integration test.

Scored on

Whether you resist mocking the database. It is the single most revealing testing question, because the mock-everything answer correlates strongly with never having had a test suite catch a real bug.

G2Testing · E2E strategy

"You have Playwright and Cucumber suites. How many E2E tests should there be, and how do you keep them from becoming a liability?"

Reveal
Weak

"As many as possible, for confidence." This is how E2E suites die. A large browser suite is slow, flaky and expensive to maintain, and once it is unreliable the team stops believing red builds — at which point it is worse than having none, because it costs time and provides no signal.

Good

Few, and chosen by consequence. Five to fifteen journeys covering the paths where a break is unacceptable: log in via SSO, submit a leave request, approve one, run a report, load the dashboard. Everything else pushed down to integration or unit tests, which are faster and localise failures better.

Keeping them healthy:

  • Never wait on time. Assert on state — an element, a network response. waitForTimeout is the primary cause of flake.
  • Each test creates its own data and does not depend on another test's effects or on run order.
  • Use role- and label-based locators rather than CSS paths, so a styling change does not break the suite.
  • Fix flakes as bugs, do not paper over them with retries.
Senior

Plus the two structural things:

Test data setup should go through the API or the database, not the UI. If a leave-approval test has to click through creating an employee and submitting a request before it can test approving one, it is slow and it fails for reasons unrelated to approval. Seed the precondition directly and let the test exercise only the journey it is named after. That single change usually halves both the runtime and the flake rate.

A flaky test is either a bad test or a real race, and it is worth finding out which. The E2E suite runs against a real browser and a real server, so it is the only place a genuine race condition shows up — and the temptation is to add a retry and lose the signal. Some of the most valuable bugs I have seen were first visible as intermittent test failures.

On Cucumber specifically, the honest answer is in competency 11: if nobody outside the engineering team reads the Gherkin, you are paying an indirection cost for a collaboration benefit you are not receiving, and I would use Playwright's own syntax instead. Saying that shows you understand what the tool is for.

Follow-up

"Where do these run — every commit?" → unit and integration on every push; E2E on the pull request and before deploy, in parallel shards to keep it under a few minutes. Running a fifteen-minute browser suite on every commit trains people to stop watching CI, which defeats the point of having it.

Scored on

Whether your instinct is "few and chosen" rather than "many". And whether you seed data via the API — that detail is the one that marks real experience.

H1Docker · image size and caching

"Your Node image is 1.4GB and every build takes eight minutes even when only one file changed. Fix both."

Reveal
Weak

"Use a smaller base image like alpine." It helps and it is one line of a real answer — and it does not touch the eight-minute rebuild, which is a layer cache problem, not a size problem.

Good

The rebuild time is layer ordering. Docker caches per layer and invalidates everything after the first changed one. If you COPY . . before npm ci, then any source change invalidates the dependency install. Copy the manifests first, install, then copy the source:

COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci                 # ← cached unless the lockfile changes
COPY . .
RUN npm run build

The size is a multi-stage build. Build in one stage with all the dev dependencies and the toolchain; copy only the compiled output and production dependencies into a slim runtime stage. The compiler, the test framework and the source never ship.

FROM node:22-slim AS build
WORKDIR /app
COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci
COPY . .
RUN npm run build

FROM node:22-slim
WORKDIR /app
COPY package*.json ./
RUN npm ci --omit=dev
COPY --from=build /app/dist ./dist
USER node
CMD ["node", "dist/main.js"]
Senior

Plus four things I would add:

A .dockerignore first, because it is the cheapest win. Without it, COPY . . ships node_modules, .git, test output and local env files into the build context — which is slow, fat, and a genuine secret leak risk. It also breaks the cache constantly, since .git changes on every commit.

npm ci, never npm install, in a build. ci installs exactly the lockfile and fails if the lockfile and manifest disagree, so the build is reproducible. install can silently resolve different versions than the ones you tested.

Run as a non-root user. USER node costs nothing and removes a whole class of container escape severity. It is the kind of thing a reviewer notices immediately.

And on alpine specifically, the trade-off worth naming: it is much smaller but uses musl instead of glibc, which breaks native modules and has produced real DNS and performance oddities in Node. I default to -slim — most of the size benefit, none of the surprises — and reach for alpine only if the image size genuinely matters and I have tested the native dependencies.

Follow-up

"In a Turborepo monorepo, how does this change?" → the naive Dockerfile copies the whole repo and installs everything, so every app's image rebuilds when any package changes. The fix is to prune the workspace to just the target app and its dependencies — turbo prune --docker produces exactly that trimmed context — so the cache only invalidates when something the app actually depends on changes. Your résumé builder is a Turborepo, so this is a fair question to get.

Scored on

Whether you separate the two problems — cache ordering versus multi-stage — rather than answering both with "smaller base image".

H2CI/CD · pipeline design

"Design the GitHub Actions pipeline for the HR platform. What gates a merge, and what happens on deploy?"

Reveal
Weak

"Run tests, then build, then deploy." The shape without any decisions in it — and it misses the one thing that reliably breaks deploys in this stack, which is migration ordering.

Good
ON PULL REQUEST — all gate the merge, run in parallel where possible
  lint + typecheck        (fast; fails in ~1 min)
  unit + integration      (against a Postgres service container)
  build                   (catches build-only breakage)
  E2E (Playwright)        (sharded, on the built app)

ON MERGE TO MAIN
  build + tag image  →  push to registry
  run migrations     →  deploy  →  smoke check  →  (rollback on failure)

Branch protection requires the checks to pass. Secrets come from the environment, never the repository. Docker layer caching between runs so builds are not eight minutes.

Senior

Plus the three decisions that matter:

Migrations must be backwards-compatible, and this is the thing that causes real outages. During a rolling deploy, old and new code run simultaneously against one database. So a migration that drops or renames a column breaks the still-running old pods. The discipline is expand-then-contract: add the new column, deploy code that writes both and reads the new one, backfill, and only drop the old column in a later deploy once no running code references it. Two deploys instead of one, and no downtime. Getting this wrong is the most common way a "simple" schema change takes production down.

Fail fast, and order the pipeline by cost. Lint and typecheck first because they take a minute; do not make someone wait fifteen minutes for the browser suite to discover a type error. And keep the PR gate under about ten minutes total, because beyond that people stop waiting for it and start merging on hope.

A smoke check after deploy, and a defined rollback. Deploying without verifying is deploying and hoping. One request to a health endpoint that actually touches the database, and an automatic rollback to the previous image on failure. I would also want the deploy to be a distinct, revertible artefact — which is what tagging the image by commit gives you: rolling back is redeploying a known tag rather than reverting code and rebuilding.

Follow-up

"How do you handle a migration that has to be destructive — you genuinely need to drop the column?" → you still do it in two deploys; you just accept that the second one is the destructive step and gate it on confirming no code reads the column. For anything with data loss I would also want a verified backup immediately before, and I would run it in a maintenance window rather than as part of a normal deploy. The rule is that the destructive step is never in the same deploy as the code change that makes it safe.

Scored on

Expand-then-contract. It is the answer that shows you have deployed a schema change to a running system rather than only to a local database.

Python, FastAPI & model serving

Architecture level only. The modelling round — features, evaluation, drift — is covered in interview/round-5-ml-and-llm-product-round/ and question-banks/08; this is the part a full-stack interviewer asks.

I1Service boundary

"How does your Nest API talk to the Python scoring service? What's the contract, and what happens when it's slow?"

Reveal
Weak

"It makes an HTTP call to the FastAPI endpoint." True and it answers neither half. "What happens when it's slow" is the reliability question from competency 09 and it is the reason the question was asked.

Good

The contract: a POST with the feature payload, validated by a Pydantic model on the Python side and by a DTO with class-validator on the Nest side. Response is the score plus the contributing factors. Versioned in the path, and both sides fail loudly on a shape mismatch rather than coercing.

When it is slow: an explicit timeout on the client — a call with no timeout can hang forever and hold a connection. Then a decision: for a single employee's score on page load, fail soft and show the last stored score with a staleness indicator. For the nightly batch, retry with backoff, because nobody is waiting.

Senior

Plus the three things that make it a designed boundary:

Feature-schema mismatch is the dangerous failure, not the timeout. If the model was trained on features in a given order and meaning, and the API sends a slightly different set — a renamed field, a changed encoding, a unit change — the service returns a plausible wrong number rather than an error. That is far worse than a 500, because nobody notices. So the model artefact should declare the feature schema it expects and the service should reject a mismatch explicitly. Silent numerical wrongness is the failure mode I would design against first.

Batch, not per-request, wherever possible. Scoring ten thousand employees one HTTP call at a time is ten thousand round trips for work that vectorises. The nightly job should send batches; per-request scoring should exist only for the what-if simulation, where the input is genuinely new. That is competency 03 — the round trip is the cost, not the arithmetic.

And the boundary should stay one-directional. The Python service knows nothing about users, roles or the database — it takes features and returns numbers. If it needed to query the database itself, I would have two services with the same data access and two places for authorisation to go wrong. Keeping it a pure function of its input is what makes it testable and replaceable.

Follow-up

"Is the FastAPI endpoint async? Does it matter for CPU-bound inference?" → it matters and the intuition misleads. async def helps when the handler awaits I/O; tree-model inference is CPU-bound, so running it inside an async handler blocks the event loop and serialises requests. Either use a normal def handler, which FastAPI runs in a threadpool, or offload explicitly. Same underlying lesson as A4 — one thread, and blocking it stalls everything.

Scored on

Whether you name the silent-wrongness failure mode. Timeouts are the expected answer; feature misalignment is the one that shows you thought about what this specific service can get wrong.

Honest gap

"It's a personal project, so the model is trained offline and deployed by hand — no retraining pipeline, no drift monitoring, no shadow deployment. I know that's what production ML needs and I haven't built it." Say this before being pushed to it.

Chapter J

Practices

Agile, code review and documentation. Short chapter, and the one place your management background is a straightforward asset rather than something to explain.

J1Code review · as reviewee

"Someone leaves twenty comments on your pull request, and you disagree with about five of them. What do you do?"

Reveal
Weak

Either "I'd just make all the changes" — which is compliance, not collaboration, and means bad suggestions land in the codebase — or defending all five, which turns a review into a negotiation. Both answers describe someone who is hard to work with, in opposite directions.

Good

Sort them first. Most of twenty comments are usually uncontroversial — make those immediately and say so, so the reviewer can see progress rather than a wall of unresolved threads.

For the five: separate "I think this is wrong" from "I did it differently on purpose". For the second kind, explain the reason in the thread — often the reviewer lacked context and agrees at once. For the first kind, ask rather than assert: "I went with X because of Y — does that change it, or am I missing something?"

Anything unresolved after one round goes to a five-minute conversation. Review threads are a poor medium for disagreement and get worse with each reply.

Senior

Plus the framing:

Distinguish blocking from non-blocking, and ask the reviewer to as well. Twenty comments with no severity marking is genuinely hard to act on — it is not clear what prevents a merge and what is a preference. I would ask for that explicitly, and I mark my own reviews that way, because it is the single change that makes reviews faster for everyone.

Volume is often a signal about the pull request, not the reviewer. Twenty comments frequently means the change was too large, or the approach should have been agreed before I wrote it. The systemic fix is a smaller PR, or a five-minute conversation about the approach first — and if this keeps happening, that is the thing to change rather than the review process.

And I would concede the aesthetic ones fast. Naming and structure preferences are not worth the goodwill; consistency with what the reviewer expects has real value even when I would have chosen differently. I save disagreement for correctness, security, and things that will be expensive to change later — which is roughly the priority order in competency 12.

Follow-up

"And if the reviewer is more senior and you're still sure they're wrong?" → the disagree-and-commit pattern from competency 15: state the concern as a consequence, propose the cheapest way to settle it, and then commit either way — with the caveat named in writing if it is a correctness or security issue, so the decision is recorded rather than lost.

Scored on

Whether you are pleasant to review and not a pushover. They are predicting what you are like on their PRs, and both failure modes are costly.

The Agile question, and how to answer it honestly

"Tell me about your Agile process" is usually a low-stakes question with one trap: describing the textbook rather than what actually happens. Nobody's process is clean, and claiming yours is reads as either inexperience or a sales pitch.

A good answer names the shape (two-week sprints, standup, refinement, retro), then one thing that worked and one that did not, specifically. "Refinement was the most valuable meeting for us, because the estimates were where we found out we disagreed about scope. Standups drifted into status reporting to the lead rather than coordination, which I think is the default failure mode unless someone actively resists it." That is a real observation and it invites a real conversation — which is what the question is for.

"Honestly — much less than you'd hope. Reading cookies() or headers() opts a route out of static rendering, so an authenticated page is dynamic and the full route cache mostly doesn't apply. The data cache still helps for anything shared across users — reference data, department lists — and the router cache still makes navigation feel fast. But per-user pages don't get much from it, and I'd rather cache deliberately at the data layer than expect the framework to do it."

Correct, and the "much less than you'd hope" is the honest answer to a question with a slightly disappointing truth. Comfortable delivering that.
Int

"Last one. We run three replicas. You've got Socket.IO on your project — what breaks?"

The multi-instance question. Expecting "in-memory adapter" immediately.
You

"The default adapter keeps the connection registry in one process's memory, so if a client is connected to pod A and pod B emits to their room, pod B looks in its own memory, finds nobody, and the event silently goes nowhere. The fix is the Redis adapter, so emits go over pub/sub and every instance forwards to its own clients. And you need sticky sessions for the handshake if long-polling is enabled, because the polling requests all have to reach the same instance."

Immediate and complete, including sticky sessions. That second half is usually missing.
Int

"Did you hit that in production?"

The honesty check. The previous answer was good enough to be either experience or study. Finding out which.
You

"No — that project runs as a single instance, so I've never actually had the bug. I know it's coming as soon as there's a second one."

This is the right answer and it costs nothing. Bluffing here would have unwound the previous answer. The boundary is drawn cleanly and the knowledge still counts.
Int

"Fine. Actually, one more — Kubernetes is on your CV. If a pod's in CrashLoopBackOff, what do you do?"

Probing the weakest claim. Predictable: it is the most senior-sounding thing on the document.
You

"I'd check the logs first — kubectl logs with --previous, since the current container is gone — then describe the pod for the exit code and events. Usually it's a config or secret that isn't there, a failing readiness probe, or the process exiting on startup because of a bad env var.

I should be precise about this one though. I wrote and own the pipeline that builds the image and deploys to our cluster, and I've written and debugged manifests for my own services. I didn't set up the cluster — no ingress, no autoscaling — and a node-level problem I'd be learning. So: comfortable as a consumer of Kubernetes, not as an operator."

Answered the question competently, then volunteered the boundary before being pushed to it. The technical answer was fine and the boundary is the more valuable part — it means I can trust the rest of the CV.

Note: had they claimed operator depth and failed the next question, everything earlier would be re-weighted.
The two wobbles, and what to learn from them

The recovered one: "did you hit that in production?" was a moment where the temptation to say yes is real, because the previous answer was strong and the honest answer sounds like a retreat. It is not — the knowledge still scored, and the honesty made every earlier answer more believable rather than less. That trade is always favourable and it is worth internalising before you are in the chair.

The one that was not a wobble at all, but felt like one: the Kubernetes question. Volunteering a limitation mid-interview feels like losing ground. In the interviewer's notes it is the second-most valuable moment in the round, because an interview is a search for your floor and a candidate who marks their own floor accurately has just done the interviewer's job for them. The alternative — holding the claim through one more question and collapsing — is the actual failure mode.

What this round did not test, and would have if it ran longer: anything behavioural, anything about working with people, and any live coding. A screen like this is a knowledge and reasoning filter. The rounds after it are where the offer is decided.

B · The deep dive on your own code

They have read your GitHub. This round tests whether you made the decisions or inherited them — and it contains the hardest moment in any loop: being caught having copied a pattern you cannot justify.

Round 3 · deep dive · 60 min · HR Insight AI · interviewer has the repo open
Int

"Your README says RBAC is enforced at the query level with four roles. Show me how that works."

Starting from a specific claim in the repo. This is the round where CV language gets checked against code.
You

"There are two layers. A guard checks whether the role can reach the endpoint at all. Then in the service, the actor's role resolves to a scope — an HR manager gets everything, a team manager gets their department subtree, a viewer gets aggregates only, and the scope is spread into the where clause. So the query can't be constructed without it."

Clear, matches the claim. Now checking whether it survives a consequence question.
Int

"So a team manager can see the employees in their subtree. Can they see those employees' salaries?"

Level two — field-level authorisation. Row scoping does not solve it and most candidates have not noticed.
You

"That's a separate problem and row scoping doesn't cover it. In my implementation there's a per-role field allowlist applied when the entity is serialised, so the role decides both which rows and which columns. It's a second mechanism, not a consequence of the first."

Knew the distinction and had built for it. Going deeper.
Int

"Your audit log records actor, entity and IP. Why IP?"

An innocuous-sounding question about a design choice. Checking whether the fields were chosen or copied from an example.
You

"Honestly — because it's a common field in audit examples and I added it without a specific requirement driving it. If I think about it now, the case for it is detecting access from an unexpected location, and for an internal tool behind SSO that's fairly weak. And there's an argument against: it's personal data under GDPR, so I'm storing something with a retention obligation attached to serve a purpose I hadn't defined.

What I'd actually want in that table and don't have is the actor's role at the time of the action. Right now the role comes from joining to the user, so if someone gets promoted the audit history silently rewrites itself — it'll claim an HR manager approved something they approved as a team lead. That's a real bug and it's more important than the IP."

This is the best moment in the round. Conceded the field was unjustified, then produced a genuine flaw in their own design that I had not asked about — and it is the correct flaw. An audit table joining to current role is a classic error.

Candidates who invent a justification here score far worse. This scored higher than a clean answer would have.
Int

"Let's stay there. How would you fix it, given the table already has rows?"

Now it is a migration question. Testing whether they can change a schema that has history in it.
You

"Add a nullable actor_role column and start writing it — that's non-destructive and safe to deploy on its own. For existing rows I can't reconstruct the role at the time, because that information was never recorded, so backfilling from current role would be manufacturing data that looks authoritative and isn't. I'd leave them null and treat null as 'unknown, pre-migration' explicitly, rather than filling it in.

Then once nothing reads the joined role, drop that path. Two deploys — expand then contract — because during a rolling deploy old and new code are both running."

Refused to backfill fabricated data and gave the reason in terms of the data's meaning. Expand-then-contract unprompted. This is the kind of judgement I would want on an audit table.
Int

"You have a six-stage pipeline streaming progress over WebSocket. If I refresh the page halfway through, what do I see?"

Level three on a different subsystem. This one usually finds a real weakness, because socket-only progress is the natural first implementation.
You

"You'd lose it. The progress is emitted over the socket and not persisted, so a refresh gives you a page with no idea a job is running. That's a real weakness.

The fix is a job row with status and current stage, updated as each stage completes, and the socket becomes a notification that the row changed rather than the source of truth. On connect or refresh the client reads the row. That one change fixes the refresh case, the crash case and the second-tab case together — and it's the same reason the Socket.IO in-memory adapter breaks across replicas: any state that only exists in one process is state you lose."

Named their own weakness before being pushed, then generalised it correctly. Connecting it to the adapter problem shows the underlying principle is understood rather than the two facts being memorised separately.
Int

"Why is the scoring service separate? Couldn't that be a function in the API?"

The architecture justification. Watching for "Python is better for ML", which is a language preference dressed as a decision.
You

"The concrete reason is XGBoost — the trained artefact and its inference path are Python, and reimplementing tree inference in TypeScript to avoid a service is a bad trade. But the boundary is where it is for a reason I'd defend beyond that: the Python service knows nothing about users. It takes features and returns a score. All the authorisation, the audit logging, the knowledge of who's asking stays in the Nest API. So the sensitive concern lives in one place and the scoring service can be tested or replaced without touching anything security-relevant.

And the honest alternative — if the model were a logistic regression I'd have exported the coefficients and scored in TypeScript, and shipped one service. It's justified by XGBoost specifically, not by 'ML'."

Boundary drawn around a concern rather than a language, and volunteered the condition under which the decision reverses. That is the three-part trade-off shape.
Int

"How do you know the model is any good?"

Deliberately open, and it is the edge of the claim. This is where a personal project meets a production standard.
You

"For the model itself I held out a test set and looked at AUC and the precision-recall trade-off rather than accuracy, because attrition is imbalanced — a model that predicts nobody leaves is highly accurate and useless.

But I should be clear about the boundary. It's a personal project on a dataset I assembled; there's no retraining pipeline, no drift monitoring, no shadow deployment, and no real-world outcome to validate against — nobody acted on a prediction and then left or stayed. So I can tell you the model separates the classes on held-out data. I can't tell you it works, and I'd be wary of anyone who claimed that from a project of this size."

Distinguished "separates classes on held-out data" from "works", which is the distinction that matters and almost nobody makes. Knew the imbalance point.

The last clause is a slight risk — mildly editorialising about other candidates — but it lands as confidence rather than arrogance here.
Int

"If you had two weeks on this project, what would you do?"

The closing question of every deep dive. Tests whether they have a view about their own work's priorities.
You

"The job row for pipeline progress, first — it's the biggest correctness gap and it's a day. Then the actor_role column on the audit log. Then tests asserting query counts on the heavy endpoints, because I've already been bitten by an N+1 once at work and there's nothing stopping the same thing here.

What I wouldn't do is the retraining pipeline. It's the most impressive-sounding thing missing and it's not what's actually wrong with the project."

Ranked by value, with a reason, and explicitly declined the impressive-sounding option. Strong close.
What made this round go well

Count the concessions: the IP field was unjustified, the pipeline progress is not persisted, the model cannot be said to work. Three admissions of weakness in one hour — and the round scored higher for them, because each one came with either a specific fix or a precise boundary, and because each was volunteered rather than extracted.

That is the whole strategy for a deep-dive round. You are being asked about a project you built alone, under time pressure, without review. It has flaws, the interviewer can see them in the repo, and the only question is whether you can see them too. A candidate who defends everything is telling you they cannot evaluate their own work — which is a much worse signal than an imperfect project, because it predicts every code review you will ever have with them.

C · The architecture round

Competency 04's eight steps, run live. Abridged to the decision points — the value here is the shape of the conversation, not the design.

Round 4 · system design · 45 min · one sentence of prompt
Int

"Design a system that lets managers see which of their people are at risk of leaving."

Deliberately one sentence. The first two minutes are the highest-signal part of the round.
You

"Let me write down what it has to do before I draw anything, and check a few things with you.

[writes: MUST DO / MUST BE TRUE / SIZE / DATA MODEL / COMPONENTS / CONTRACT / FAILURE / 10×]

So functionally: ingest employee data from somewhere, produce a risk score per person, show a manager their team ranked by risk, and probably explain why someone is at risk — a score with no reason is not actionable. Am I right that explanation matters, or is a number enough?"

Headings first, then a requirement question that is actually a product insight. "A score with no reason isn't actionable" is the kind of thing that gets remembered from a design round.
Int

"Explanation matters. HR will not act on a black box."

Good — they engaged with it, which means the design now has a constraint that came from the candidate.
You

"Then two non-functional questions. How many employees — a thousand or a million? And does the score have to be live, or is nightly acceptable? Nightly versus live is the biggest fork in the design, so I'd rather ask than assume."

Asked the fork question explicitly and said why. Most candidates assume real-time and design something far too complex.
Int

"Ten thousand employees. Nightly is fine."

Now watching whether they do the arithmetic or reach for streaming infrastructure.
You

"Then let me size it out loud. Ten thousand employees, one scoring run a night, call it forty features each — that's four hundred thousand values a night. That's nothing; it fits in memory on a laptop. So this is not a data-volume problem. It's a correctness and access-control problem, and that changes what I should spend the design on.

Which means: a Postgres table and a nightly job. No streaming, no queue partitioning, no warehouse."

Did the arithmetic, reached the right conclusion, and stated what the problem actually is. This is the single most differentiating moment available in a design round — the majority of candidates over-engineer here.
You

"Now the data model, before components, because I think that's where the real decision is.

The obvious model is one row per employee with their current attributes and a risk score. I don't want that. If we overwrite the employee row each time, we can't answer 'was this person flagged before they left', which is the only way we ever find out whether the system works. And we can't answer 'what was headcount last March'.

So: an employee table with facts that don't change — hire_date, not tenure_months, because tenure is derived and storing it makes it wrong tomorrow. Then employee_snapshot at a grain of one row per employee per month, holding the mutable attributes. Then risk_score per snapshot per model run, so a score is always attributable to a version of the model and a version of the person.

That last part matters for the explanation requirement too — the factors get stored with the score, so the explanation is a record rather than something recomputed later against different data."

Data model fourth, and the grain decision made explicitly with the consequence named. The hire_date vs tenure_months point and "was this person flagged before they left" are both senior-level observations.

Tying the score versioning back to the explanation requirement they surfaced themselves is very good.
Int

"Good. Draw me the components."

Now the boxes — earned, and they will take four minutes rather than forty.
You

[draws] "Next.js frontend, Nest API owning authorisation and employee data, Postgres. A nightly job that reads snapshots, calls a stateless scoring service, and writes scores. The scoring service knows nothing about users — features in, score and factors out.

The one thing I'd flag: the manager's view needs row scoping, and I'd put that in the query rather than a guard, so there's no code path that returns someone outside their team. And field scoping separately — a manager seeing a risk score is different from a manager seeing the salary that fed it."

Boxes plus what each owns, and authorisation raised unprompted in a design round. Very few candidates bring up authorisation without being asked.
Int

"What breaks?"

Step seven, and the highest-scoring section. Most candidates have nothing here.
You

"Going through the arrows. Scoring service down during the nightly run: I'd serve the previous score with a staleness flag rather than showing nothing, because a slightly old risk score is useful and a broken page isn't. Job crashes halfway: it needs to be idempotent and restartable, keyed on the run and the employee, so a retry overwrites rather than duplicating.

The failure I'd actually worry about most isn't availability though — it's feature misalignment. If the model was trained on features in a particular meaning and the pipeline sends a slightly different set, we get plausible wrong scores and nobody notices. That's much worse than an outage, because an outage tells you about itself. So the model artefact should declare its expected feature schema and the service should refuse a mismatch loudly."

Went through arrows systematically, then named the silent-wrongness failure as the one that matters most. That reprioritisation — a correctness failure above an availability failure — is the most senior thing said in the round.
Int

"At a hundred thousand employees?"

Step eight. Also checking they will not now propose rebuilding everything.
You

"The nightly run stops fitting in its window first. I'd partition by department and parallelise the scoring, and batch the calls rather than one per employee. Probably a day's work.

I wouldn't build any of it now. It costs nothing to defer and the design doesn't have to change to accommodate it later — the job is already a job, the service is already stateless. If I were designing for a hundred thousand from the start I'd have the same diagram."

Named the first bottleneck, the fix, the cost, and then explicitly declined to build it — with the reason that the design already accommodates it. That final sentence is the correct answer to a scaling question and it is rare.
The four moments that carried this round

The headings, written before anything else. Sixty seconds, and from then on the round had a structure the interviewer could follow.

The arithmetic. "Four hundred thousand values a night is nothing" reframed the entire problem and prevented the over-engineering that loses this round most often.

The data model before the boxes. The grain decision — one row per employee per month — is the actual engineering in this design, and doing it fourth instead of last is what left time for it.

Prioritising a correctness failure over an availability failure. Anyone can say "add a retry". Saying "the thing I'd worry about is silently wrong numbers, because an outage tells you about itself" is a different level of thinking about systems.

Tactics under pressure

What to actually say in the eight moments where interviews go wrong. Memorise the shapes; you will not compose them under stress.

The eight sentences
1  YOU DON'T UNDERSTAND THE QUESTION
   "Let me make sure I've got this — you're asking about X, in the case
    where Y? " ← restating is free and never reads badly

2  YOU NEED THINKING TIME
   "Give me twenty seconds to think about that."
   ← buys real silence, legitimately. Far better than filling it with noise.

3  YOU'RE STUCK, GENUINELY, FOR OVER A MINUTE
   "I'm stuck on X. Here's what I've considered: A, which fails because…,
    and B, which works but is O(n²). Which would you rather I take?"
   ← not giving up. Showing reasoning and inviting a hint, which almost
     every interviewer will give.

4  YOU REALISE MID-SENTENCE THAT YOU'RE WRONG
   "Wait — that's not right. If the list is empty this throws."
   ← catching your own error scores HIGHER than never making it. Direct
     evidence you check your own work. Never trail off and hope.

5  YOU DON'T KNOW
   "I don't know. I've used X but never below the API. If I had to reason
    about it, I'd expect Y because… — but that's inference. I'd read the
    source to confirm."
   ← four positives from one gap: honesty, a clean boundary, reasoning
     from mechanism, initiative.

6  YOU'RE BEING PUSHED ON A WEAK CLAIM
   "I should be precise about that one. What I've done is A. What I
    haven't done is B, and I'd be learning there."
   ← concede early and specifically. The alternative is holding it for
     one more question and collapsing.

7  THE INTERVIEWER SAYS SOMETHING YOU THINK IS WRONG
   "That's not what I'd expected — my understanding was X. Am I missing
    something?"
   ← never "no, you're wrong". If you're right, this gets there anyway.
     If you're wrong, you've learned something at no cost.

8  YOU WANT TO REDIRECT TO YOUR STRENGTH
   "…I haven't done that specifically. The closest thing I have is the
    query optimisation on the leave page, where the same issue came up
    as [X]." ← honest bridge, not a dodge. Never leave a weak answer as
    the last thing you said.

Whiteboard discipline

Write the headings before the content. Draw boxes only after you know what the system must do. Leave white space — you will need to add to it. And say what you are drawing as you draw it, because a silent diagram is as bad as silent code.

Remote-interview mechanics

Test the screen share before, not during. Have your editor at a readable font size. Keep your CV and a one-page numbers sheet visible off-camera. And when you share code, scroll slowly — the interviewer's stream is behind yours.

When you have already answered badly

You can come back to it: "can I revisit the caching question? I think I gave you an incomplete answer." Interviewers respect this and almost nobody does it. Better at the end of the round than never.

Reading the signal

Short follow-ups and rapid topic changes usually mean you are clearing the bar and they are sampling breadth. Repeated digging on one topic means either genuine interest or that they are trying to find the floor. Neither is bad; both mean keep going.

  • You can say the ninety-second pitch without notes, starting with now rather than 2013.

  • You have looked up their stack, and the versions, and you will ask about them.

  • Screen share tested. Editor font size readable. CV and numbers sheet off-camera.

The hour before

Do not learn anything new — it displaces what you already have. Re-read the ninety-second pitch and the eight tactical sentences, and nothing else. Have water. Being slightly under-prepared and calm beats being fully prepared and rushed, every time.

The day after

Write down every question you were asked, while you still remember. This is the single highest-value thing you can do for the next interview, and after three loops you will have a list far better targeted than any guide. Note especially the ones where you had nothing.

One last thing

Of the five things they score, exactly one is knowledge. The other four — reasoning under uncertainty, judgement, ownership and communication — are what Part 1 is about, and they are the reason two candidates with identical CVs get different offers.

Which is also the good news for you specifically. Two years and eight months of production code is not much, and you cannot manufacture more of it before Thursday. But the eight-step architecture method, the three-part trade-off sentence, the hypothesis-driven debugging story, and the ability to say "I don't know" cleanly are all available to you now, and most candidates with twice your experience have never deliberately practised any of them.
