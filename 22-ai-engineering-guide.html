The course · 3 of 4 · AI engineering

Anyone can call the API. Building something that keeps working is a different job.

A course in how to think about products built on foundation models — the judgement that decides whether your thing survives real users, real cost and real failure. Ten reasoning procedures, eleven build modules, and the data science underneath.

Part I · how to think — the largest part Balance · ~75% AI engineering, 25% data science API surface · lives in cheat sheet 1, not here

There is a specific gap this course exists to close. Following a tutorial gets you a working demo in an afternoon — a RAG chatbot, an agent that calls two tools. Then it meets real users and you discover that it is confidently wrong about 8% of the time, nobody can tell you whether last week's prompt change helped, it costs four times what you projected, and one customer got another customer's data because a retrieved document contained an instruction.

None of those are API problems. They are judgement problems, and they are what this course is about.

How to use this

Part I first, slowly. Ten reasoning procedures. They contain almost no code and they are the part that transfers when the models change — which they will, twice a year.

Then Part II as reference while you build something. It assumes the API surface from cheat sheet 1 and does not repeat it; this is the why and the when.

Do the exercises before opening the solution. Write your answer down. The gap between yours and the reveal is the lesson, and it vanishes if you read the answer first.

Part I · the largest part

How to think

Ten procedures you run in your head, in a design review, or at a whiteboard. They are what an interviewer means by "strong AI engineer" when the framework on the job description is one you have never used.

Competency 01 · runs before everything

Is this actually an AI problem?

The most valuable judgement available, and the one most often skipped because the answer is unfashionable.

The industry incentive runs one way: everyone wants to have shipped AI. So the question "should this be a model at all?" gets asked rarely and answered defensively. Asking it properly is a differentiator, and it will occasionally save a quarter of engineering time.

The procedure

  1. State the task as input → output, concretely. Not "AI-powered insights" — "given 40 fields about an employee, produce a paragraph explaining their attrition risk". If you cannot write that sentence, you do not have a specification, you have an aspiration.
  2. Ask whether a rule does it. Genuinely try. Many "AI features" are three if statements with better marketing, and the rule version is faster, free, testable and explainable. If the rule gets 80% of the value, the model has to justify the remaining 20% against real cost.
  3. Ask whether classical ML does it. If the input is tabular and you have labels, a gradient-boosted tree will usually beat an LLM — cheaper, faster, auditable, with feature importances. Module 22 is entirely this.
  4. Ask what "wrong" costs. Models are wrong sometimes. Not occasionally — structurally. If a wrong answer is unrecoverable and nothing catches it, either add the catcher or do not ship it.
  5. Split the computation from the language. This is the move that makes most AI features safe: the deterministic system computes; the model narrates. Calculate margin in SQL, and have the model write the explanation around the number. A hallucination is then a clumsy sentence rather than a wrong figure in a board pack.
  6. Name the cheapest version that would still be useful. Often it is one prompt on one screen, and the agent architecture in the design doc is speculative.
LLMs are genuinely good atLLMs are the wrong tool for
Unstructured text and images in, judgement outArithmetic, aggregation, anything auditable
Summarising, rewriting, explaining, draftingExact reproducibility — same input, same output, forever
Extraction into a schema from messy inputTabular prediction where you have labels
Classification where the categories are fuzzy or numerousClassification where a regex or a lookup works
Open-ended tasks you cannot enumerateSub-200ms latency or very high volume at low margin
Interfaces — turning intent into structured actionBeing the only guard on a destructive action
Exercise 1.1 · 20 minutes

A product manager asks for "an AI feature that tells managers which of their people are at risk of leaving, and why." Work through the six steps. What do you actually build?

Show the solution

The request contains two different problems welded together, and separating them is the whole answer.

1 · Input → output, concretely

"Which people are at risk" — input is tabular employee data, output is a ranked list with scores. "And why" — input is that score plus the contributing factors, output is a paragraph a manager can read.

2–3 · Rule? Classical ML?

The risk scoring is a classical ML problem, not an LLM problem. Tabular input, historical labels (who actually left), a need for auditability and feature importances. Gradient boosting. An LLM asked to score attrition from a table would be slower, more expensive, unauditable, and worse.

Worth checking the rule baseline first: "flagged if tenure < 12 months and no promotion and manager changed twice" may capture much of the signal. If it does, you now know the model has to beat it, and by how much.

4 · What does wrong cost?

Substantial, and asymmetric. A false positive means a manager has an awkward retention conversation with someone who was happy. A false negative means someone leaves unexpectedly. And there is a category of harm neither metric captures: this is a prediction about a person, shown to their manager. That demands a minimum accuracy bar, a review step, and a conversation with HR and legal before it ships. Not a technical decision.

5 · Split computation from language

Here is the architecture the question is really asking for:

employee data ──► GRADIENT BOOSTING ──► risk score + SHAP factors
                  (deterministic,          │
                   auditable, testable)    │
                                           ▼
                                    LLM writes the narrative
                                    ("tenure and a recent manager
                                     change are the largest
                                     contributors...")
                                           │
                                           ▼
                                    manager reads it

⭐ The model NEVER produces the score. It only turns factors
  the model produced into a sentence. So the worst LLM failure
  is a badly-worded explanation, not a wrong risk figure.

6 · The cheapest useful version

A ranked list with the top three factors per person, as bullet points, with no LLM at all. Ship that. Add the narrative layer once someone confirms managers actually want prose — often they prefer the bullets, and you have saved the entire LLM surface.

What to say to the PM

Two features, not one.

The scoring is a classical ML problem — tabular data, historical
labels, and we need to be able to explain it to HR. That's a
gradient-boosted model, and I'd want a baseline rule first so we
know whether the model earns its place.

The "why" is the LLM part, and only after we have factors from the
scoring model to hand it. That keeps the numbers auditable.

Before either: this predicts something about a person and shows it
to their manager. I'd want HR and legal in the room on what accuracy
bar we need and what the review step is. That's not a blocker, it's
just earlier than we'd normally involve them.
Competency 02 · the mental shift

Designing systems that are sometimes wrong

Every instinct from deterministic software engineering has to be re-derived.

Software engineers are trained on systems that are correct or broken. A model is neither: it is usually right, in a way that varies with input, and it fails by producing something plausible rather than by throwing. That single property invalidates a lot of ordinary practice.

Deterministic instinctWhat replaces it
"It works" / "it's broken"A distribution of quality. The question is not whether it works but what percentage, on which inputs, and how it fails on the rest.
Unit tests with exact assertionsEvals with a score. You assert on aggregate quality across a set, not on one exact output.
A bug is reproducibleThe same input can produce different output. Reproduce with a fixed input set, not one case, and expect a rate rather than a yes/no.
Fix the bug, it stays fixedFixing one case can break others. Prompt changes are global. This is why you need a regression set before you start tuning.
Errors throwFailures are silent and fluent. A hallucinated citation looks exactly like a real one. You must build the detector.
Input validation at the boundaryOutput validation too. Schema-check, fact-check, guardrail the response — the model's output is untrusted input to the next stage.
Refactor freely if tests passPrompt = behaviour. Reformatting a prompt is a behaviour change. Version it like code and re-run the eval.
The design principle that follows

Build the system so that the model being wrong is survivable. Concretely, four patterns:

Constrain the output so wrong answers are at least well-formed — a schema, an enum, a set of allowed actions. Verify what you can programmatically: does the cited document exist, does the SQL parse, is the total within range. Make it reversible — drafts a human sends, not emails the model sends. Show your work — cite sources so a user can check, which converts an invisible failure into a visible one.

Competency 03 · the discipline

Eval-first

The single practice that separates a product from a demo. Almost nobody does it early, and everybody who has shipped one wishes they had.

Here is the sequence that plays out on nearly every LLM project. You build a prompt. It looks good on the three examples you tried. You ship. Users find failures. You change the prompt. It now handles those and you have no idea what it broke. Three months in, nobody will touch the prompt because nobody can tell whether a change is an improvement.

That is not a discipline problem, it is a measurement problem, and it is entirely preventable by building a twenty-example eval set on day two.

The procedure

  1. Define what "good" means, in writing, before building. Not "helpful and accurate". Specific, checkable properties: cites only documents in the context; refuses when the answer isn't there; under 200 words; correct category from the allowed list. Most quality requirements are checkable programmatically once you state them precisely — and stating them precisely is most of the work.
  2. Collect 20–50 examples. Real transcripts if you have them; hard cases you have hit; synthesised only as a last resort, because synthesised examples encode your assumptions about what is hard, which is exactly what you cannot know yet.
  3. Split them: train / validation / test. Tune against train and validation. Score test every round and report that as the headline. Without a held-out split you will hill-climb on noise and overfit the prompt to your own examples.
  4. Grade with the cheapest method that works. Exact match → programmatic check → LLM-as-judge → human. People reach for judges far too early; most requirements are checkable.
  5. Wire it into CI. An eval you run manually is an eval you stop running. A score that appears on every pull request changes the team's behaviour permanently.
  6. Change one thing at a time and record the score. Keep the history. In two months "why is the prompt like that?" has an answer.
Trap · the eval that passes while production gets worse

Three causes, and you should know which one you have. The golden set isn't representative — synthesised, or drawn from an old traffic distribution. You tuned on the test set until it stopped being held out. Or the metric misses what users care about — you measured factual accuracy and they left because the tone was wrong.

The counter is continuous: sample real production traffic into the eval set on a schedule, and keep a test split you look at rarely and never tune against.

Exercise 3.1 · 25 minutes

You have a feature that summarises a customer support thread into three bullet points for the next agent. Design the eval. What do you check, how, and what does "good" mean?

Show the solution

The instinct is "use an LLM judge to rate summary quality 1–10". That is the expensive, noisy, hardest-to-trust option and it should be the last layer, not the first.

Layer 1 · Programmatic — free, deterministic, catches most regressions

  • Exactly three bullets. Trivially checkable and it will fail more often than you expect.
  • Length bounds per bullet — a summary that is longer than the thread is a real failure mode.
  • No hallucinated entities. Extract proper nouns, order numbers, dates, product names from the summary and assert every one appears in the source thread. This is the single highest-value automated check for any summarisation feature and it costs nothing.
  • No PII that wasn't in the source, and ideally none at all — run a detector.
  • Language matches the thread. Cheap, and it fails on multilingual traffic.

Layer 2 · Reference-based — where you have gold summaries

For 20 threads, have a support lead write the ideal summary. Then check key-fact recall: list the 3–5 facts the next agent must know (the issue, what was tried, the current state, any promise made) and assert the summary contains each. This is more useful than any similarity score — ROUGE and embedding similarity reward paraphrase, not correctness.

Layer 3 · LLM-as-judge — only for what the above cannot reach

Two things remain genuinely un-programmable: faithfulness (does the summary misrepresent what happened?) and usefulness (could an agent act on this without reading the thread?). Judge those, with a rubric, one criterion at a time, on a binary or three-point scale — and validate the judge against ~30 human labels before trusting it. If judge-human agreement is 65%, your eval is measuring the judge.

Layer 4 · The production signal that beats all of it

Log whether the agent opened the full thread anyway. That is a behavioural measure of whether the summary was sufficient, it needs no labelling, and it is closer to the business outcome than any offline score. Pair it with handling time.

What "good" means, written down

MUST (gate — fails the build)
  · exactly 3 bullets, each 8–30 words
  · every entity in the summary appears in the source thread
  · no PII beyond what the source contains
  · language matches the thread

SHOULD (tracked, alerts on regression)
  · key-fact recall ≥ 90% on the 20 gold threads
  · judged faithful ≥ 95% (judge validated at ≥ 80% human agreement)
  · judged actionable ≥ 85%

PRODUCTION
  · full-thread open rate after reading the summary — trending down
  · agent handling time — trending down

The transferable lesson: layer your evals cheapest-first. The entity-check alone catches most hallucination regressions for zero cost, and it would be skipped by anyone who started with a judge.

Competency 04 · the real skill

Context engineering

"Prompt engineering" was always a misnomer. The job is deciding what information the model sees, in what form, in what order — and the wording is the smallest part of it.

The hierarchy — in order of payoff 
1  the right CONTEXT          ← biggest win, by a wide margin.
                               most "bad output" is missing
                               information, not bad wording.
2  the right TOOLS            let it go and get what it needs
3  a constrained OUTPUT SHAPE a schema beats a plea
4  EXAMPLES for the edge cases
5  the right EFFORT level
6  rewording the instruction  ← smallest lever. most fiddled with.

When output is bad, the reflex is to rewrite the instruction. Nine times in ten the model did not misunderstand — it did not know something. It did not know the customer's tier, that the order had already been refunded, what your product calls that feature, or that the user is in Germany. No wording fixes an absence.

The procedure — debugging a bad output

  1. Read the fully rendered prompt. Not the template — the actual bytes sent, with every variable interpolated. A shocking share of prompt bugs are visible immediately here: an empty variable, a truncated document, an escaped newline, retrieved chunks that are all irrelevant.
  2. Ask: could a competent human do this task with only this context? If not, the problem is context, and no prompt tuning will fix it. This question resolves most cases.
  3. Check what got lost. Was the document truncated? Did retrieval return the wrong chunks? Did conversation history get trimmed and take the crucial turn with it?
  4. Check ordering. Instructions before or after the data matters. Long context gets less attention in the middle — put the critical instruction and the most relevant material near the ends.
  5. Only now, change the wording. And change one thing, re-run the eval, record the score.
Context decisionThe trade-off
How much to includeMore is not better. Excess context dilutes attention, costs money, and adds latency. Include what a competent human would need — no more.
Where to put itStable content first (it is the cache prefix), volatile content last. This is a cost decision and an attention decision at once.
How to delimit itExplicit markers — <document>…</document> — with a statement that the content inside is data, not instructions. Also a mild injection defence.
Summarise or include rawSummarising loses detail you cannot recover; including raw costs tokens. For conversation history, summarise old turns and keep recent ones verbatim.
What to do when it won't fitIn order: retrieve less, compress old turns, use compaction, chunk the task. Never silently truncate — that removes information without telling anyone, and the failure looks like a model failure.
Competency 05

Decomposition — pipeline or agent

Most things built as agents should be pipelines. Knowing when the extra freedom earns its cost is a level marker.

THE SPECTRUM — and the cost of each step right ONE CALL you write the prompt. deterministic control flow. │ easiest to eval, cheapest, fastest, most reliable. ▼ CHAINED CALLS you write the sequence. each step evaluable │ separately. still deterministic. ⭐ most production │ LLM value lives here and it is deeply unglamorous. ▼ ROUTED / BRANCHED you write the branches; a classifier picks one. │ still bounded. still testable. ▼ AGENT THE MODEL chooses the sequence. unbounded paths, unbounded cost, and each run is different — which is exactly what makes it hard to evaluate. ⭐ THE TEST: can you enumerate the steps in advance? If yes → pipeline. You get determinism, per-step evals, cheaper runs, and debuggability, for free. If genuinely no → agent, with iteration caps and budgets.
PatternUse forNote
ChainExtract → transform → generateEach step gets its own eval and its own model. Often the extraction step can run on Haiku while only the final generation needs Opus.
RouteClassify the request, then send it to a specialised promptA cheap classifier plus focused prompts beats one giant prompt trying to handle everything. Also easier to improve one route without touching the others.
Parallel + reduceProcess N documents, then combineLatency scales with the slowest, not the sum. The map step is often batchable at 50% cost.
Generate then critiqueQuality-critical outputA second pass reviewing the first catches real errors — but it doubles cost and latency, so measure whether it actually helps before keeping it.
AgentOpen-ended investigation, unknown number of stepsCap iterations. Set a token budget. Log every tool call. Accept that evaluation is genuinely harder.
Competency 06

Cost and latency as design constraints

Not optimisations to do later. They are architectural, and retrofitting them means rebuilding.

A demo costs nothing because you ran it eleven times. The same design at ten thousand requests a day has a monthly bill, and the levers that would have made it cheap — cache-friendly prompt structure, a routing step, batchable work — are structural choices you make at the start or not at all.

Estimate before you build — the arithmetic takes two minutes
WORK IT THROUGH, OUT LOUD, AT DESIGN TIME

  requests/day        × tokens in  × input price
                      + tokens out × output price
                      = daily cost → × 30 → monthly

  worked example, a support-summary feature:
    2,000 threads/day
    ~4,000 input tokens (thread + instructions)
    ~150 output tokens
    on Opus 5 ($5 / $25 per MTok):
      in  2,000 × 4,000  = 8M tok/day  × $5/1M  = $40/day
      out 2,000 × 150    = 0.3M        × $25/1M = $7.50/day
      ⇒ ~$47/day ≈ $1,425/month

  NOW APPLY THE LEVERS
    · the instruction block is identical every time → cache it.
      if 1,500 of the 4,000 input tokens are the stable prefix,
      caching them cuts that portion to ~0.1× ⇒ saves ~$13/day
    · nobody is waiting for a summary → BATCH IT ⇒ 50% off
    · does summarisation need Opus? measure Sonnet 5 on the eval
      ⇒ potentially 60% off the remainder

  ⇒ the same feature is plausibly $200–400/month instead of $1,425,
    and every one of those levers had to be designed in.
The framing that prevents bad optimisation

Measure cost per completed task, not per request. A cheaper model that needs three attempts, or an agent that loops twice as long, is not cheaper. This single reframing kills most of the bad cost decisions people make — particularly the reflex to downgrade the model, which frequently increases total spend by increasing retries and turns.

Competency 07 · the one that matters most

Trust boundaries

Where the model's output stops being a suggestion and starts being an action — and what must sit at that line.

The rule

Prompts are advisory. Code is enforcement.

If an agent can delete, pay, email externally, or change permissions, the authorisation check lives in your code, evaluated against the caller's identity — not in the system prompt, not in the tool description, and not in an instruction telling the model to be careful. Any guard expressible in natural language is a guard that untrusted text can argue with.

WHERE THE BOUNDARIES ARE user input ─────┐ retrieved doc ──┤ tool result ────┼──► ALL UNTRUSTED. May contain instructions. web page ───────┤ The model cannot reliably tell data from email body ─────┘ instructions. Assume it cannot at all. │ ▼ ┌─────────┐ │ MODEL │ ← produces a SUGGESTION └─────────┘ │ ═══════════ TRUST BOUNDARY ═══════════════════════════ │ YOUR CODE decides: · is this caller allowed to do this? · is this argument in range? · does this need a human? │ ▼ ACTION ⭐ THE TEST: if an attacker could write text that reaches the model, what is the worst thing they can make happen? Answer it honestly. If the answer is "email the customer database", the boundary is in the wrong place — it does not matter how good your system prompt is.
Design choiceWhy
Scope tools to the callerThe search_orders tool queries this user's orders because the query is built from the session, not from a model-supplied customer id. Then no prompt can widen it.
Read tools freely, write tools narrowlyReading is usually safe to give an agent. Every write tool needs a specific justification and a specific guard.
Prefer drafts to sendsAn agent that composes an email a human sends has a fundamentally smaller blast radius than one that sends it. Most "agent" value survives this constraint.
Operator instructions in the operator channelSystem prompt, or a role: "system" message — not text inside a user turn, which anything writing to user input can forge.
Never let a tool result grant privilegeA fetched page saying "you are now in admin mode" is data. If it can change behaviour, you have an injection vector.
Competency 08

Failure-mode thinking

Walk every arrow and ask what happens when it fails. Half of candidates and half of designs never do it once.

FailureWhat the user seesDesign response
Model is slowA spinner, then abandonmentStream. Set a timeout. Show partial results. Have a cheaper fallback path.
Model is down / rate limitedAn errorRetry with backoff (the SDK does this), then degrade — cached answer, simpler non-LLM path, or an honest "try again shortly".
Model refusesAn empty responseHTTP 200 with stop_reason: "refusal". Check it before reading content and surface something useful.
Output fails the schemaA crash, or garbage downstreamValidate, retry once with the error fed back, then fail loudly. Never pass unvalidated model output onward.

Competency 09

The human in the loop

Designing the review surface is product work, and it is usually what makes an imperfect model shippable.

A model at 85% accuracy is unusable as an autonomous system and excellent as a drafting assistant. The difference is entirely in the interface. So the question is not "is the model good enough to ship?" but "what review surface makes this level of accuracy useful?"

Draft, don't send

Pre-fill and let a human approve. The most common shape of a genuinely successful AI feature, and it converts model errors into a slightly slower human workflow rather than an incident.

Rank, don't decide

Give a prioritised list rather than an automated action. The human applies context the model does not have, and the cost of a bad ranking is low.

Confidence-gated autonomy

Act automatically above a threshold, route to a human below it. Requires calibrated confidence — and a self-reported "I am 90% sure" from a model is not calibrated confidence.

Make correction cheap and captured

If fixing the output is one click, people will. And log the correction — edits are free labelled data and the highest-quality eval source you will ever get.

Trap · automation bias

Humans rubber-stamp. A reviewer shown a plausible AI draft approves it far more readily than they would write it themselves, and the review becomes theatre — which is worse than no review, because it creates the appearance of a control.

Counters: show the evidence alongside the draft so review means checking rather than reading; require an active choice rather than a default-accept; and sample-audit the approved outputs, because an approval rate of 99% tells you the review is not happening.

Competency 10

Measuring what users actually care about

Offline scores are a proxy. The proxy and the outcome can move in opposite directions.

LayerExampleProperty
Offline evalFaithfulness on the golden setFast, cheap, runs in CI, and only as representative as the set.
Online quality signalThumbs, edit rate, regeneration rate, copy rateReal users, real distribution — but noisy and biased toward the vocal.
Behavioural signalDid they open the full thread anyway? Did they accept the draft unedited?The best of the three. No labelling needed, hard to game, closest to usefulness.
Business outcomeHandling time, resolution rate, conversion, retentionWhat you were actually hired to move. Slow, confounded, and the only one that ultimately counts.
Instrument the behavioural signal on day one

It is nearly free and almost nobody does it. Did the user accept the output unchanged, edit it, regenerate, or abandon? Those four states tell you more about quality than any offline metric, they arrive continuously, and the edits themselves become your next eval set.

The regeneration rate in particular is a superb signal — it is an unambiguous statement that the output was not good enough, given for free, by the user.

Checklist · Part I, before you move on
  • You ask "should this be a model at all?" and you can name what a rule or classical ML would do instead.
  • You separate the computation from the narration.
  • You think in distributions of quality, not works/broken.
  • You build a 20-example eval before tuning anything, with a held-out test split.
  • When output is bad you read the fully rendered prompt before rewording it.
  • You can name the test for pipeline vs agent — can you enumerate the steps?
  • You estimate monthly cost at design time, and you know which levers are structural.
  • You can say where the trust boundary is and what enforces it.
  • You walk every arrow and ask what happens when it fails.
  • You design the review surface, not just the model call.
  • You instrument accept / edit / regenerate / abandon from day one.

Module 11

The model surface

What the knobs actually do to your product, rather than what they are called.

DecisionHow to make it
Which modelStart with the most capable one. Get it working, build the eval, then measure whether a cheaper model holds quality on your specific task. Starting cheap means you never learn whether the ceiling was the model or your prompt.
Effort levelA property of the route, not a global setting. Coding and long-horizon agentic work repay high effort; classification and chat usually do not. Tune per route on real requests.
Thinking displayIf users watch generation, showing summarised reasoning turns a long silence into visible progress. If they do not, leave it off — it is billed either way, so this is purely a UX choice.
StreamingDefault to it for anything user-facing. It does not reduce total time; it changes which number the user experiences from "total" to "time to first token", and that is the one they call speed.
max_tokensGenerous. Truncated output is wasted output plus a retry. The cost of a high ceiling is zero if the model does not use it.
Batch vs liveAsk "is anyone waiting?" Bulk classification, backfills, enrichment and eval runs are all batchable at half price. Most teams never check.
The architectural decision that pays for years

Put every model call behind one internal interface. One module that owns model selection, retries, logging, token accounting and prompt versioning — and application code that calls summarise(thread) rather than the SDK directly.

The payoff: switching models is one file. Adding cost tracking is one file. Adding caching, a fallback, or an eval hook is one file. Teams that scatter messages.create across forty call sites cannot do any of those things without a refactor, and they discover it exactly when they need to move fastest.

  Stable first is what makes caching work; critical-material-near-the-
  ends is what makes long contexts work. They point the same way.
When output is wrongLook here, in this order
It invented somethingWas the fact in the context at all? Grounding instruction present? Is "I don't know" permitted?
Wrong formatUse a schema instead of asking. This is not a prompting problem.
Too long / too shortState the length as a constraint and show an example at that length. Examples beat adjectives.

Trap · the prompt that grew

Every failure gets a new instruction appended. Six months later the system prompt is 2,000 words of accumulated patches, several contradicting each other, and nobody knows which lines still matter — so nobody dares delete any.

The fix is the eval. With a regression set you can delete lines and see whether the score moves. Prompts need pruning as much as code does, and without a measurement you can only ever add.

Module 13

Structure & tool design

Schema over instruction

"Reply with JSON like this" is a request. A schema is a constraint. Whenever the shape matters, use structured output — you remove an entire class of parsing failure rather than mitigating it.

Enums over free text

If there are seven valid categories, make the field an enum. The model then cannot invent an eighth, and you never write normalisation code for "Urgent", "urgent" and "URGENT!".

The tool description is a prompt

It is how the model decides whether to call it. Vague descriptions cause wrong tool choice. Write them like documentation for a colleague — what it does, when to use it, when not to.

Fewer tools, clearly separated

If two tools could plausibly serve the same request, the model will sometimes pick wrong — and that is a documentation bug, not a model failure. Merge them or sharpen the descriptions.

Tool results are context

A tool returning 400 lines of JSON burns context and buries the signal. Return what the model needs to decide the next step, not everything the API gave you.

Errors are information

Return a tool error as a result the model can read and react to — "no customer with that id" — rather than throwing. The model can then correct itself, which is the whole point of the loop.

Module 14

RAG end to end

The mechanics are in cheat sheet 1. This is how to build it so it can be debugged.

Build the measurement before the pipeline

The reason most RAG systems are hard to improve is that "the answer was wrong" has four possible causes and no way to tell them apart. Instrument the split from day one:

① COVERAGE      is the answer in the corpus AT ALL?
                 → if no, this is a content problem. No amount of
                   retrieval tuning will help.
② RECALL@k      was the right chunk in the retrieved set?
                 → if no, fix chunking, embeddings, or hybrid search.
③ PRECISION     did it survive reranking into the final context?
                 → if no, fix the reranker or k.
④ FAITHFULNESS  given the right chunk, did the model use it?
                 → if no, fix the prompt or the grounding instruction.

Build 30 question/answer pairs where you know which document holds the answer. Then each of the four is a number you can watch. Without this split, RAG debugging is guesswork — and teams spend weeks tuning chunk size when the document was never ingested.

The decisions, ranked by how much they matter

  1. Do you need RAG at all? Context windows are 1M tokens. If the corpus fits, put it in the prompt and cache it — no vector store, no chunking, no retrieval bugs, and cache reads are ~0.1× input. RAG earns its complexity when the corpus far exceeds the window, changes constantly, or needs per-user access filtering.
  2. Chunking, and specifically what you attach to each chunk. The highest-value cheap win in RAG: prepend the document title and section heading to every chunk. A chunk that reads "…must be refrigerated at 2–8 °C" is useless without knowing which product it describes.
  3. Hybrid search, if your corpus contains identifiers. Product codes, error codes, names and part numbers are semantically meaningless — pure vector search misses exact matches. If users search by identifier, hybrid is not an optimisation, it is a requirement.
  4. Reranking. Retrieve ~50, rerank, keep ~5. Usually the largest single accuracy gain available for one extra call, and frequently skipped.
  5. Access control at retrieval time. Filter by permission in the query, not after. And know whether your store pre-filters or post-filters, because post-filtering can return nothing when matching documents exist further down the ranking.
  6. Citations, and verifying them. Require the model to cite chunk ids, then check programmatically that each cited id was actually in the context. An unverifiable citation is the classic hallucination tell, and this check is a dozen lines.
Exercise 14.1 · 25 minutes

Your RAG assistant over internal policy documents gets a complaint: "I asked about parental leave in Germany and it told me the UK policy." Walk through the diagnosis.

Show the solution

Do not touch the prompt yet. Walk the four stages in order — the answer is almost always at one specific stage, and each has a different fix.

① Coverage — is there a German parental leave document?

Search the corpus directly, not through the vector store. Surprisingly often the answer is no: the document exists in a different system, was never ingested, or failed ingestion silently. If coverage is the problem, everything downstream is irrelevant — and the fix is content, not engineering.

② Recall — was the German chunk retrieved at all?

Log the retrieved chunk ids for that query and look. Three common causes here:

  • The chunk lost its country. If the document is "Parental Leave Policy" with a "Germany" section heading, and chunking split mid-section, the chunk text may never say "Germany" — so it does not match a query about Germany. This is the most likely cause, and the fix is prepending title + heading to every chunk.
  • Country should be metadata, not text. "German parental leave" is a filter plus a similarity question. Extract country at ingestion and filter on it.
  • The UK document dominates semantically — longer, more detailed, more overlapping vocabulary.

③ Precision — was it retrieved but ranked out?

If the German chunk was in the top 50 and did not survive reranking into the top 5, that is a reranker problem — or you have no reranker, which is itself the finding.

④ Faithfulness — was it in the context and ignored?

If the German chunk was in the final context and the model answered with UK policy, now it is a prompt problem. Likely causes: no instruction to prefer the most specific match, no instruction to state which jurisdiction it is answering for, or contradictory chunks with nothing telling the model how to choose.

The fixes, in the order I would apply them

  1. Prepend document title and section heading to every chunk. Cheap, fixes a whole class of this, and requires re-indexing but no design change.
  2. Extract jurisdiction as metadata and filter on it when the query names a country. This turns a similarity problem into a filter, which is what it always was.
  3. Make the model state the jurisdiction in its answer — "Under the German policy…". That converts a silent wrong answer into one the user can immediately catch, which is worth more than it sounds.
  4. Add this exact question to the eval set with the correct chunk id, so the fix is verified and the regression is caught.

The transferable lesson: the complaint sounded like a model failure and was almost certainly a chunking failure. Four stages, four different fixes — and the instinct to rewrite the prompt would have addressed the one stage that was probably working.

Module 15

Agents

Before you build one

Agents are the most over-applied pattern in AI engineering. They are slower, more expensive, less predictable and much harder to evaluate than a pipeline — and most tasks people build agents for can be enumerated in advance, which means they are pipelines.

The test from competency 05: can you list the steps? If yes, write them down and get determinism for free.

What makes an agent operableWhy
An iteration capNon-negotiable. An unbounded loop with a tool that keeps returning results is an unbounded bill and an unbounded wait.
A token budgetDistinct from max_tokens: a task budget tells the model the ceiling so it paces itself and finishes gracefully rather than being cut off mid-task.
A full traceEvery tool call, argument and result, keyed to a run id. Debugging an agent without the trace is impossible — you cannot reproduce the path.
A context strategyTool results accumulate forever. Decide up front: clear old results (context editing), summarise them (compaction), or persist outside the window (memory).
Scoped toolsBuilt from the caller's session, not from model-supplied identifiers. See competency 07.
A stopping condition the model understands"Stop when you have the answer" is vague. Give it an explicit completion tool or a defined output shape so finishing is a positive action rather than running out.
Evaluating an agent — harder, and different

You cannot assert on the exact path, because a valid agent takes different routes to the same answer. So evaluate three things separately: outcome (did it get there — the only one that ultimately matters), efficiency (how many steps and tokens, tracked as a distribution rather than a mean), and safety (did it call anything it should not have — assert on tool calls that must never appear).

And log the failures with full traces. Agent failure modes are specific and repetitive — looping between two tools, calling the same read three times, giving up early — and you only see the pattern with traces in front of you.

Module 16

Evals in depth

The layered eval — cheapest first, always
LAYER 1 · PROGRAMMATIC       free · deterministic · runs in CI
  schema valid? · length in range? · required fields present?
  every cited id was in the context? · no PII? · language matches?
  every named entity appears in the source?    ⭐ the single best
    automated hallucination check for summarisation and extraction

LAYER 2 · REFERENCE-BASED    cheap · needs gold answers
  exact / fuzzy match for classification and extraction
  KEY-FACT RECALL for generation: list the 3–5 facts that must appear,
  assert each. More useful than any text-similarity score, which
  rewards paraphrase rather than correctness.

LAYER 3 · LLM-AS-JUDGE       costs a call · MUST BE VALIDATED
  only for what layers 1–2 genuinely cannot reach: faithfulness,
  tone, usefulness.
  · give it a RUBRIC with separate criteria, not "is this good?"
  · ask for a score AND A REASON — the reason is how you debug it
  · BINARY or 3-point. 1–10 scales are noise; no two runs agree on 7.
  · validate against ~30 human labels and report the agreement rate.
    below ~80% agreement your eval is measuring the judge.
  · randomise A/B order — judges favour the first option
  · never judge with the prompt that generated the output

LAYER 4 · HUMAN              expensive · the calibration for everything
  sample it. don't skip it entirely.

LAYER 5 · PRODUCTION         the real answer
  accept / edit / regenerate / abandon rates. behavioural, continuous,
  unfakeable, and closest to the outcome you were hired to move.
The eval that earns its keep in CI

Thirty examples, layer-1 checks as a gate (the build fails), layer-2 and layer-3 as tracked scores with an alert on regression. Runs in about a minute, costs pennies, and changes the team's behaviour permanently — because a prompt change now shows a number on the pull request.

An eval you run manually is an eval you stop running.

Module 17

Cost engineering

The lever order is in cheat sheet 1. What belongs here is the discipline: cost problems are almost always measurement problems first.

The procedure when someone says "the bill is too high"
1  GET THE TOKEN PROFILE FIRST. Which route, which model, input vs
   output vs cached, per feature. Without this every conversation is
   speculation and every fix is a guess.
   → if you cannot answer "which feature is the bill?", that is the
     first thing to fix, not the cost.

2  FREE WINS BEFORE TRADE-OFFS
   caching → input hygiene → output hygiene → loop hygiene → batch
   ⭐ caching is almost always the largest single win, and it costs
     no quality at all.

3  THEN TRADE-OFFS, MEASURED ONE AT A TIME
   effort → model choice → cascade (last, because caches are
   model-scoped and a cascade forfeits reuse across its models)

4  JUDGE COST PER COMPLETED TASK
   a cheaper call that needs three retries is not cheaper.

5  RE-RUN THE EVAL AFTER EVERY CHANGE
   a cost saving that silently degrades quality is a quality
   regression you chose to pay for.
SymptomUsual cause
Cache hit rate near zeroA silent invalidator — a timestamp, a UUID, an unsorted serialisation, or a per-user tool set in the prefix.
Input tokens growing over timeConversation history never trimmed, or retrieval returning more chunks than it used to.
Output tokens far above expectationNo length constraint. "Be concise" is weaker than an example at the target length, and weaker still than a schema.
Spend spikes on some daysAn agent loop with no cap, a retry storm, or one user pasting very large inputs.
Cost per task rising while cost per call fallsYou downgraded the model and it now needs more turns. The classic false economy.

Module 19

Safety

The threat model in one paragraph

Anything that reaches the model can carry instructions, and the model cannot reliably distinguish data from instructions. A retrieved document, a fetched web page, an email body, a filename, a tool result, a PDF — all untrusted. So the question is never "how do I stop injection?" but "what is the worst thing an attacker could make happen, and is that acceptable?"

LayerWhat it does
Input guardrailsLength caps, content filters, PII redaction before the call. Cheap, and they bound the damage rather than preventing it.
Delimiting & framingExplicit markers around untrusted content plus a statement that it is data. Real but partial — treat it as friction, not a control.
The operator channelInstructions in system or a role: "system" message, which user text cannot forge. Structurally better than embedding operator text in a user turn.
Output guardrailsSchema validation, PII detection, citation verification, a policy check on anything before it is shown or sent.
⭐ Authorisation in codeThe only real control. Tools scoped to the caller, permissions checked against the session, destructive actions gated. Everything above is defence in depth; this is the boundary.
Blast-radius designDrafts instead of sends. Read tools freely, write tools narrowly. Reversible actions preferred. This is what makes the residual risk acceptable.

Module 21

Fine-tuning

Covered in depth in cheat sheet 1. Here: the judgement about whether to start.

The three questions that settle it

1 · Can a strong prompt get it right sometimes? If not, fine-tuning will not fix it either — fine-tuning makes a model do reliably what it can already do occasionally. It does not add capability.

2 · Is this a knowledge problem or a behaviour problem? Knowledge → RAG, always. Facts baked into weights are uncitable, stale on change, and unverifiable. Behaviour — style, format, domain conventions — is what fine-tuning is genuinely for.

3 · Is the volume high enough that the saving is real? The engineering cost is weeks, the data cost dominates, and the base model will improve underneath you — prompt-based systems inherit that for free, fine-tuned ones do not.

Part III

The data science underneath

Two modules. An AI engineer who cannot recognise a classical ML problem will build an LLM solution to it — and that is one of the more expensive mistakes available.

Module 22

When classical ML wins

SignalWhy it points away from an LLM
The input is a tableGradient boosting handles mixed types, missing values and non-linearity natively, and beats both LLMs and neural networks on tabular data more often than not.
You have historical labelsThen you can train and measure. That is strictly more reliable than prompting a general model to guess.
The output is a number or a classNot language. If nothing needs to be written, the language model's core capability is unused.
It must be auditableFeature importances and coefficients are explainable to a regulator. "The model said so" is not.
High volume, low marginA trained model costs microseconds per prediction. An LLM call costs cents and hundreds of milliseconds.
Same input must give same outputDeterministic. LLM output is not guaranteed identical even at fixed settings.
The hybrid that is usually right

Classical model computes; LLM communicates. The boosted model produces the score and the contributing factors; the LLM turns those factors into a paragraph a human reads. You get auditable numbers and natural explanation, and the worst LLM failure is a clumsy sentence rather than a wrong figure.

This pattern covers a very large share of real "AI features" in business software, and recognising it is a strong signal in a design conversation.

Module 23

The fundamentals you still need

Five ideas from data science that apply directly to LLM systems, and that AI engineers routinely rediscover the hard way.

ConceptHow it shows up in AI engineering
Leakage Your eval set contains examples that were in the few-shot prompt. Or your golden answers were written by looking at the model's output. Both make the score meaningless in exactly the way a leaked feature does.
Train/test discipline Tuning a prompt against the same set you report on is overfitting. Hold out a test split, score it every round, and never tune against it.
Base rates A classifier or guardrail flagging a rare event will produce mostly false positives — the false-positive paradox applies identically to an LLM-based detector. Report precision, and tell the stakeholder before they act on the list.
Distribution shift Your prompt was tuned on last quarter's queries. Users changed. Nothing errors, quality decays. This is drift, and the counter is sampling production traffic into the eval set continuously.
The baseline Before claiming the LLM helps, know what the simple version scores — the keyword search, the rule, the template. A surprising number of LLM features do not beat a well-written template, and nobody checked.

The curriculum

Eight weeks, built around shipping one thing properly rather than reading about eleven.

WeekFocusBuild this — not just read it
1Part I, and the API surfaceRead Part I. Then make ten calls: system prompts, structured output, streaming. Deliberately trigger a 400 by sending budget_tokens so the drift becomes muscle memory.
2One real feature, simplest tierPick something small and genuine — summarise, classify, extract. One call. Ship it behind a flag. Resist the agent.
3EvalsTwenty examples for that feature. Layer-1 programmatic checks. Wire it into CI. Then improve the prompt and watch the number move. This is the week that changes how you work.
4Cost & cachingInstrument all four token counts per call. Restructure the prompt for caching and verify cache_read_input_tokens is non-zero. Compute the monthly bill at 10× volume.
5RetrievalBuild RAG over a corpus you know well. Build the four-stage measurement first — coverage, recall, precision, faithfulness — then tune. Add hybrid search and a reranker and measure each.
6Tools & one agentTwo tools with the Tool Runner. Then a small agent with an iteration cap and a full trace. Break it deliberately: make a tool fail, make it loop, hit pause_turn.
7Safety & opsTry to prompt-inject your own system through a retrieved document. Fix it at the authorisation layer, not the prompt. Add tracing, then a dashboard with spend, latency, refusal rate and cache hit rate.
8JudgementTake something you built and write the honest post-mortem: what would break at 100× traffic, what a hostile user could do, what you cannot measure, what you would remove.

Portfolio: show the measurement

An eval harness with a documented golden set, a before/after score, and a written analysis of the failure modes beats a slicker demo. Almost nobody shows this, and it is exactly what distinguishes someone who has shipped from someone who has followed a tutorial.

Portfolio: show the cost

"This costs $0.004 per request, $240/month at current volume, and here are the three levers I used to get it there from $900." Real engineering, and rarer than it should be.

Portfolio: show a failure

A documented case where the model was confidently wrong, why, and what you changed in the system so it is caught next time. Judgement is more persuasive than polish.

Don't collect frameworks

The orchestration library will change. The judgement in Part I will not. Tooling anxiety consumes the time that should go into evals, retrieval quality and cost — which is what the work actually is.

One last thing

Of the twenty-five modules here, four are about the API and one is about fine-tuning. The rest are about deciding what to build, proving it works, keeping it affordable, and making it safe when something hostile reaches it. That ratio is the job.

The models will change — twice a year, in ways that invalidate specific paragraphs of cheat sheet 1. What will not change is that somebody has to decide whether this should be a model at all, build the measurement before the feature, put the authorisation in code, and design the surface where a human catches what the model got wrong.
