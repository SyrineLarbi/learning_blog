The course · 4 of 5 · data science

A model that scores 0.91 offline is worth nothing. Only the one that survives contact counts.

A course in the judgement that decides whether a data science project produces value or a notebook nobody opens again — framing, leakage, validation design, metric choice, error analysis, and knowing when the honest answer is "don't model this".

Part I · how to think — the largest part Bias · toward what breaks after the notebook Formulas · in cheat sheet 2, not here

Data science education optimises for the middle of the pipeline. You learn algorithms, tuning, and metrics — the part with clean datasets and a leaderboard. Then you join a company and discover that the algorithm was never the hard part.

The hard parts are: nobody agreed what you were predicting; the data that would answer it was never recorded; your excellent offline score came from a feature that will not exist at prediction time; the metric you optimised is not what the business pays for; and after all of it, the model sits in a notebook because nobody designed how it would be used.

How to use this

Part I first. Ten reasoning procedures. Almost no maths — they are what makes the difference between a project that ships and one that quietly dies, and they transfer across every algorithm you will ever use.

Part II is the project lifecycle, in order, with the decision at each stage. Part III covers the three specialisms most people meet. Formulas and metric definitions live in cheat sheet 2 and are not repeated here.

Do the exercises before opening the solution. The gap between your answer and the reveal is the lesson.

Part I · the largest part

How to think

Ten procedures. They are what a senior data scientist does differently — and almost none of them involve choosing an algorithm.

Competency 01 · before any code

Framing

Turning "can you predict churn?" into something that can be built, measured and acted on.

The six questions — none of them optional
1  WHAT DECISION does this change, and who makes it?
     "we want to understand churn" is not a decision.
     "we'll offer a retention discount to the top 200 by risk each
      month" is — and it tells you the model needs to RANK, that
      only the top 200 matter, and that precision@200 is the metric.
     ⭐ NO DECISION → it's a curiosity. Log it, don't plan it.

2  WHAT IS THE UNIT of prediction?
     customer? customer-month? subscription? household?
     This is the GRAIN. Get it wrong and every downstream number
     is wrong in a way that is hard to see.

3  WHAT EXACTLY IS THE TARGET?
     "churn" is not a target. A target is:
       "no purchase in the 90 days following a 30-day observation
        window, for customers with ≥1 prior purchase"
     Every clause in that sentence is a decision someone must make.

4  WHEN is it predicted, and WHAT IS KNOWABLE THEN?
     ⭐ THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION IN THIS LIST.
     It defines the feature set and it prevents most leakage.

5  WHAT IS THE BASELINE?
     the current rule · the majority class · last period's value ·
     what a human does today.
     Without it you cannot say the model helps.

6  WHAT DOES EACH ERROR COST?
     false positive vs false negative, in money or harm.
     This — not convention — sets the metric and the threshold.
Trap · accepting the question as asked

"Can you predict which customers will churn?" is usually a proxy for "how do we keep more customers?" Those need different work. A perfect churn predictor that identifies people who are leaving because they moved country is accurate and worthless — nothing can be done.

The better question: which customers are both at risk and saveable? That reframing changes the target, the features and the evaluation, and it is the difference between a model that gets used and one that gets admired once.

Competency 02

Predict or explain — they are different jobs

PredictionExplanation / causation
Question"What will happen?""What happens if we act?"
Cares aboutAccuracy on unseen dataUnbiased estimation of one effect
Correlated featuresFine — use them allA problem — they confound the estimate
Best methodWhatever scores best. Black boxes welcome.Design first (experiment, quasi-experiment), model second
Feature importanceTells you what predictsDoes not tell you what causes. Ever.
The error that gets acted on

A churn model finds that contacting support is associated with lower churn. A well-meaning stakeholder concludes: encourage more support contacts.

But the arrow may run the other way — customers who were staying anyway are the ones invested enough to ask for help. Driving up contact volume will not retain anyone, and it will cost you a support team.

The habit: when a stakeholder starts a sentence with "so if we increase…", stop them. That is a causal claim about a predictive model, and your job at that moment is to say so — clearly, without being tiresome about it, and with the design that would answer it (a holdout, a staggered rollout) and what it would cost.

Competency 03

Thinking in uncertainty

Every number you produce is an estimate. Presenting estimates as facts is the most common way analysts and data scientists mislead people without lying.

A point estimate is half an answer

"AUC 0.84" means nothing without the spread. Report the variation across CV folds. If model A scores 0.84 ± 0.03 and model B scores 0.85 ± 0.04, you have not shown B is better — and picking B is a coin flip dressed as a decision.

Distributions before summaries

Business quantities are right-skewed. The mean describes nobody. Look at the shape before you choose what to report — and if it is bimodal, any single summary is actively misleading.

Small segments are noise

Slice enough and something looks dramatic. Set a minimum cell size before you start slicing, and when a small segment looks alarming, read the rows — with 300 cases you can.

Common cause vs special cause

A metric bouncing inside its usual range is the process being itself, not a signal. Reacting to it makes things worse and manufactures false attributions. Plot 30+ points before you call anything a change.

The sentence that separates levels

"The model scores 0.84, with fold-to-fold variation of about 0.03. That's meaningfully better than the current rule at 0.71, and I would not read anything into the difference between this and the 0.85 variant — that's inside the noise."

Confidence about what you know, and equal confidence about what you do not, is the whole of scientific communication.

Competency 04 · the highest-value habit

The prediction-time test

One question, asked of every feature, prevents most of the expensive failures in applied machine learning.

The question

"At the moment this prediction is made, would this value exist — and would it have this value?"

Both halves matter. A field may exist and be empty. A field may exist and be updated later, so the value in your training table is the post-outcome value.

FeatureFails the test because
cancellation_reasonOnly populated after cancelling. A perfect predictor of the thing that caused it.
days_since_last_loginIf churn is defined as inactivity, this is the target wearing a different name.
total_lifetime_ordersComputed over the whole history including after the prediction date. Must be computed as-at.
account_statusOverwritten in place. Your training snapshot holds today's status, not the status at prediction time.
support_tickets (count)Fine if counted up to the cut-off. Leakage if counted over the full window.
salesperson_notesOften written after the outcome is known, and sometimes literally say it.
The structural fix: build features as-at

Do not build a feature table from current state and hope. Pick a cut-off date per row and compute every feature using only data before it. It is more work, it is the only way to be sure, and it is what a point-in-time feature store exists to make routine.

And the diagnostic that catches the rest: a suspiciously good score is a leakage alarm, not a success. AUC 0.99 on a real business problem, or one feature dominating importance, means go and look for the leak. Distrusting your own good result is the most valuable instinct in modelling.

Competency 05

Designing the split to match reality

Your validation scheme is a simulation of deployment. If it does not resemble how the model will actually be used, the score is fiction.

Match the split to the deployment
HOW IT WILL BE USED               HOW TO VALIDATE

predict next month from this   →  TIME SPLIT. train on the past,
month, every month                test on the future, rolling forward.
                                  ⚠ never shuffle.

predict for NEW customers      →  GROUP SPLIT on customer id.
we've never seen                  otherwise the model memorises
                                  customers, not behaviour.

predict for a new REGION /     →  hold out an entire region / store /
store / factory                   site. a random split massively
                                  overstates how well it generalises.

same population, no time       →  STRATIFIED K-FOLD.
dimension, i.i.d. rows            the classic default — and rarer in
                                  business than textbooks suggest.

⭐ THE TEST: if the split does not resemble deployment, the score is
  a number about a situation that will never occur.

AND THE COMPOUND CASE, which is common and usually missed:
  a customer-month table for a monthly-run model needs BOTH a time
  split AND grouping by customer — the same customer must not appear
  on both sides even within the training period, or the model learns
  individuals rather than patterns.
Trap · the test set that stopped being one

You tune hyperparameters against the test set, try five feature sets against it, and pick the best. It is now a validation set, and your reported number is optimistic by an unknown amount.

Three splits. Train to fit, validation to choose, test touched once at the end. If you must iterate more, use nested cross-validation — and if you have already peeked, say so when you report, because an honest caveat is worth more than a number nobody can trust.

Competency 06

Metrics from costs, not convention

The default metric is whatever the library prints. That is a decision made by accident. The metric should fall out of what each kind of error costs, and stating that connection out loud is one of the clearest senior signals available.

Deriving the metric from the decision
ASK      what does a FALSE POSITIVE cost?
         what does a FALSE NEGATIVE cost?
         how many can we act on at all?

THEN     the metric follows.

  "we can contact 200 people a month"
      → PRECISION@200. Nothing else matters — recall at 5,000 is
        irrelevant because you will never call 5,000.

  "missing a fraud case costs €4,000, reviewing one costs €5"
      → cost ratio 800:1 → optimise RECALL, tolerate false
        positives, and set the threshold from the ratio.

  "we act on everything above the threshold, automatically"
      → CALIBRATION matters as much as ranking. A score of 0.7 must
        mean 70%, or the expected-value maths is wrong.

  "it's a ranked feed"
      → NDCG or precision@k. Position matters and accuracy cannot
        express that.

  "the classes are 95/5"
      → NEVER accuracy. PR-AUC, and report the base rate alongside
        it, because PR-AUC's floor is the positive rate.

⭐ THEN CONVERT THE SCORE TO MONEY, ONCE.
  "at this threshold we flag 340 accounts a month, about 110 are
   real, catching €440k of fraud for 230 wasted reviews."
  That sentence is what makes the model fundable — and no AUC
  ever did that.
Competency 07

Baseline discipline

Build the baseline before the model. Always.

It costs an hour and it does four things nothing else does: it tells you whether there is signal at all, it gives every later number meaning, it sometimes ends the project early and correctly, and it protects you from shipping something that is worse than what exists.

BaselineUse
The current ruleWhatever the business does today. The one that actually matters — you must beat this or there is no reason to switch.
Majority class / meanThe floor. Tells you what "accuracy" means on this data — often uncomfortably high.
Last value / seasonal naiveFor forecasting. Surprisingly hard to beat, and many published forecasting wins do not.
Logistic / linear regressionThe modelling baseline. If boosting only beats it by a point, take the interpretable one.
A humanWhere a person does the task today, sample their accuracy. It sets the realistic ceiling — and sometimes reveals the task is genuinely ambiguous.
Competency 08 · the underused one

Error analysis

Look at what the model got wrong, individually, with your eyes. It is the highest value-per-hour activity in modelling and the one people skip to go tune hyperparameters.

The procedure — an afternoon, and it usually beats a week of tuning
1  PULL 50 ERRORS. Not aggregate metrics — the actual rows.
     stratify: the confident-wrong, the near-threshold, the
     biggest-loss cases. Confident-wrong first; they are the most
     informative and the most dangerous.

2  READ THEM. One at a time. Note what you see.

3  CATEGORISE. Almost always they cluster into 3–6 groups:
     · a data quality problem (a field is null for this segment)
     · a label problem (the "wrong" answer is arguably right —
       ⭐ very common, and it caps your achievable score)
     · a missing feature (the signal exists but you didn't give it)
     · a genuinely hard case (irreducible; accept it)
     · a segment the model never saw enough of

4  COUNT EACH GROUP and estimate the ceiling.
     "40% of errors are one label problem" → fixing labels beats any
     amount of tuning, and you now know by how much.

5  FIX THE BIGGEST GROUP. Then re-run.

⭐ WHY THIS BEATS TUNING: hyperparameter search moves a metric by a
  point or two. Discovering that a fifth of your labels are wrong,
  or that one segment has a systematically missing field, changes
  what is possible.
Exercise 8.1 · 25 minutes

Your churn model has AUC 0.83. You pull 50 errors and find: 18 are customers who "churned" because their company was acquired; 12 have almost no activity data at all; 9 are cases where the label date looks wrong; 11 look genuinely hard. What do you do, in what order?

Show the solution

Notice first that 39 of 50 errors are not modelling failures. Tuning would address none of them. That is the normal result of error analysis and it is why the exercise exists.

1 · The 18 acquisitions — a framing problem, and the biggest finding

These customers did not churn in any sense the business can act on. They ceased to exist. No retention offer would have helped, and including them in training teaches the model to predict something unpreventable — which pollutes the score and, worse, will put unsaveable accounts at the top of the action list.

Action: go back to the target definition. Either exclude acquisitions as a distinct outcome, or split into "voluntary" and "involuntary" churn and model the voluntary one — which is what competency 01's "at risk and saveable" reframing was pointing at. This is a conversation with the business, not a code change, and it is the most valuable thing in the list.

2 · The 9 label-date problems — fix before anything else

If the churn date is wrong, some rows are labelled churned when they were still active, and your observation windows are misaligned. Label noise caps your achievable performance and no model can exceed it. Nine in fifty extrapolates to ~18% of errors — worth an hour with whoever owns the source to find out whether it is systematic (a timezone or a batch job) or random.

3 · The 12 with almost no activity data — a coverage problem

Two questions before deciding. Who are they? New customers with genuinely short histories, or an integration that stopped writing for a segment? If the second, it is a pipeline bug and fixing it is worth more than the model.

If genuinely new: they are a different population and probably need separate handling — a simple rule for cold-start customers and the model for those with history. Forcing one model to serve both makes it worse at each. And check whether missingness correlates with the outcome, because if it does you have MNAR and imputation will bury the bias.

4 · The 11 genuinely hard — leave them

Irreducible error. Their value is that they tell you roughly where the ceiling is: if 22% of errors are irreducible, you now have a realistic target rather than an aspiration to reach 0.95.

The order, and what to say

  1. Target definition (the 18) — a conversation, biggest impact, no code.
  2. Label quality (the 9) — caps everything else.
  3. Data coverage (the 12) — may be a pipeline bug worth fixing regardless.
  4. Then, and only then, consider features or tuning.
Before I tune anything: I looked at 50 errors and most of them
aren't model problems.

The biggest group is customers who left because they were acquired.
We can't retain those, so training on them teaches the model to
predict something we can't act on — and it'll push unsaveable
accounts to the top of the list. I'd like to split voluntary from
involuntary churn and model the voluntary one.

Second, about a fifth of the errors have a churn date that looks
wrong. That's a ceiling on any model we build, so I want to check
with data engineering whether it's systematic.

I'd expect those two to move the number more than any tuning, and
I'll have a better estimate of what's actually achievable after.
Competency 09

Interpreting without overclaiming

Do not saySay
"Tenure is the biggest driver of churn.""Tenure is the strongest predictor in the model. That doesn't tell us that changing tenure changes churn — it's association."
"The model is 87% accurate.""At the threshold we chose, of the 340 we flag each month about 110 actually churn. We miss roughly 60."
"This customer has a 73% chance of leaving."Only if the model is calibrated — otherwise 0.73 is a rank position wearing a percentage sign.
"The model says to focus on X.""Among the features we gave it, X separates the groups most. There may be causes we didn't measure."
"It'll perform like this in production.""On a held-out split resembling deployment, it scored this. I'd expect some decay — I'd want a shadow period before we act on it."
The two you will repeat for your whole career

"Feature importance is not causal effect." Stakeholders make that leap automatically and then act on it. Saying the sentence is part of the job.

"That score is on data resembling deployment, not deployment." Every offline number is a forecast about a future that has not happened.

Competency 10

When not to model

Recommending against a model is often the highest-value thing you will do in a quarter, and almost nobody is rewarded for it — so you have to volunteer it.

SituationThe better answer
No decision attachedNothing. Or a dashboard. Find the decision first.
A rule captures most of itShip the rule. Revisit if it stops being enough. It is testable, explainable and free.
The data does not existStart recording it. A half-day building a daily snapshot makes the question answerable in a year — and it is often the highest-value output of the whole project.
Labels are unreliableFix the labels. A model on noisy labels learns the noise, and the ceiling is set before you begin.
Nobody can act on the outputFind who could, or stop. A ranked list with no owner is a report nobody reads.
The base rate is minuscule and action is costlyDo the precision arithmetic first. If a great model still yields mostly false alarms, say so before building it.
It predicts something about a person, with consequencesSlow down. Involve whoever owns fairness and data protection before building, not at review.
How to say it without sounding obstructive

"I don't think this needs a model yet, and here's why — the current rule already captures most of the signal, and the thing that would actually move the number is that we don't record when someone downgrades. If I spend half a day setting up that snapshot, this becomes answerable properly in a couple of months. Want me to do that instead?"

Declining with an alternative and a date is not obstruction. It is the judgement they are paying for.

Checklist · Part I
  • You can name the decision, the unit, the exact target, the baseline and the cost of each error.
  • You distinguish predicting from explaining, and you interrupt "so if we increase…".
  • You report spread, not point estimates, and you know when a difference is inside the noise.
  • You ask the prediction-time question of every feature, and you build features as-at.
  • Your validation split resembles deployment — time, group, or region, as applicable.
  • Your metric came from the cost of errors, and you have converted the score into money once.
  • You built the baseline before the model.
  • You have read 50 errors with your own eyes and categorised them.
  • You say "predictor, not driver" without being asked.
  • You are willing to recommend not building it, with an alternative and a date.
Part II

The project

Nine stages in order, with the decision at each and the artefact it produces. Formulas live in cheat sheet 2; this is the sequence and the judgement.

Stage 11

Define the problem

Output: a one-page spec someone else could build from. Time: half a day. Skipping it costs weeks.

The project spec — write it before touching data
DECISION      after seeing this, ____ will decide whether to ____
UNIT          one row = ____
TARGET        exactly: ____ (every clause is a decision)
PREDICTED AT  ____ — and what is knowable then
POPULATION    who is in scope; who is deliberately excluded
BASELINE      current approach scores ____ on ____
METRIC        ____ , because a false positive costs ____ and a
              false negative costs ____
SUCCESS       we ship if ____ (a number you could fail)
CONSTRAINTS   latency · interpretability · regulation · refresh
              cadence · who may see the output
OUT OF SCOPE  ____
ASSUMPTIONS   each flagged [unverified] with a date it gets checked
RISKS         if assumption N is false, the fallback is ____

⭐ THE DEFINITION-OF-DONE LINE IS THE ONE PEOPLE OMIT. Modelling has
  no natural end — you can always tune more. A stated threshold lets
  you stop, and reveals early when the threshold is impossible.
Stage 12

Get and understand the data

Where projects are quietly decided. The question is not "can I load it" but "can this data answer the question at all".

  1. Inventory the sources, with grain. For each table: what one row means, row count, date range, owner, refresh cadence, and whether it is a system of record or a downstream copy. When two numbers disagree the question is never "which query is right" — it is "which of these is authoritative".
  2. Run the grain test on every table. Group by the assumed key, count, keep anything above one. It must return nothing. Five minutes, every time.
  3. Resolve the assumptions from the spec. Each one is a query, and they gate the project. Do them before anything else — discovering on day nine that the key feature was never recorded is the failure this stage exists to prevent.
  4. Ask the source owner the questions you cannot answer alone. What does one row mean? Which columns should I never trust? Are there rows in here that are not real business events? Do rows change after they are written? That last one decides whether point-in-time features are even possible.
  5. Profile before promising. Nulls by row count and by value; top 25 values of every categorical (the single highest-yield check); ranges; date bounds; orphans via a left join, never an inner one.
  6. Reconcile a total to something nobody in the argument controls. The ledger, a filing, a physical count. It is the only check that tests accuracy rather than internal consistency.
The failure this stage prevents

Discovering the analysis is impossible — not hard, impossible, because the value was never recorded. A table holding current state with no history cannot answer "what was true last March", and no index, query or model recovers it.

Finding that out on day one is competence. Finding it on day nine, with a deadline, is the characteristic way data science projects fail.

Stage 13

EDA that earns its time

Exploratory analysis expands to fill any time allowed. Give it a purpose and a budget.

AskBecause it changes what you do next
What is the target's base rate?Decides the metric, the threshold strategy, and whether imbalance handling is needed at all.
What does the target look like over time?A step change means a definition or system change. Seasonality means your split must respect it.
Which features have coverage?A feature that is 60% null for the segment you care about is not a feature yet.
Does missingness correlate with the target?If yes, missingness is itself a signal — and imputation will bury it.
Are there obvious leaks?A single feature that separates the classes almost perfectly is a leak until proven otherwise.
What do 20 rows actually look like?Read them. Whole categories of misunderstanding die here, and nothing else substitutes.
The correlation matrix trap

The reflex first move — a big correlation heatmap — is mostly decorative. It shows linear pairwise association only, tells you nothing about interactions, is dominated by scale, and invites the reader to read causation into a colour.

More useful in the same ten minutes: the target's distribution, coverage per feature, target rate by each categorical level, and twenty raw rows.

Stage 14

Features

The order that works

1 · Five obvious features from domain knowledge. Ask an operator what they would look at. Build those. This is usually 80% of the final model.

2 · Measure. Now you know the headroom, and whether more features are worth anything.

3 · Add deliberately, one group at a time, checking the score each time. Adding forty at once tells you nothing about which mattered.

One feature an operator suggests — "was this ordered after the cut-off time?" — routinely outperforms fifty generated polynomial terms. Ask the people who do the work.

RuleWhy
Compute as-at, alwaysEvery aggregate respects the prediction cut-off. This is the structural leakage fix.
Fit transformers inside the foldScalers, imputers, encoders and selectors all leak if fitted on the full dataset. Use a pipeline object so it is structurally impossible.
Target encoding needs out-of-foldThe most common accidental leak. Compute inside folds, with smoothing.
Flag every imputed valueAn imputed number that looks measured is worse than a null. The flag lets you re-run excluding them and see if the conclusion survives.
Units in the nameweight_g, duration_ms. A column called weight is an outage waiting for the day a source switches units.
Keep the feature list in codeNot in a notebook cell. It is the thing serving must reproduce exactly.
Stage 15

Train and select

THE LADDER — climb it in order, stop when the gain stops paying 0 BASELINE the current rule / majority / naive │ ← if you can't beat this, stop and say so ▼ 1 LINEAR or LOGISTIC fast, interpretable, tells you whether │ the signal exists at all ▼ 2 GRADIENT BOOSTING ⭐ where you will probably end up on │ tabular data. XGBoost / LightGBM / CatBoost ▼ 3 TUNING early stopping first, then a modest search. │ ⚠ diminishing returns arrive fast — a point │ of AUC rarely changes a decision ▼ 4 ENSEMBLE usually not worth the operational cost outside competitions ⭐ AT EVERY RUNG ASK: does this gain change the DECISION? Going from 0.83 to 0.845 almost never does. Fixing the label noise you found in error analysis usually does.
Trap · tuning as procrastination

Hyperparameter search is comfortable, measurable and feels productive. It also has the worst return per hour of anything in this list once you are past defaults with early stopping.

When you catch yourself on a third tuning run, go and do error analysis instead. Fifty rows read by hand will tell you more than a fourth search.

Stage 16

Evaluate

  1. Score on the test set, once, with the metric from the spec — and report the spread across folds, not a point estimate.
  2. Compare to the baseline explicitly. "0.84 vs the current rule's 0.71" is the sentence. A number alone means nothing.
  3. Choose the threshold from the costs, on the validation set, not the default 0.5. This is frequently the single largest improvement available and needs no retraining.
  4. Check calibration if the probability will be used as a number. Reliability diagram; fix with Platt or isotonic on held-out data. Boosted trees are often poorly calibrated out of the box.
  5. Break the score down by segment. Overall 0.84 can hide 0.91 on one group and 0.62 on another — and the weak group is often the one that matters, or the one that raises a fairness question.
  6. Do error analysis. Competency 08. Fifty rows, categorised.
  7. Convert the score into money, once. "At this threshold: 340 flagged, ~110 real, €440k caught, 230 wasted reviews." That is the sentence that gets a model funded.
Stage 17

Explain and deliver

Correct work nobody acts on has produced nothing. This stage is where most of the value is realised or lost.

The delivery structure — answer first
1  THE ANSWER            one sentence, with the number and the
                         comparison. "We can identify about a third
                         of churners a month, roughly 110 of 340
                         flagged, against 60 the current rule finds."
2  WHAT IT'S WORTH       in money or risk, at the chosen threshold
3  WHAT TO DO            the recommendation, with an owner
4  HOW IT WORKS          two sentences. not the architecture.
5  LIMITATIONS           what it can't do, who it's worse for,
                         what would invalidate it
6  APPENDIX              methodology, CV design, segment breakdown,
                         error analysis. Not presented — it exists so
                         you can answer a question in ten seconds.

⚠ NEVER LEAD WITH THE METHOD. "We tried five models and tuned with
  Bayesian search" answers a question nobody asked. The conclusion
  arrives at minute eighteen and the room has already formed a view.
ChallengeResponse
"How do I know it works?"The held-out design and the comparison to today's approach. Offer a shadow period — it converts an argument into evidence.
"Why did it flag this person?"Per-prediction contributions (SHAP). Have this ready before the meeting; it is the first question in any human-facing model.
"So if we change X, churn drops?"Stop. Predictor, not driver. Then offer the design that would answer it and what it costs.
"Can we just use it for everyone?"Segment performance. If it is materially worse for a group, that is a decision for them to make knowingly.
"It missed someone obvious."One case is not evidence. Show the aggregate — and take the case into the error analysis, because sometimes they are right about a pattern.
Stage 18

Deploy

The failure that dominates this stage

Training/serving skew. Features computed one way in the notebook and another way in production. The model receives inputs it has never seen and performs nothing like the offline score — and the discrepancy is invisible until someone checks.

Structural fixes, in order of strength: share the transformation code between training and serving (not a re-implementation); serialise the whole pipeline as one artefact, not the model alone; or use a feature store so both read the same definition. Then verify: run the same rows through both paths and diff the features.

Batch or real time?

Most business models are batch — score nightly, write to a table, let the application read it. Simpler, cheaper, easier to monitor and to reproduce. Real time only when the decision genuinely happens at request time.

Version everything together

Model, feature code, training data snapshot, and the metric it achieved — as one versioned artefact. "Which model produced this prediction" must be answerable months later.

Shadow first

Run it alongside the current approach without acting on it. Compare. The safest way to ship a model, and it costs only patience.

Log the inputs, not just the output

Store the feature vector with each prediction. Without it you cannot debug a bad prediction, detect drift, or reproduce anything.

Stage 19

Monitor and retrain

Monitor in this priority order — labels arrive last
1  IS IT RUNNING?        volume, errors, latency.
                         ⭐ the failure people forget: it stopped, and
                           nothing said so. Predictions just go stale.
2  INPUT DISTRIBUTIONS   per-feature mean, percentiles, null rate vs
                         training. drifts here precede everything and
                         are visible IMMEDIATELY.
3  PREDICTION DISTRIB.   score histogram over time. a shift with no

Part III

Specialisms

Three areas most data scientists meet, and the one thing about each that is different enough to catch you out.

Module 20

Time series

What's differentConsequence
Observations are not independentOrdinary cross-validation is invalid. Split by time, roll forward, and never shuffle.
The future must not leakRolling features look strictly backwards. A centred window sees ahead and produces a beautiful, worthless score.
Naive baselines are strongSeasonal naive (same period last year) is genuinely hard to beat. Many published forecasting wins do not.
One split is not a backtestUse a rolling origin. One split tells you about one moment; the business runs every month.
MAPE breaks near zeroUndefined at zero, explodes near it, and punishes over-prediction more than under. Use WAPE for demand.
Intermittent demand is a different problemMany zeros breaks most methods and every percentage metric. Croston's or a count model.
Module 21

Text, and the bridge to LLMs

TaskClassical approachWhen to use an LLM instead
ClassificationTF-IDF + linear model; or a fine-tuned small transformerWhen you have no labels — an LLM classifies zero-shot. With thousands of labels, the classical model is cheaper, faster and often better.
Similarity / searchBM25 keywordEmbeddings for semantic matching. Hybrid beats either alone whenever identifiers matter.
ExtractionRegex, NER modelsLLM with a schema, for messy or varied input. Regex still wins on rigid formats — and costs nothing.
SummarisationExtractive (pick sentences)LLM, essentially always. This is where they genuinely changed the field.
Topic discoveryLDA, clustering on embeddingsCluster embeddings, then have an LLM name the clusters. A good hybrid.
The decision rule

Labels and volume → classical. No labels, or open-ended output → LLM. And the hybrid that often wins: use an LLM to generate labels for a few thousand examples, then train a small fast model on them. You get the LLM's judgement at classical cost and latency — and a model you can audit.

Module 22

Deep learning in practice

The honest position

On tabular data, gradient boosting usually wins — not marginally, and not only on small data. It handles mixed types and missing values natively, trains in minutes, needs almost no tuning to be good, and gives you feature importances.

Reach for deep learning when the input has structure a network exploits — spatial, sequential, relational — or when you can start from a pre-trained model. For a CSV of 40 columns, use boosting.

Transfer learning is the practical path

Almost nobody trains from scratch. Take a pre-trained model, freeze the body, train a head — or fine-tune with adapters. "We don't have enough data for deep learning" is usually false: you need enough to adapt, not to learn vision or language from nothing.

The learning rate is the hyperparameter

If it is not learning, suspect the learning rate before architecture, data or anything else. Use a schedule with warmup and decay.

The overfit-ten-examples test

Before debugging anything else: can the model drive loss to near zero on ten examples? If not, you have a bug — in the loss, the labels, or the data pipeline — not a modelling problem.

Report across seeds

GPU non-determinism means runs differ even with a fixed seed. A single-run number is not a result. Report mean and spread over several.

The curriculum

Ten weeks, built around finishing one project properly rather than starting five.

WeekFocusDo this, not just read it
1Part IRead it. Then take a project you have done and write its one-page spec retrospectively. Notice what you cannot fill in.
2Data, honestlyTake a messy public dataset — government open data is ideal. Profile it. Find the sentinels, the duplicates, the orphans. Write down what it cannot answer.
3Baseline & framingPick a target. Write the spec. Build the baseline. Do not model yet. Most people cannot resist this week; it is the point of it.
4Validation designDesign the split to match deployment. Build the pipeline object so transformers fit inside folds. Deliberately create a leak, watch the score jump, then fix it — that memory is worth more than reading about leakage.
5Model & thresholdLogistic, then boosting. Choose the threshold from a cost ratio you invent and justify. Convert the score into money.
6Error analysisFifty errors, read and categorised. Fix the biggest group. Re-run. This is the week that changes how you work.
7Calibration & segmentsReliability diagram. Score by segment. Find the group it is worst for and write the paragraph you would say about it.
8Ship itSerialise the whole pipeline. Score a held-out batch through the serving path and diff the features against training. Find the skew — there is usually some.
9MonitorLog inputs and predictions. Build the three charts: volume, input distributions, prediction distribution. Simulate drift and see whether you would catch it.
10CommunicateOne page, answer first, with the money sentence and the limitations. Present it to someone non-technical and watch where they get lost.

Portfolio: show the framing

A project that opens with the spec — decision, target, baseline, cost of errors — and reports against the baseline, beats a notebook with a higher AUC. Almost nobody does this.

Portfolio: show the errors

An error analysis section categorising what the model got wrong, with counts and a ceiling estimate. It is the clearest evidence of judgement you can produce.

Portfolio: show a "don't build it"

A written case where you concluded the data could not answer the question, with what you would record instead. Rare, and immediately credible.

Don't collect algorithms

Knowing ten model families is worth less than knowing how to frame, validate and evaluate one. The algorithm is the part libraries already solved.

One last thing

Of the twenty-four modules here, one is about choosing an algorithm and one is about tuning it. The rest are about deciding what to predict, proving the score is real, understanding what the model got wrong, and getting it into a place where somebody acts on it.

That ratio is not an accident of how this course was written. It is the distribution of where data science projects actually succeed and fail — and it is why the person who reads fifty errors by hand beats the person who runs a fourth hyperparameter search.
