Part I

The map

Foundation 01

What the job actually is

Data science is using data to answer questions and make predictions that change decisions. That is the whole thing. Everything else — the algorithms, the libraries, the statistics — is machinery in service of it.

The four levels — and which one a question is asking for
DESCRIPTIVE    "what happened?"
               revenue by month, churn rate last quarter
               → counting and aggregation. This is analytics.

DIAGNOSTIC     "why did it happen?"
               margin fell 2 points — which driver?
               → decomposition, segmentation, causal reasoning

PREDICTIVE     "what will happen?"
               which customers will churn next month?
               → THIS IS WHERE MODELS LIVE

PRESCRIPTIVE   "what should we do?"
               which customers should we contact, given a budget
               of 200 calls and these costs?
               → optimisation on top of a prediction

⭐ MOST REQUESTS ARRIVE PHRASED AS PREDICTIVE AND ARE ACTUALLY
  DIAGNOSTIC. "Can you predict churn?" usually means "why are we
  losing customers, and what do we do?" Building a predictor for a
  diagnostic question is the most common wasted quarter in the field.

⚠ AND PRESCRIPTIVE IS NOT PREDICTIVE. "Who will churn" is a
  prediction. "Will this discount stop them" is causal — a different
  question, needing a different method and different data. A churn
  model cannot answer it.
Foundation 02

The four roles, and what each owns

The titles are used inconsistently everywhere. These are the real distinctions.

RoleOwnsDay to day
Data analyst Descriptive and diagnostic. What happened and why. SQL, dashboards, metric definitions, investigating why a number moved. The most business-adjacent role and often the most influential.
Data scientist Predictive and inferential. Models, experiments, causal claims. Framing problems, feature engineering, training and evaluating models, designing A/B tests. Heavily statistical.
ML engineer Models in production. Serving, scale, reliability. Pipelines, deployment, latency, monitoring, retraining. More software engineering than statistics.
AI engineer Products built on models somebody else trained. Prompting, retrieval, agents, evals, cost. Newest of the four; see the companion foundations file.
also: Data engineer The data getting there at all. Pipelines, warehouses, ingestion. Everyone above depends on them, and the dependency is usually under-appreciated.
The honest version

In a company under about 200 people, one person does all of it and the title is arbitrary. In a large company the boundaries are real and political. Read job descriptions for the verbs, not the title: "build dashboards" and "deploy models" and "design experiments" are three different jobs that all get called data scientist.

Part II

The taxonomy

The vocabulary everything else is built on. If you learn nothing else from this file, learn this part.

Foundation 03 · the core distinction

Supervised, unsupervised, and the others

The learning types — separated by one question: do you have answers?
SUPERVISED LEARNING          ⭐ ~90% of business machine learning
  You have INPUTS and the CORRECT ANSWERS for historical examples.
  The model learns the mapping from one to the other.

    inputs (features)                    answer (label / target)
    ─────────────────────────────────    ───────────────────────
    tenure, salary, dept, last promo  →  left within 6 months? YES
    tenure, salary, dept, last promo  →  left within 6 months? NO
    ...5,000 more rows with answers...

  then, for a NEW person with no answer yet, predict it.

  ⭐ THE DEFINING FEATURE: you can MEASURE whether it is right,
    because you held back some rows whose answers you know.
    This is why supervised learning dominates — it is checkable.

UNSUPERVISED LEARNING
  You have inputs and NO answers. The model finds structure.

    5,000 customers × 20 behavioural columns, no labels
      → "there appear to be four distinct groups"
      → but nobody said what the right grouping IS

  ⚠ THERE IS NO SCORE TO OPTIMISE AND NO TEST SET. You cannot be
    "right". You can only be USEFUL — which is judged by whether a
    human can name the groups and act differently on them.
  used for: segmentation, anomaly detection, compressing many
  columns into few, exploring data you don't understand yet

SEMI-SUPERVISED
  A few labelled examples, many unlabelled. Use the labelled ones to
  bootstrap. Common when labelling is expensive (medical images).

SELF-SUPERVISED
  Labels created automatically FROM the data itself — hide a word,
  predict it; hide part of an image, reconstruct it.
  ⭐ THIS IS HOW LLMs ARE TRAINED. No human labelled the internet;
    the "label" is just the next word. It looks unsupervised from
    the outside and is mechanically supervised.

REINFORCEMENT LEARNING
  No labels. An AGENT takes ACTIONS in an ENVIRONMENT and receives
  REWARDS, learning a policy that maximises reward over time.
    game playing · robotics · some recommendation and pricing
  ⚠ RARE IN ORDINARY BUSINESS DATA SCIENCE. It needs an environment
    you can interact with cheaply and repeatedly, which most business
    problems do not have. Don't feel behind for not using it.
The question that classifies any problem

"For historical examples, do I know the answer?"

Yes → supervised. Now: is the answer a category or a number? (Next section.)
No, and I want structure → unsupervised.
No, but I could get some cheaply → get them. Labelling 500 examples by hand is often a day's work and converts an unmeasurable problem into a measurable one. This is under-used and almost always worth it.

Foundation 04

The task types

Within supervised and unsupervised, the shape of the output decides everything downstream: which models apply, which metrics mean anything, how you validate.

TaskOutputExample · and the metric family
Supervised
Binary classification One of two categories, usually with a probability Will this customer churn? Is this fraud?
→ precision, recall, PR-AUC. Not accuracy if the classes are imbalanced.
Multi-class One of N categories Which of 12 support topics is this ticket?
→ per-class precision/recall, macro vs weighted F1, a confusion matrix.
Multi-label Any number of categories at once Which tags apply to this article?
→ per-label metrics. Genuinely different from multi-class and often confused with it.
Regression A continuous number What will this house sell for? How long will delivery take?
→ MAE, RMSE, and a percentage error only if the target never approaches zero.
Ranking An ordering Which 10 products to show first.
→ NDCG, precision@k. Position matters and accuracy cannot express that.
Forecasting Future values of a series Demand next month.
→ MAE/WAPE, and you must beat the seasonal-naive baseline. Special rules — see the guide.
Survival / time-to-event How long until something happens Time until churn, until failure.
→ Handles the fact that some cases haven't happened yet (censoring), which ordinary regression gets badly wrong.
Unsupervised
Clustering Group assignments Customer segments.
→ silhouette score for geometry, but the real test is whether a human can name the groups.
Dimensionality reduction Fewer columns, most of the information Compress 200 features to 10 for visualisation or speed.
→ explained variance.
Anomaly detection Normal / unusual Fraud with no labels, equipment about to fail.
→ hard to evaluate without labels; usually validated by human review of the flagged set.
Association Co-occurrence rules "People who buy X also buy Y."
→ support, confidence, lift.
The distinction beginners get wrong most often

Classification vs regression is decided by the target, not the features. Predicting a customer's spend next month from categorical inputs is regression (the answer is a number). Predicting which of three tiers they fall into from numeric inputs is classification (the answer is a category).

And a related trap: turning a number into buckets to "make it a classification problem" throws information away. Sometimes it is right — because the decision is genuinely a threshold — but do it deliberately, not because classification feels easier.

Foundation 05

Which do I have?

SQL and a chart. │ ├─ "tell me why" ───────────────────► diagnosis. maybe a model as │ evidence, but the work is │ decomposition and reasoning. │ ├─ "tell me what will happen" ──────► PREDICTION. continue ↓ │ └─ "tell me what happens IF WE ACT" ► CAUSAL. an experiment, or a quasi-experimental design. ⚠ NOT a prediction model. How learning actually works

Four ideas. Once you have these, every algorithm in Part IV is a variation on the same theme.

Foundation 06

What a model is

A model is a function with adjustable numbers in it
That is the entire concept. Everything else is detail.

  a FUNCTION      takes features in, produces a prediction out
  with PARAMETERS numbers inside it that can be changed
                  (weights, split points, coefficients...)

  the simplest possible example — predicting salary from years:

      prediction  =  w × years  +  b

  w and b are the PARAMETERS. "Training" means finding the values of
  w and b that make the predictions closest to the real answers in
  your historical data. Nothing more mystical than that.

  a gradient-boosted model has thousands of split points instead of
  two numbers. a neural network has millions of weights. THE IDEA IS
  IDENTICAL: adjustable numbers, tuned to fit examples.

⭐ PARAMETERS vs HYPERPARAMETERS — a distinction that confuses
  everyone once and then never again:

    PARAMETERS       learned FROM the data, by training
                     w and b · tree split points · network weights
                     you never set these by hand

    HYPERPARAMETERS  chosen BY YOU, before training
                     how deep the trees · learning rate · how many
                     trees · k in k-means
                     "hyperparameter tuning" = searching for good
                     values of these, usually by trying combinations
                     and scoring each on validation data
Foundation 07

Loss and optimisation

How the machine knows it is wrong, and what it does about it.

The loop that trains every model
1  PREDICT     run the current parameters over the training examples
2  MEASURE     compute the LOSS — one number saying how wrong you are
3  ADJUST      change the parameters to make the loss smaller
4  REPEAT      until it stops improving

THE LOSS FUNCTION is how "wrong" is defined, and the choice matters:

  MEAN SQUARED ERROR      average of (prediction − truth)²
  (regression)            squaring means BIG errors hurt
                          disproportionately — so the model works
                          hard to avoid large misses and tolerates
                          many small ones

  MEAN ABSOLUTE ERROR     average of |prediction − truth|
  (regression)            all errors count linearly. more robust to
                          outliers. use when a few extreme values
                          shouldn't dominate.

  CROSS-ENTROPY           punishes CONFIDENT WRONG answers hardest.
  (classification)        saying "95% sure" and being wrong costs far
                          more than saying "55% sure" and being wrong.
                          this is what makes probabilities meaningful.

⭐ THE LOSS IS A DESIGN DECISION, NOT A DEFAULT. It encodes what you
  consider a bad mistake. Choosing MSE says large errors are
  disproportionately bad. Sometimes that is true and sometimes it is
  just the library's default doing your thinking.

HOW "ADJUST" WORKS — gradient descent, in plain language
  imagine the loss as a landscape and the parameters as your position.
  the GRADIENT is the direction of steepest uphill. so you step in the
  OPPOSITE direction, a little, and repeat.

    "a little" = the LEARNING RATE
      too big  → you overshoot the valley and bounce, or diverge
      too small → you crawl, or stall on a slope

  ⭐ THE LEARNING RATE IS THE MOST IMPORTANT HYPERPARAMETER in
    anything trained this way. When a model won't learn, suspect it
    before you suspect the architecture or the data.

  ⚠ NOT ALL MODELS TRAIN THIS WAY. Decision trees don't do gradient
    descent — they search for good split points directly. k-NN doesn't
    train at all. The loop above is the dominant pattern, not the only
    one.
Foundation 08 · the central problem

Overfitting

The single most important concept in machine learning, and the reason validation exists.

THE GOAL IS NOT TO FIT THE TRAINING DATA. THE GOAL IS TO PREDICT DATA YOU HAVE NEVER SEEN. Those pull in opposite directions past a point, and that tension is the whole discipline. UNDERFIT GOOD FIT OVERFIT too simple captures the pattern memorised the noise · · · · · · ──────── ╭──╯ ╰──╮ ╭╮ ╱╲ ╭╮ · · · · ╰╯· ╲╯╰· (passes through misses the real generalises EVERY point) pattern train error HIGH train error low train error ~ZERO test error HIGH test error low test error HIGH ⭐ THE DIAGNOSTIC IS THE GAP between train and test error — not the level of either one on its own. WHY OVERFITTING HAPPENS a flexible model can always find SOME pattern, including in pure noise. with enough parameters it can memorise the training set exactly — and memorising is not learning, because the noise in your sample is not in the next sample. THE FIXES MORE DATA ⭐ the only one with no downside. harder to memorise a million rows than a thousand. SIMPLER MODEL fewer parameters, shallower trees REGULARISATION a penalty on complexity, added to the loss — the model must "pay" for using a parameter EARLY STOPPING stop training when validation error starts rising, even though training error is still falling. the cheapest and most effective. MORE FEATURES? ⚠ NO. usually makes it worse. more columns means more ways to find a spurious pattern. ⚠ AND THE ONE THAT ISN'T OVERFITTING AT ALL: if your test score is great and PRODUCTION is bad, that is usually LEAKAGE, not overfitting. Different problem, different fix — see the guide.
Foundation 09

Train, validate, test

The mechanism that makes an honest number possible.

Three sets, three jobs
  ┌──────────── all your labelled data ────────────┐
  │  TRAIN  60%     │ VALIDATION 20% │   TEST 20%  │
  └─────────────────┴────────────────┴─────────────┘

  TRAIN       the model fits its parameters on this
  VALIDATION  YOU use this to choose: which model, which
              hyperparameters, which features, which threshold
  TEST        touched ONCE, at the very end, to report an honest
              number. never used to decide anything.

⭐ WHY THREE AND NOT TWO: every time you look at a set and change
  something because of what you saw, you have used it to make a
  decision — and it stops being an unbiased estimate. Try twenty
  hyperparameter combinations against your test set and the best one
  looks good partly by luck. That luck does not repeat in production.

⚠ IF YOU TUNE AGAINST THE TEST SET, IT IS NO LONGER A TEST SET.
  Every peek leaks a little, and you cannot tell how much.

CROSS-VALIDATION — when you don't have enough data to give 20% away
  split into k parts (usually 5). train on 4, test on 1. rotate.
  average the 5 scores.

    fold 1  [test][train][train][train][train]
    fold 2  [train][test][train][train][train]
    fold 3  [train][train][test][train][train]   ... etc

  + uses all the data, and the SPREAD across folds tells you how
    stable the estimate is — which a single split cannot.
  − k times the compute.

  STRATIFIED k-fold: keeps the class balance the same in every fold.
  ⭐ the default for classification. Without it, a rare class can be
    absent from a fold entirely.

⚠ AND THE RULE THAT CATCHES EVERYONE ONCE: any transformation that
  LEARNS from data — a scaler, an imputer, an encoder, feature
  selection — must be fitted INSIDE the fold, on the training part
  only. Fitting it on everything first leaks information from the
  test rows into training. Use a pipeline object so it is
  structurally impossible to get wrong.

The models

Each one explained by how it actually decides — because once you can picture the mechanism, when-to-use follows on its own instead of being memorised.

Model 10

Linear and logistic regression

The foundation everything else is measured against.

Linear regression · how it decides
  prediction = w₁·feature₁ + w₂·feature₂ + ... + b

Each feature gets a WEIGHT. The prediction is a weighted sum. That is
all. Training finds the weights that minimise squared error.

WHAT THE WEIGHTS MEAN — and this is why the model matters
  "w₁ = 2,400" means: holding everything else constant, one more unit
  of feature₁ is associated with 2,400 more of the target.
  ⭐ You can read the model. Nothing else on this list gives you that
    as directly.

GOOD AT           interpretability · small data · a baseline ·
                  anywhere you must explain the model to a regulator
BAD AT            non-linear relationships · interactions between
                  features (unless you create them by hand) ·
                  outliers drag the line badly
KNOBS             regularisation strength (below)
ASSUMES           roughly linear relationship, roughly constant error
                  variance, errors roughly independent

REGULARISATION — the two you must know
  RIDGE (L2)   penalty on the SQUARE of each weight
               → shrinks all weights toward zero, none to exactly zero
               → good when many features are correlated
  LASSO (L1)   penalty on the ABSOLUTE VALUE of each weight
               → drives some weights to EXACTLY zero
               → so it performs FEATURE SELECTION for you
  ELASTIC NET  both. the sensible default when unsure.
  ⚠ all three require SCALING the features first, or the penalty
    punishes whichever feature happens to have big units.
Logistic regression · the same idea, for categories
Despite the name it is CLASSIFICATION, not regression.

  step 1   compute a weighted sum, exactly like linear regression
  step 2   squash it into 0–1 with the logistic (sigmoid) function

           1.0 ┤          ╭─────────
               │        ╭─╯
           0.5 ┤    ╭───╯
               │  ╭─╯
           0.0 ┤──╯
               └──────────────────────
                     weighted sum

  the output is a PROBABILITY. you then apply a THRESHOLD to turn it
  into a decision — and 0.5 is an arbitrary default, not a right
  answer. Choose it from what each error costs.

⭐ WHY IT REMAINS THE FIRST THING TO TRY, ALWAYS
  fast · interpretable (as odds ratios) · well-calibrated
  probabilities out of the box · impossible to overfit badly with
  regularisation · and it tells you IMMEDIATELY whether there is
  signal in your features at all.
  If logistic regression gets 0.79 and boosting gets 0.81, take the
  one you can explain.

READING THE COEFFICIENTS
  they are in LOG-ODDS, which nobody thinks in. exponentiate them and
  you get ODDS RATIOS: "e^w = 1.6" means the odds multiply by 1.6 per
  unit. That is the sentence you say to a stakeholder.
Model 11

Decision trees

HOW IT DECIDES — a flowchart it learned by itself tenure < 18 months? ╱ ╲ yes no │ │ last promo > 2y? dept = Sales? ╱ ╲ ╱ ╲ yes no yes no │ │ │ │ LEAVES STAYS LEAVES STAYS (78%) (61%) (55%) (91%) HOW IT LEARNS at each node, try every feature and every possible split point. pick the one that best SEPARATES the classes — measured by Gini impurity or entropy, which both just mean "how mixed is this group". repeat on each side. stop when a rule says to. GOOD AT interpretability you can SHOW someone · captures interactions automatically (a split inside a split IS an interaction) · no scaling needed · handles mixed data types · handles missing values in most libraries BAD AT ⚠ OVERFITS SEVERELY on its own — an unconstrained tree will split until every leaf is one row, which is pure memorisation ⚠ UNSTABLE — change a few rows and you get a visibly different tree, which makes it feel arbitrary KNOBS max depth · min samples per leaf · min impurity decrease. these all limit memorisation. ⭐ IN PRACTICE YOU ALMOST NEVER USE A SINGLE TREE FOR PREDICTION. You use it when you need to SHOW the logic to a human, and you use forests or boosting when you need accuracy. Which is exactly what the next section is about.
Model 12 · the workhorses

Random forests and gradient boosting

Both are "many trees". They combine them in opposite ways, and the difference explains everything about when to use which.

The contrast that makes both make sense
RANDOM FOREST — BAGGING · trees built in PARALLEL, independently
  · train hundreds of trees, each on a random sample of the ROWS
  · at each split, each tree may only consider a random subset of
    the COLUMNS
  · predict by VOTING (classification) or AVERAGING (regression)

  WHY IT WORKS: individual trees overfit in DIFFERENT directions.
  Averaging cancels the errors out. The two sources of randomness
  are there to make the trees DISAGREE — correlated trees average
  to no improvement.

  ⇒ REDUCES VARIANCE. Fixes the overfitting a single tree has.

GRADIENT BOOSTING — trees built in SEQUENCE, each fixing the last
  · train a weak tree. look at what it got wrong.
  · train the next tree to predict THOSE ERRORS.
  · add it in, scaled by the learning rate. repeat, hundreds of times.

  WHY IT WORKS: each tree specialises in the mistakes still
  remaining. The ensemble incrementally grinds the error down.

  ⇒ REDUCES BIAS. Usually more accurate — and it CAN overfit,
    because it will keep fitting noise if you let it run.

┌──────────────┬─────────────────────┬────────────────────────────┐
│              │ RANDOM FOREST       │ GRADIENT BOOSTING          │
├──────────────┼─────────────────────┼────────────────────────────┤
│ trees built  │ in parallel         │ in sequence                │
│ each tree    │ independent         │ fixes the previous ones    │
│ reduces      │ variance            │ bias                       │
│ overfits?    │ resistant           │ YES if unchecked           │
│ tuning       │ forgiving           │ needs care                 │
│ accuracy     │ very good           │ usually best on tabular    │
│ parallel?    │ fully               │ sequential by nature       │
│ ⭐ use when  │ a strong, low-effort│ you want the best tabular  │
│              │ baseline; little    │ score and can tune         │
│              │ time to tune        │                            │
└──────────────┴─────────────────────┴────────────────────────────┘

BOOSTING'S KEY KNOBS — and what each does
  n_estimators     how many trees. more = more capacity to overfit.
  learning_rate    how much each tree contributes. LOWER is better
                   quality but needs MORE trees. classic pairing:
                   low rate + many trees + early stopping.
  max_depth        3–8 is typical. deeper trees = more interactions
                   captured and more overfitting.
  subsample        fraction of rows per tree. <1 adds randomness and
                   regularises.
  ⭐ EARLY STOPPING is the main regulariser: watch validation error
    and stop when it stops improving. Set n_estimators high and let
    early stopping decide.

THE THREE IMPLEMENTATIONS
  XGBoost    the established one. huge ecosystem.
  LightGBM   faster on large data, grows trees leaf-wise.
  CatBoost   best handling of categorical features out of the box,
             and strong defaults — good when you don't want to tune.
  ⇒ Differences are real but modest. Pick one and learn it properly.
If you learn one model family properly, make it this one

Gradient boosting is the default winner on tabular data — the shape of most business problems. It handles mixed types, missing values and non-linear relationships natively, needs almost no feature scaling, and beats neural networks on tables more often than not.

The realistic pattern for a business project: baseline → logistic regression → gradient boosting → stop. Everything after that is usually diminishing returns compared to fixing your labels, your features, or your framing.

Model 13

k-NN, SVM and Naive Bayes

Three you should understand, and reach for less often than the textbooks suggest.

ModelHow it decidesUse / avoid
k-nearest neighbours No training at all. To predict, find the k most similar rows in the training data and take their majority vote (or average). "You are like your neighbours." Use: small data, genuinely similarity-shaped problems, a quick sanity baseline. Avoid: large data (prediction is slow — it searches everything), many features (in high dimensions everything is roughly equidistant, so "nearest" stops meaning anything), and unscaled features (the biggest-unit column dominates distance).
Support vector machine Finds the boundary that separates the classes with the widest possible margin — the most confident separation rather than just any line. The "kernel trick" lets it draw curved boundaries by implicitly mapping into higher dimensions. Use: small-to-medium data, many features, clear separation, text classification. Avoid: large n (training scales badly), and when you need probabilities (its outputs need calibrating). Needs scaling.
Naive Bayes Applies Bayes' rule, assuming every feature is independent given the class. That assumption is almost always false — hence "naive" — and it works anyway, surprisingly often. Use: text classification (spam, topic), very high dimensions, very little data, or when you need something that trains in milliseconds. Avoid: when features are strongly dependent and you need calibrated probabilities — it tends to be overconfident.
Model 14

Clustering and PCA

The unsupervised workhorses — where there is no right answer to check against.

k-means · how it decides
  1  pick k random points as CENTROIDS
  2  assign every row to its NEAREST centroid
  3  move each centroid to the CENTRE of its assigned rows
  4  repeat 2–3 until nothing moves

  that is the whole algorithm. it minimises the total squared
  distance from points to their centroid.

  ⚠ YOU MUST CHOOSE k IN ADVANCE. The data does not tell you.
    the "elbow method": plot the total distance against k and look
    for the bend. it is genuinely fuzzy and often has no clear elbow.
    the silhouette score is a better-behaved alternative.

  ASSUMES  roughly spherical clusters of roughly similar size.
           ⚠ it will happily split one elongated cluster in two and
             merge two adjacent small ones — and report success.
  NEEDS    scaling. otherwise the column with the largest units
           defines the distance and therefore the clusters.
  ALSO     sensitive to the random start — run it several times
           (k-means++ initialisation does this sensibly).

WHEN k-MEANS ISN'T RIGHT
  DBSCAN / HDBSCAN   finds clusters of ARBITRARY SHAPE and marks
                     outliers as noise rather than forcing everything
                     into a group. no k needed. struggles when
                     densities vary a lot.
  HIERARCHICAL       builds a tree of nested clusters — you get a
                     dendrogram and pick the cut afterwards. doesn't
                     scale to large n.
  GAUSSIAN MIXTURE   soft assignment: a probability of belonging to
                     each cluster, rather than a hard label.

⭐ THE ONLY EVALUATION THAT MATTERS: can a domain expert NAME each
  cluster and act differently on it? A statistically tidy
  segmentation nobody can describe is a colouring, not a segmentation.
PCA · compressing many columns into few
Finds new axes ("components") along which the data varies MOST, and
lets you keep only the first few.

  200 correlated features → 10 components holding, say, 85% of the
  variation. Same information, a twentieth of the columns.

USE FOR      speeding up training · removing collinearity ·
             visualising high-dimensional data in 2D · denoising
COSTS        ⚠ INTERPRETABILITY. A component is a blend of all
             original features — "component 1" has no business
             meaning, so you can no longer say which variable
             mattered.
NEEDS        scaling first, always. PCA follows variance, and an
             unscaled large-unit column will dominate every component.
LINEAR ONLY  it can only find linear structure.

⚠ t-SNE AND UMAP are for LOOKING, not measuring. They produce
  beautiful 2D plots of high-dimensional data — but distances between
  clusters in the plot are not meaningful, the layout changes with
  the random seed, and you must never cluster on their output and
  treat the result as real structure.
Model 15

Neural networks

What they are, in one page — and the honest answer about when to use one.

A STACK OF LAYERS. The honest position on neural networks

On tabular data — a CSV of columns — gradient boosting usually wins. Not marginally, and not only on small data. Boosting handles mixed types and missing values natively, trains in minutes on a laptop, needs almost no tuning to be good, and hands you feature importances. A neural network on the same table needs more preprocessing, more tuning, more compute, and typically lands at similar or worse performance.

Reach for deep learning when the input has structure a network can exploit — spatial (images), sequential (text, audio), relational (graphs) — or when you can start from a pre-trained model. Not because it is more advanced.

The choosing table

If your situation is…Start with
Anything, at the very beginningThe non-model baseline — the current rule, the majority class, last period's value. Always.
Tabular, labelled, and you want a number for whether signal existsLogistic / linear regression. Fast, interpretable, and it sets the bar.
Tabular, labelled, and you want the best scoreGradient boosting. This is where most business projects end.
Tabular, and you have almost no time to tuneRandom forest. Forgiving defaults, hard to get badly wrong.
You must show the logic to a person or a regulatorA single decision tree, or logistic regression with odds ratios.

Predicting the future of a seriesSeasonal naive first, then ETS or Prophet, then boosting with lag features.

Part V

What you actually need

The two questions every beginner asks, answered honestly rather than aspirationally.

Foundation 17

The maths, honestly

Far less than the internet implies — but the parts you need, you need properly.

AreaDepth neededWhat that means concretely
Descriptive statistics Deep Mean vs median vs percentiles and when each lies. Distributions and skew. Variance. You will use this every single day — more than any other item on this list.
Probability Solid Conditional probability, independence, Bayes' rule, expected value. Base rates especially — the false-positive paradox is the most practically important piece of probability in the job.
Inferential statistics Solid What a p-value is and is not. Confidence intervals. Type I/II error and power. Multiple comparisons. You need to reason with these, not derive them.
Linear algebra Conceptual What a vector and a matrix are; that a dot product measures alignment; that multiplying by a matrix transforms space. You will not compute an eigenvector by hand. You need the intuition so PCA and embeddings are not magic.
Calculus Conceptual That a derivative is a slope, and gradient descent walks downhill on it. That is genuinely enough for applied work. You will never differentiate anything by hand in this job.
Optimisation Conceptual Local vs global minima, learning rate, convergence. Enough to debug "it isn't learning".
Experimental design Solid Randomisation, sample size, power, guardrails. Frequently the highest-value thing you know — and it is more logic than maths.
Measure theory, real analysis, matrix calculus Skip For research. Not for applied work. Nobody at a normal company will ever ask.
The honest summary

Statistics deep, probability solid, linear algebra and calculus conceptual. A strong grasp of distributions, base rates and experimental design will take you further than a weak grasp of eigendecomposition.

The failure mode to avoid is spending six months on mathematics before touching data. Learn the maths as the problems demand it — you will retain it, and you will learn the parts that actually recur rather than the parts a textbook happens to order first.

The notebook trap

Notebooks are excellent for exploring and terrible for anything that must run again. Cells run out of order, state persists invisibly, and the thing that worked yesterday cannot be reproduced.

The rule: explore in a notebook, then move anything that has to run twice into a .py file with functions. The moment you find yourself scrolling up to re-run a cell, that logic wants to be a function.

Part VI · what you asked for

Beginner to expert

Five rungs. What you can do at each, what you cannot yet, and the specific thing that gets you to the next one.

Foundation 19

The ladder

Level 1 · Beginner — "I can follow a tutorial"
CAN DO      load a CSV · fit a model with sklearn · print an accuracy
            score · make a chart
CANNOT YET  tell whether that score means anything
THE GAP     you are running steps, not making decisions. Every choice
            — the metric, the split, the model — came from the
            tutorial, not from the problem.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: take a dataset with NO tutorial attached and
  answer a question somebody actually asked. The discomfort of having
  no cell to copy is the learning.

TYPICAL: a few weeks to a few months.
Level 2 · Practitioner — "I can build a model that works"
CAN DO      clean data · engineer features · choose a sensible model ·
            cross-validate · report the right metric · explain a
            confusion matrix
CANNOT YET  reliably notice when a great score is a lie
THE GAP     ⭐ LEAKAGE. This is the rung almost everyone gets stuck on,
            and the symptom is a model that is excellent offline and
            useless in production.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: ship something and watch it fail. Then learn
  the prediction-time question — "would this value exist, with this
  value, at prediction time?" — and apply it to every feature you
  ever build again.

TYPICAL: 6–18 months. Most self-taught people plateau here, and the
thing that ends the plateau is contact with production.
Level 3 · Independent — "I can be given a vague problem"
CAN DO      turn "we're losing customers" into a target, a unit, a
            baseline and a metric · design a validation split that
            matches deployment · spot leakage before it bites ·
            choose a threshold from the cost of errors · do error
            analysis · present to non-technical people
CANNOT YET  judge which problems are worth solving at all
THE GAP     you can execute any project handed to you. You are not
            yet the person deciding which projects exist.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: start saying no. Recommend against a model
  when a rule would do, or when the data cannot support the question.
  The first time you do this and are right, you have changed rung.

TYPICAL: 2–4 years. This is a solidly employable, well-paid level and
many good data scientists stay here happily.
Level 4 · Senior — "I decide what gets built"
CAN DO      everything above, plus: reframe the question the business
            asked into the one it needed · estimate value before
            building · recognise a causal question and refuse to
            answer it with a predictive model · design the human
            review surface · own something in production and monitor
            it · mentor
CANNOT YET  change how the organisation makes decisions
THE GAP     your judgement is trusted on your own projects. It is not
            yet the standard others work to.

⭐ WHAT MOVES YOU UP: influence without authority. Getting three
  teams to agree one definition of "active customer" is a political
  problem, not a technical one, and no amount of modelling skill
  helps.

TYPICAL: 4–8 years. The transition is from THROUGHPUT to JUDGEMENT —
and it is genuinely hard to be recognised for, because senior value
often shows up as things NOT built.
Level 5 · Expert / staff — "I change how the org decides"
CAN DO      set standards others follow · design the measurement
            system, not just the model · know which of the last ten
            requests should have been rules · build the platform that
            makes everyone else faster · say the uncomfortable thing
            in the room where the decision is made

NOT DEFINED BY  knowing more algorithms. Nobody reaches this rung by
                learning an eleventh model family.

DEFINED BY      the quality and speed of judgement under uncertainty,
                and the ability to make other people's judgement
                better.

TYPICAL: 8+ years, and not everyone wants it — it is substantially
less hands-on modelling and substantially more conversation.
The honest thing about the ladder

Look at what separates each rung: rung 1→2 is skills. Every rung after that is judgement. Leakage, framing, knowing when not to model, influence — none of them is an algorithm.

Which is why the most common career mistake is spending year three learning a fourth model family instead of learning to frame a problem. Nobody has ever been promoted for knowing CatBoost. People are promoted for being the one whose numbers turn out to be right, and whose projects turn out to matter.

Foundation 20

The six-month plan

Zero to solidly employable, assuming serious part-time study. Built around finishing things rather than starting them.

MonthFocusDo this — the doing is the point
1 Python + SQL Enough Python to manipulate data comfortably. SQL properly: joins, group by, window functions, CTEs. SQL first if you have to choose — you will use it more than anything else.
2 pandas + real messy data Take a government open dataset — real and unpolished. Load it, profile it, find the sentinels and duplicates. Write down what it cannot answer. That last habit is worth more than the pandas.
3 Statistics that gets used Distributions, base rates, p-values and what they are not, confidence intervals, correlation vs causation. Work the base-rate confusion matrix by hand until it is fast.
4 Your first supervised model, done properly Pick a target. Write the spec: decision, unit, target, baseline, metric, cost of errors. Build the baseline first. Then logistic regression. Then boosting. Compare all three.
5 Validation and leakage Design the split to match deployment. Use a Pipeline so transformers fit inside folds. Deliberately create a leak, watch the score jump, then fix it — that memory is worth more than any amount of reading about leakage.
6 Evaluation, error analysis, communication Threshold from a cost ratio. Convert the score into money. Read fifty errors by hand and categorise them. Then write one page, answer first, and show it to someone non-technical.

Then: one portfolio project

Not four half-finished notebooks. One project with a written spec, a baseline, an honest evaluation, an error analysis, and a paragraph on what you would do differently. That beats a higher score every time.

Show the mess

"I found 12% disguised nulls, reconciled to within 0.1% of the published total, and concluded the regional split was unusable" demonstrates more judgement than a flawless model on a clean file. You are hired for what you do when the data is bad.

Don't collect certificates

A finished project with honest evaluation outperforms six course certificates. The certificates prove attendance; the project proves judgement.

Read other people's analyses

Specifically, look for what they didn't check. It is the fastest way to build the instinct for what is missing in your own.
