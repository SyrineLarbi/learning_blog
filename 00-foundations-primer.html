Software engineering foundations · the entry-level course

The discipline before the stack. What software engineering actually is.

Before any framework, before any language: what the profession is, how software gets built from an idea to a running system, who does what, what kinds of languages exist and why, how engineers design before building — and what the career and the responsibilities actually look like. Nothing here assumes you have written code.

Part I · Module 1

The discipline

What the field is, how software gets built start to finish, how teams choose to move through that, and who is involved.

Foundations 01

Programming versus software engineering

ProgrammingSoftware engineering
ProducesA program that worksA system that keeps working — for other people, under change, over years
TimescaleNowYears, including after you leave
AudienceYouUsers who did not ask how it works, and colleagues who must modify it
SuccessIt runsIt runs, it is correct at the edges, it can be changed safely, and someone would notice if it stopped
ConcernsLogicLogic plus requirements, design, testing, deployment, operation, maintenance, cost, security, and the people involved
A definition worth keeping
⭐ SOFTWARE ENGINEERING IS THE DISCIPLINED APPLICATION OF
  ENGINEERING PRINCIPLES TO BUILDING SOFTWARE THAT IS CORRECT,
  MAINTAINABLE AND FIT FOR PURPOSE — AT A SCALE AND OVER A
  LIFETIME WHERE INDIVIDUAL CLEVERNESS IS NOT ENOUGH.

THE KEY PHRASE IS THE LAST ONE.
  a script you write for yourself needs no process. A system
  forty people will modify over a decade needs:
    · a shared understanding of what it should do
    · a structure people can navigate without asking
    · a way to change it without breaking what worked
    · a way to know it is broken before a customer tells you
  ⇒ THE PROCESS IS NOT BUREAUCRACY. It is what makes a system
    survivable by people who were not there when it was built.

⭐ WHAT MAKES SOFTWARE "ENGINEERED" — five properties
  ①  IT IS SPECIFIED — somebody wrote down what it must do
  ②  IT IS DESIGNED — the structure was chosen, not accreted
  ③  IT IS VERIFIED — there is evidence it works, not a belief
  ④  IT IS MAINTAINABLE — the next person can change it
  ⑤  IT IS OPERABLE — you can tell what it is doing in
      production, and fix it when it is not

⇒ code that has none of these can still be perfectly good code.
  It is just not yet part of an engineered system.
Foundations 02

Why the discipline exists

The software crisis, and what it taught
IN THE 1960s AND 70s, LARGE SOFTWARE PROJECTS ROUTINELY:
  · ran years late
  · cost several times their estimate
  · shipped unusable, or never shipped at all
  · could not be maintained after the original team dispersed

⚠ AND THE CAUSE WAS NOT BAD PROGRAMMERS. It was the absence of
  any method for coordinating many people around a system too
  large for any one of them to hold in their head.

⭐ THE OBSERVATIONS THAT CAME OUT OF IT ARE STILL THE FIELD'S
  MOST DURABLE FINDINGS:

  ①  ADDING PEOPLE TO A LATE PROJECT MAKES IT LATER.
     communication paths grow faster than the workforce — every
     new person must be brought up to speed by someone who was
     already busy.

  ②  THERE IS NO SINGLE TECHNIQUE THAT MULTIPLIES PRODUCTIVITY.
     the hard part is understanding the problem — and no tool,
     language or process removes that.

  ③  THE COST OF FIXING A DEFECT RISES SHARPLY WITH HOW LATE IT
     IS FOUND. See reflex 04. ⭐ THIS ONE SHAPES EVERY
     METHODOLOGY IN THE FIELD.

  ④  MOST OF A SYSTEM'S TOTAL COST IS SPENT AFTER IT FIRST
     SHIPS. Maintenance is the main event, not the epilogue.
     ⇒ WHICH IS WHY READABILITY AND STRUCTURE ARE NOT
       AESTHETICS — they are the majority of the budget.

⭐ AND THE MINDSET THAT FOLLOWS FROM ALL FOUR: ASSUME YOU WILL
  BE WRONG ABOUT SOMETHING, AND MAKE BEING WRONG CHEAP. That is
  the whole of engineering judgement in one sentence, and every
  practice in this primer is a version of it.
Foundations 03

The SDLC — seven phases

THE SOFTWARE DEVELOPMENT LIFECYCLE the sequence every piece of software goes through, whether or not anyone names it. ① PLANNING is this worth building? what would success look like? who is it for? ⇒ output: a goal and a rough scope. ② REQUIREMENTS what exactly must it do, and not do? ⇒ output: written requirements, functional and non-functional (reflex 07). ⭐ THE HIGHEST-LEVERAGE PHASE — a misunderstanding here is carried faithfully through all six that follow. ③ DESIGN how will it be structured? what are the pieces, the data, the interfaces? ⇒ output: a design, and diagrams where they help (Part IV). ④ IMPLEMENTATION write the code. ⚠ THE PHASE EVERYONE PICTURES WHEN THEY IMAGINE THE JOB, and typically well under half the effort. ⑤ TESTING does it do what was specified, including at the edges and under load? ⑥ DEPLOYMENT get it running where users are, safely and repeatably. ⑦ MAINTENANCE fix, adapt, extend, operate. ⭐ THE LONGEST AND MOST EXPENSIVE PHASE BY FAR — usually most of the system's total lifetime cost. ⭐ THE CRUCIAL POINT: THESE ARE PHASES, NOT NECESSARILY STEPS IN ORDER. Doing them strictly once, in sequence, is one methodology (waterfall). Doing all seven every two weeks on a small slice is another (agile). THE PHASES ARE ALWAYS PRESENT; WHAT VARIES IS HOW OFTEN YOU GO ROUND.
Foundations 04

The cost of finding problems late

RELATIVE COST TO FIX THE SAME DEFECT, BY WHERE IT IS FOUND requirements █ 1× design ██ ~5× coding ████ ~10× testing ████████ ~25× production ████████████████████████████████ ~100× ⭐ THE NUMBERS VARY BY STUDY AND CONTEXT — THE SHAPE DOES NOT. The curve is why the entire field is organised the way it is. WHY IT RISES SO STEEPLY · a requirements error costs a conversation · a design error costs rework of code that exists · a production error costs the rework PLUS the incident, PLUS the data already written incorrectly, PLUS the trust, PLUS doing it under time pressure ⭐ AND HERE IS WHAT EVERY PRACTICE IN THIS PRIMER IS ACTUALLY FOR — MOVING DETECTION LEFT: asking questions before building ⇒ catches it at 1× writing requirements down ⇒ catches it at 1× designing and reviewing the design ⇒ catches it at 5× code review ⇒ catches it at 10× automated tests ⇒ catches it at 10× staging and canary deploys ⇒ catches it at 25× monitoring and alerting ⇒ 100×, but fast ⇒ SO WHEN SOMEONE ASKS WHY YOU SPENT TWENTY MINUTES ASKING QUESTIONS BEFORE STARTING, THIS CHART IS THE ANSWER.
Foundations 05

Methodologies

ModelHow it movesRight when
WaterfallEach phase once, in order, each signed off before the nextRequirements are genuinely fixed and knowable up front — regulated, contractual, or physical-world work. Its weakness: you learn whether it was right at the very end.
V-modelWaterfall with a matching test level for each design levelSafety-critical work where every requirement must be traceably verified.
Iterative / incrementalBuild a piece, review, build the nextThe bridge to agile. Delivers something usable early and learns from it.
AgileShort cycles, working software each cycle, plan adjusted as you learnMost software, most of the time — because requirements usually are not knowable up front.
Lean / XPEliminate waste; engineering practices (pairing, TDD, continuous integration)XP contributed most of the technical practices agile teams now take for granted.
What agile actually says — and the common misreading
THE FOUR PREFERENCES (each says "over", not "instead of")
  people and collaboration   OVER   process and tools
  working software           OVER   comprehensive documentation
  customer collaboration     OVER   contract negotiation
  responding to change       OVER   following a plan

⚠ THE MISREADING IS UNIVERSAL: "agile means no documentation, no
  planning and no process."
  ⭐ IT SAYS THE RIGHT-HAND ITEMS HAVE VALUE — just less than
    the left when they conflict. An agile team still plans,
    still documents, still has process. It just does not let
    those become the point.

⭐ AND THE FOUR PRACTICES THAT PRODUCE MOST OF THE BENEFIT,
  WHATEVER THE FRAMEWORK IS CALLED:
  ①  short cycles with something usable at the end
  ②  talking to the person who wanted it, directly
  ③  adjusting the plan when you learn something
  ④  looking at what went wrong, without blame

⚠ THE CEREMONY TRAP: a team can perform every ritual —
  stand-ups, retros, story points — and still not do ①–④.
  ⭐ WHEN JUDGING A TEAM'S PROCESS, ASK WHETHER THOSE FOUR
    HAPPEN, NOT WHICH MEETINGS ARE ON THE CALENDAR.
Foundations 06

Scrum and Kanban

ScrumKanban
RhythmFixed-length sprints, commonly two weeksContinuous flow, no sprints
ScopeCommitted at the start of the sprintPulled when there is capacity
Central controlThe sprint commitmentThe work-in-progress limit — only N items in a column at once

The one idea worth stealing from Kanban regardless

Limit work in progress. Six things at 80 % complete are worth nothing; two things finished are worth two things. A WIP limit forces finishing over starting, and it makes bottlenecks visible instead of hiding them in everyone's parallel workload.

Foundations 07

Requirements

Two kinds, and the second is where systems fail
FUNCTIONAL — what it must DO
  "a manager can generate a report for their own team"
  ⇒ testable as a yes/no.

NON-FUNCTIONAL — how well it must do it
  ⭐ THE ONES ROUTINELY OMITTED, AND THE ONES THAT DECIDE THE
    ARCHITECTURE:
      PERFORMANCE   "the page loads in under two seconds for
                    95% of requests"
      SCALE         "500 concurrent users; 50,000 employees"
      AVAILABILITY  "available during business hours in three
                    time zones"
      SECURITY      "salary is visible only to HR and the
                    employee"
      PRIVACY       "EU employee data stays in the EU"
      USABILITY · ACCESSIBILITY · MAINTAINABILITY

  ⚠ "IT SHOULD BE FAST" IS NOT A REQUIREMENT. It cannot be
    tested, cannot be designed for, and cannot be argued about
    productively. ⭐ A REQUIREMENT WITHOUT A NUMBER IS AN
    OPINION.

WHAT MAKES A REQUIREMENT GOOD
  □  UNAMBIGUOUS — one reading only
  □  TESTABLE — you can demonstrate it is met
  □  NECESSARY — someone loses something if it is absent
  □  FEASIBLE — possible within the constraints
  □  ⭐ TRACEABLE — you can say who asked for it and why. Six
     months later "why does it do that?" has an answer.

⭐ THE FORMAT THAT WORKS FOR MOST TEAMS — the user story:
  "AS A manager, I WANT to see my team's attrition risk, SO
  THAT I can have a conversation before someone resigns."
  ⇒ THE "SO THAT" IS THE VALUABLE PART. It states the purpose,
    which is what lets you propose a better solution than the
    one that was requested.

⚠ AND THE MOST COMMON FAILURE OF ALL: TAKING A REQUEST AT FACE
  VALUE. Half of all requests are a proposed solution wearing
  the costume of a requirement. Ask what they will do with it.
Foundations 08

Quality, versions, testing, documentation

Four practices, compressed
WHAT "QUALITY" MEANS — and it is two different things
  EXTERNAL   what the user experiences: it works, it is fast,
             it does not lose data, it is usable.
  INTERNAL   what the next engineer experiences: it is
             readable, structured, testable, changeable.
  ⭐ USERS PAY FOR EXTERNAL QUALITY AND ONLY INTERNAL QUALITY
    LETS YOU KEEP DELIVERING IT. Neglect the inside and the
    outside degrades a year later, when every change becomes
    risky.

SEMANTIC VERSIONING — MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH  (e.g. 3.4.1)
  PATCH  a bug fix, nothing else changes            3.4.1 → 3.4.2
  MINOR  new functionality, nothing breaks          3.4.1 → 3.5.0
  MAJOR  ⭐ SOMETHING BREAKS — callers must change   3.4.1 → 4.0.0
  ⇒ SO A VERSION NUMBER IS A PROMISE, and reading one tells you
    how carefully to upgrade. `^3.4.1` means "any 3.x" — which
    is why a lock file matters (Part II).

TESTING — the levels
  UNIT         one piece in isolation. Many, fast.
  INTEGRATION  pieces together, with a real database.
               ⭐ THE BEST VALUE PER TEST in most web
               applications, because most defects live in the
               seams rather than inside a function.
  SYSTEM / E2E the whole thing as a user experiences it. Few,
               slow, and the only proof it actually works.
  ACCEPTANCE   does it satisfy the requirement? Often a person.
  ⇒ AND THE ORTHOGONAL PAIR: REGRESSION testing (did this
    change break something that worked?) and SMOKE testing (is
    it fundamentally alive after deploy?).

DOCUMENTATION — write the kinds that get read
  ⭐ THE CODE SAYS WHAT. ONLY DOCUMENTATION CAN SAY WHY.
  worth writing: a README that gets someone running · API
  documentation GENERATED from the code so it cannot drift ·
  a decision record for anything architectural · a runbook for
  a known failure.
  ⚠ not worth writing: prose describing what the code does
    line by line. It is wrong within a month and nobody trusts
    it after that.

Three things to understand about this table

Roles are not people. In a small team one person covers five of these. In a large one, a single role is a department. The table is a list of responsibilities that must be owned by somebody — and the useful question on any project is which of them currently has no owner.

Job titles do not map cleanly onto it. "Full-stack developer" spans three rows; "software engineer" can mean any of eight.

A cross-functional squad contains the roles needed to ship something end to end — which is precisely so that shipping does not require queueing behind another team.

Part II · Module 2

How software gets built

Foundations 10

Client and server

THE MODEL UNDER ALMOST EVERYTHING CLIENT ──── request ────► SERVER (browser, ◄─── response ── (a program on a machine phone, app) you control) the client ASKS; the server ANSWERS. The client never touches the data directly, and the server never assumes the client is honest. WHAT HAPPENS WHEN YOU OPEN A WEB PAGE — the short version ① you type a URL ② the name is resolved to an address ③ a connection is opened, and secured ④ the browser requests the page ⑤ the server responds with HTML ⑥ the browser reads it and requests what it references — stylesheets, scripts, images, fonts ⑦ it draws the page ⑧ scripts run, and the page becomes interactive ⑨ from then on, the page requests DATA rather than pages ⭐ STEP ⑨ IS THE MODERN WEB. Older sites fetched a whole new page for every action; today the page stays and asks for data as needed. That single shift is why front-end engineering became a specialism. ⭐ AND THE PROPERTY THAT EXPLAINS MOST OF WEB SECURITY: HTTP IS STATELESS — the server remembers nothing between requests. So every request must carry its own proof of identity, and "logged in" is not a state the server holds.
Foundations 11

Front end, back end, and the API between them

Front endBack end
RunsIn the user's browser or appOn a server you control
Responsible forRendering, interaction, requesting data, displaying itBusiness rules, data storage, authentication, authorisation, talking to other services
Can be trusted?No. The code has been delivered to someone else's machine; it can be read and modifiedYes — it is the only place a rule can actually be enforced
Typical concernsLayout, state, accessibility, bundle size, perceived speedCorrectness, permissions, data integrity, throughput, cost
The API — a contract, explained by analogy and then plainly
THE RESTAURANT: you read the MENU and order from a waiter. You
never enter the kitchen, and you do not know whether the soup
was made fresh. You know the CONTRACT and nothing else.

⭐ AND THAT IGNORANCE IS THE POINT. The kitchen can be rebuilt
  in a different language and your order still works, as long as
  the menu is unchanged.

PLAINLY: AN API IS A PUBLISHED SET OF OPERATIONS ONE PROGRAM
OFFERS ANOTHER, WITH AGREED INPUTS AND OUTPUTS.
  · the front end calls the back end's API
  · the back end may call another service's API
  · they exchange data in a common format — usually JSON, plain
    text that every language can read, which is why two
    services written in different languages can cooperate at
    all

⭐ WHY THE SPLIT IS WORTH THE COMPLEXITY — four reasons
  ①  SEPARATION OF CONCERNS — UI problems and business-rule
     problems are genuinely different kinds of problem
  ②  INDEPENDENT WORK — agree the contract, then proceed in
     parallel
  ③  THE RIGHT TOOL FOR EACH JOB
  ④  ⭐ SECURITY — the non-negotiable one. Password checks,
     permission decisions and database access must happen
     somewhere the user cannot modify.
     ⇒ A DISABLED BUTTON IS A COURTESY, NOT A CONTROL.
Foundations 12

What "the cloud" means

Renting computers, with layers of how much you manage
⭐ THE CLOUD IS SOMEBODY ELSE'S COMPUTERS, RENTED BY THE HOUR,
  WITH AN API TO CREATE AND DESTROY THEM. Everything else is a
  consequence of those three facts.

THE SERVICE MODELS — how much you still manage

  IaaS   you rent the machine
         ⇒ you manage the operating system, the runtime, the
           application, patching and scaling. Most control,
           most work.
  PaaS   you rent a place to run your application
         ⇒ push code, it runs. ⭐ WHERE MOST SMALL TEAMS SHOULD
           LIVE.
  SaaS   you rent the finished software
         ⇒ email, a managed database, an authentication
           provider. You manage nothing.

  ⇒ AND THE MODERN ADDITION: SERVERLESS — you rent the
    execution of a single function, and pay nothing when idle.

⭐ THE JUDGEMENT THAT MATTERS MORE THAN THE ACRONYMS: EVERY
  LAYER YOU MANAGE IS A LAYER YOU MUST PATCH, MONITOR, SCALE
  AND BE WOKEN UP BY. A small team running its own database
  cluster has chosen to spend its scarcest resource —
  attention — on something a provider does better and cheaper.

WHY TEAMS USE IT
  · no capital purchase, and capacity in minutes
  · elasticity — scale up for a spike, back down after
  · managed services that would otherwise be a full-time job
  · geographic reach without owning buildings

⚠ AND THE HONEST COSTS
  · it is not automatically cheaper — a steady predictable load
    can be cheaper on your own hardware
  · ⭐ THE BILL IS A DESIGN CONSEQUENCE. An inefficient query
    now has a price, which is a genuinely new discipline.
  · you are dependent on a provider, and on your network

What a stack is

The set of technologies chosen to work together
A STACK IS THE LAYERS A SYSTEM IS BUILT FROM:

  presentation   what the user sees
  application    the logic
  data           where it is stored
  infrastructure where it all runs

THE NAMED ONES YOU WILL HEAR
  LAMP    Linux · Apache · MySQL · PHP        — the classic
  MEAN    MongoDB · Express · Angular · Node
  MERN    MongoDB · Express · React · Node
  ⇒ these names matter less than they used to; most teams now
    assemble their own.

⭐ THIS PROJECT'S STACK, AS AN EXAMPLE OF THE SHAPE
  presentation    Next.js + React + Ant Design
  application     NestJS (TypeScript) + FastAPI (Python)
  data            PostgreSQL, via Prisma
  infrastructure  containers on a managed platform

  ⇒ NOTE THE TWO APPLICATION LANGUAGES. That is a deliberate

    SHOULD ALWAYS HAVE A REASON YOU CAN STATE IN ONE SENTENCE.

HOW STACKS ARE ACTUALLY CHOSEN — honestly
  ①  what the team already knows      ⭐ the biggest factor,
                                        and a legitimate one
  ②  what the problem requires
  ③  what you can hire for
  ④  what has a healthy ecosystem and good documentation
  ⚠ NOT: what is newest, or what a company at a thousand times
    your scale uses. Their solution is a response to problems
    you do not have.
Foundations 15

Working in a team

Why this is a core engineering skill, not a soft one
⭐ SOFTWARE IS BUILT BY GROUPS, AND THE LIMIT ON WHAT A GROUP
  CAN BUILD IS USUALLY COORDINATION RATHER THAN INDIVIDUAL
  ABILITY. That is the lesson of the software crisis
  (reflex 02), and it has not changed.

HOW TEAMS ARE ORGANISED
  BY FUNCTION   all the frontend engineers together, all the
                backend engineers together.
                ⚠ shipping one feature then requires
                  coordinating three teams' priorities.
  BY PRODUCT    ⭐ A SQUAD — a small cross-functional group with
                the roles needed to ship something end to end.
                ⇒ THE POINT IS AUTONOMY: it can deliver without
                  queueing behind anyone.

THE DAILY RITUALS, AND WHAT EACH IS FOR
  STAND-UP        ⭐ for surfacing BLOCKERS, not for reporting
                  status to a manager. If it is a status
                  recital, it has failed.
  PLANNING        agreeing what the next cycle contains
  REVIEW / DEMO   showing working software to the people who
                  asked for it
  RETROSPECTIVE   ⭐ THE HIGHEST-VALUE ONE, and the first to be
                  cancelled when busy. It is where a team
                  actually improves.

⭐ THE PRACTICAL HABITS THAT MAKE SOMEONE GOOD TO WORK WITH
  □  ASK EARLY. Thirty minutes stuck is learning; a day stuck
     is waste, and nobody thanks you for it.
  □  WRITE THINGS DOWN where others can find them.
  □  SAY WHEN YOU ARE BLOCKED, IMMEDIATELY.
  □  ⭐ SAY "I DON'T KNOW" AND "I WAS WRONG" WITHOUT DRAMA. A
     team where those are cheap finds problems early; a team
     where they are expensive hides them until they are large.
  □  keep changes small, so review is fast
  □  assume good intent in review comments — text has no tone
  □  ⭐ FLAG A SLIPPING ESTIMATE EARLY. A revised date is a
     manageable problem; a missed date discovered on the day is
     a trust problem.
Foundations 16

Pairing, mobbing and review

PracticeWhat it isBest for
Pair programmingTwo people, one piece of work. One drives, one navigates; they swapHard problems, onboarding someone, high-risk code. Slower per hour, and often faster to a correct result — and it spreads knowledge as a side effect.
Mob programmingThe whole team on one thing, one screenAn important decision, an unfamiliar area, or a crisis. Expensive, and occasionally exactly right.
Code reviewSomeone reads your change before it mergesThe default, and the highest-value collaboration ritual there is. Asynchronous, and it leaves a written record.
What review is actually for — and it is not mainly bugs
⭐ THE BIGGEST OUTPUT IS THAT TWO PEOPLE NOW UNDERSTAND THE
  CHANGE. That is knowledge spreading, and it is why a reviewed
  codebase survives someone leaving.

WHAT TO LOOK FOR, IN ORDER
  ①  does it do the RIGHT THING? (read the description, then
     the code) ⭐ THE MOST EXPENSIVE DEFECT IS A CORRECT
     SOLUTION TO THE WRONG PROBLEM
  ②  are permissions enforced?
  ③  the edge cases — empty, one, many, absent, twice
  ④  ⭐ WHAT ELSE DOES THIS CHANGE? A changed default or error
     case, visible only in the diff
  ⑤  is it clear? would a stranger understand WHY?

HOW TO COMMENT SO IT LANDS
  ⚠ "this is wrong" — no information, defensive author
  ✅ THE FAILURE · THE CONSEQUENCE · A SUGGESTION
  ⭐ AND LABEL THE SEVERITY: blocking · suggestion · question ·
    nit · praise. Without labels every comment reads as
    blocking, and a review of twelve nits feels like a
    rejection.

⚠ WHAT NOT TO SPEND REVIEW ON: formatting, import order, quote
  style. Automate all of it — every comment spent there is
  attention taken from ①–④.
Part III · Module 3

Programming basics

How code becomes action, why there are so many languages, and the logic that is identical in every one of them.

Foundations 17

What a language is, and how source becomes action

A PROGRAMMING LANGUAGE IS A NOTATION FOR EXPRESSING INSTRUCTIONS PRECISELY ENOUGH THAT A MACHINE CAN CARRY THEM OUT — and readably enough that a person can maintain them. ⭐ BOTH HALVES MATTER. A notation only a machine can read is assembly; a notation only a human can read is a specification. FROM SOURCE TO EXECUTION source code text you write │ │ TRANSLATION (a compiler, an interpreter, or both) ▼ machine instructions the processor's own operations │ ▼ THE PROCESSOR EXECUTES THEM ⭐ EVERYTHING A COMPUTER DOES REDUCES TO A SMALL SET OF PRIMITIVE OPERATIONS: move a value, add, compare, jump if a condition holds. Every language, every framework and every model is built out of those. ⇒ WHICH IS WHY THE LOGIC IN REFLEXES 20–23 IS UNIVERSAL: it is what those primitives compose into. AND THE THING THAT ACTUALLY RUNS IT THE RUNTIME — the program that executes your program. the browser's engine · Node.js · CPython · a virtual machine. ⇒ so "which language" and "which runtime" are two questions, and the second decides where your code can run.
Foundations 18

Compiled, interpreted, and the hybrid middle

HowConsequences
Compiled
C, Rust, Go		A build step translates the whole program to machine code before it runsFast to run; errors caught at build time; a separate binary per platform. Slower to iterate.
Interpreted
Python, Ruby		A runtime reads and executes the source as it goesNo build step, quick to start, portable. Slower to run, and many errors appear only when that line executes.
Bytecode + VM
Java, C#		Compiled to an intermediate form, then run by a virtual machine"Compile once, run anywhere the VM exists." A middle position on both axes.
JIT
JavaScript		Interpreted, but frequently-run code is compiled to machine code while runningMost of both benefits. Why JavaScript is far faster than its interpreted description suggests.
Transpiled
TypeScript		Translated to another language first — TypeScript becomes JavaScriptThe types are deleted in that step, so they catch mistakes before the program runs and are entirely absent while it runs.
Why the distinction is worth knowing

It predicts when you find out you were wrong — at build time or at run time — and that single difference shapes how you work. In a compiled or typed language you lean on the build; in an interpreted one you lean on tests, because nothing else will tell you.

Foundations 19

Levels and paradigms

Two independent ways of classifying a language
LEVEL — how far from the machine
  MACHINE CODE   the processor's own numeric instructions
  ASSEMBLY       a readable name per instruction. One line, one
                 machine operation.
  LOW-LEVEL      C — you manage memory yourself
  HIGH-LEVEL     Python, JavaScript, Java — memory, and much
                 else, is managed for you
  ⭐ THE TRADE IS ALWAYS THE SAME: CONTROL AND SPEED VERSUS
    SAFETY AND PRODUCTIVITY. Higher level means fewer things
    you can get catastrophically wrong, and less control over
    the machine.

PARADIGM — how you organise the instructions
  IMPERATIVE       describe the STEPS. "Do this, then this."
  PROCEDURAL       imperative, grouped into procedures.
  OBJECT-ORIENTED  organise around THINGS that hold data and
                   behaviour together. ⇒ Part IV.
  FUNCTIONAL       ⭐ describe TRANSFORMATIONS; avoid changing
                   state. Its central ideas — immutability,
                   pure functions, map/filter/reduce — are now
                   everywhere, including in React.
  DECLARATIVE      describe the RESULT and let something else
                   work out how. ⭐ SQL AND HTML ARE BOTH
                   DECLARATIVE, which is why they feel
                   different from writing code.
  ⇒ MOST MODERN LANGUAGES ARE MULTI-PARADIGM, and good code
    often mixes them: objects for structure, functional style
    for transformation.

AND THE LANGUAGES THAT ARE NOT PROGRAMMING LANGUAGES
  QUERY (SQL)  ⭐ you state WHAT you want; the database decides
               how to get it
  MARKUP (HTML, XML)  structure and meaning, no logic
  STYLING (CSS)       presentation
  DATA (JSON, YAML)   ⚠ these describe data, not behaviour, and
                      calling them programming languages
                      confuses a real distinction
Foundations 20

Variables and types

The universal building blocks — part one
A VARIABLE IS A NAMED PLACE TO HOLD A VALUE.
  the name is for humans; the machine cares only about the
  location. ⭐ SO NAMING IS NOT COSMETIC — it is the only
  documentation that is always present.

THE TYPES EVERY LANGUAGE HAS, UNDER SOME NAME
  INTEGER    whole numbers                       42
  FLOAT      numbers with a fractional part      0.73
  STRING     text                                "Alice"
  BOOLEAN    true or false
  NULL       ⭐ THE ABSENCE OF A VALUE — and it is the source of
             more bugs than any other single concept in
             programming

⚠ THE THREE THINGS THAT ARE NOT THE SAME, AND ARE CONSTANTLY
  TREATED AS THE SAME:
    0            a real value that happens to be zero
    ""           a real value that happens to be empty
    null         no value at all
  ⇒ A DISCOUNT OF ZERO AND NO DISCOUNT ARE DIFFERENT FACTS. A
    system that conflates them reports the wrong number and
    never errors.

STATIC vs DYNAMIC TYPING
  STATIC   the type is fixed and checked before running
           ⇒ mistakes caught early, more to write
  DYNAMIC  the type is whatever the value currently is
           ⇒ quicker to write, mistakes found at run time
  ⭐ AND THE MIDDLE THAT MOST TEAMS NOW CHOOSE: a dynamic
    language with an optional type layer — TypeScript over
    JavaScript, type hints in Python. You get early checking
    where it pays and flexibility where it does not.

⚠ AND ONE FACT THAT SURPRISES EVERYONE ONCE: FLOATING-POINT
  ARITHMETIC IS NOT EXACT. 0.1 + 0.2 does not equal 0.3 in most
  languages. ⭐ NEVER STORE MONEY AS A FLOAT — use integer minor
  units, or a decimal type.
Foundations 21

Control flow

EVERY PROGRAM EVER WRITTEN IS MADE OF THREE SHAPES. ⭐ THAT IS A PROVEN RESULT, NOT A SIMPLIFICATION. ① SEQUENCE — one thing after another step 1 ──► step 2 ──► step 3 ② SELECTION — a decision ┌── condition true ──► do this ──► test ──┤ └── condition false ─► do that if / else if / else · switch ⚠ AND THE PART BEGINNERS MISS: WHAT HAPPENS WHEN NO BRANCH MATCHES? An `if` with no `else` silently does nothing, and "silently does nothing" is a bug that is very hard to see. ③ REPETITION — a loop ┌─────────────────┐ │ do the thing │ └────────┬────────┘ │ more to do? └── yes ──► round again no ──► carry on `for` when you know how many · `while` when you loop until a condition changes ⚠ THE TWO CLASSIC ERRORS: · THE INFINITE LOOP — the condition never becomes false. ⭐ ASK OF EVERY LOOP: WHAT MAKES THIS STOP? · THE OFF-BY-ONE — running once too often or too few. Almost always at the boundary: the first item, the last item, or an empty collection. ⭐ AND THE THING TO CARRY INTO EVERY LANGUAGE YOU EVER LEARN: ONCE YOU CAN READ THESE THREE SHAPES, YOU CAN READ THE STRUCTURE OF ANY PROGRAM. What changes between languages is spelling.
Foundations 22

Functions

The unit of reuse, and of thought
A FUNCTION IS A NAMED PIECE OF LOGIC THAT TAKES INPUTS AND
PRODUCES AN OUTPUT.

  function riskLevel(score):
      if score > 0.7:  return "high"
      if score > 0.4:  return "medium"
      return "low"

  PARAMETERS   the inputs it declares
  ARGUMENTS    the actual values passed in
  RETURN       what it hands back
  SCOPE        ⭐ names created inside are invisible outside —
               which is what stops two parts of a program
               accidentally sharing a variable

⭐ WHY FUNCTIONS ARE THE MOST IMPORTANT IDEA IN THIS PART
  ①  REUSE — write once, call anywhere
  ②  NAMING — a good name replaces a comment, permanently
  ③  TESTABILITY — a function with inputs and an output can be
     checked in isolation
  ④  ⭐ ABSTRACTION — the caller need not know how it works.
     This is how a program of a million lines stays thinkable.

⭐ THE ONE DISTINCTION THAT MATTERS MOST, AND IT RECURS AT EVERY
  LEVEL OF ENGINEERING:
    A PURE FUNCTION depends only on its inputs and changes
    nothing outside itself. Same inputs, same output, always.
    A SIDE EFFECT is anything else — writing to a database,
    sending an email, reading the clock, printing.

  ⇒ PURE LOGIC IS EASY TO TEST, EASY TO REASON ABOUT AND EASY
    TO REUSE. So the durable design habit is to SEPARATE THE
    DECISION FROM THE I/O:
        fetch  →  decide  →  act
        (I/O)    (pure)     (I/O)
    ⭐ THE MIDDLE PIECE IS WHERE YOUR BUSINESS RULES LIVE, and
      keeping it pure is the single most valuable structural
      habit in this primer.

GOOD FUNCTION HYGIENE
  □  ONE JOB. ⭐ If the name needs "and", split it.
  □  a name that says what it does, not how
  □  few parameters — many usually means a missing concept
  □  ⚠ and a return that is predictable: a function that
     sometimes returns a value and sometimes nothing will
     eventually be called by someone who assumes the first.

Basic data structures

StructureHoldsUse it when
Array / listAn ordered sequence, read by positionThe default. You have several of something and order matters.
Object / dictionary / mapKey → value pairsThe most useful one in practice. Looking something up by id, repeatedly, without searching.
SetUnique values, no order"Have I seen this?" and removing duplicates.
Tuple / recordA fixed group of related valuesA pair or triple that belongs together — coordinates, a date range.

The one insight to take from this table

A map turns searching into looking up. If you find yourself scanning a list inside a loop over another list, building a map first collapses the work dramatically — and it is the single most common fix for slow application code at any level of experience.

Everything else here is vocabulary. That one is a tool you will use constantly.

Foundations 24

Pseudocode and flowcharts

Thinking about logic without committing to a language
PSEUDOCODE — the logic in structured plain language

  READ the team's employees
  FOR EACH employee
      IF satisfaction is missing
          FLAG as "no data" and SKIP
      COMPUTE risk from tenure, satisfaction, last promotion
      IF risk is above the threshold
          ADD to the review list
  SORT the review list by risk, highest first
  RETURN the top 10

⭐ WHY THIS IS WORTH DOING BEFORE WRITING CODE
  · it exposes the decisions — ⭐ look at line 3: SOMEONE HAD
    TO DECIDE what happens when data is missing, and writing it
    in plain language made that decision visible instead of
    accidental
  · it can be checked by a non-programmer who knows the domain
  · it separates "what should happen" from "how do I express
    this in TypeScript", which are two different difficulties

FLOWCHARTS — the same logic as a picture
    ▭  a step        ◇  a decision
    ▱  input/output   ⬭  start or end
  ⇒ useful for a branchy process a stakeholder must validate.
  ⚠ USELESS FOR REAL CODE — a flowchart of a real function is
    unreadable, and it goes out of date immediately.

⭐ THE HABIT THAT SURVIVES: WRITE THE STEPS IN PLAIN LANGUAGE
  FIRST, IN A COMMENT OR A TICKET. If you cannot state the
  logic in seven plain sentences, you do not yet understand the
  problem — and code written before that point will be a
  confident answer to the wrong question.

Foundations 25

The problem OOP solves

Organising code around things, not just steps
THE PROBLEM
  a purely procedural program of any size becomes a large set
  of functions operating on a large set of shared data. ⚠ ANY
  FUNCTION MIGHT CHANGE ANY DATA, so understanding one change
  means understanding everything.

THE IDEA
  ⭐ GROUP THE DATA WITH THE OPERATIONS THAT ARE ALLOWED ON IT,
    AND LET NOTHING ELSE TOUCH IT DIRECTLY.

  A CLASS is a template: what an Employee has, and what can be
  done to one.
  AN OBJECT is one actual employee, built from that template.

    class Employee
        has:  name, salary, hireDate
        can:  tenureYears(), applyRaise(percent)

    alice = new Employee("Alice", 62000, "2021-03-01")

  ⇒ so the rule "a raise may not exceed 20%" lives INSIDE
    Employee, next to the salary it protects — rather than in
    whichever four places happen to change salaries.

⭐ WHAT YOU ACTUALLY GAIN, STATED PLAINLY
  · the rules about a thing are in one findable place
  · the data cannot be corrupted from outside
  · the concepts in the code match the concepts in the business
    — an Employee, a Team, a Report. ⭐ THIS IS THE UNDERRATED
    BENEFIT: the code becomes discussable with non-engineers.

⚠ AND THE HONEST CAVEAT: OOP IS NOT THE ONLY GOOD WAY, AND IT
  IS OFTEN OVERUSED. A pure transformation is better as a
  function than as a class with one method. Modern code mixes
  objects for structure with functional style for computation —
  and this project's NestJS services are exactly that mix.
Foundations 26

The four pillars

PillarMeansWhy it helps
EncapsulationData is private; access goes through methods that enforce the rulesThe most important of the four. An invariant enforced inside the object holds for every caller, forever — including the caller written next year.
AbstractionExpose what it does, hide howCallers depend on a small stable surface, so the inside can be rewritten without breaking them. This is the same idea as an API.
InheritanceA class extends another, reusing and specialising itReal reuse where a genuine "is-a" relationship exists — and the most misused of the four. See reflex 27.
PolymorphismDifferent types respond to the same call in their own wayThe one that pays off most in practice. Code written against a general shape works with implementations that did not exist when it was written.
Polymorphism, concretely, because it is the abstract-sounding one
you need to send a notification. Three channels exist: email,
in-app, SMS.

⚠ WITHOUT POLYMORPHISM
  if channel is "email"      → sendEmail(...)
  else if channel is "inapp" → sendInApp(...)
  else if channel is "sms"   → sendSms(...)
  ⇒ AND EVERY NEW CHANNEL MEANS FINDING AND EDITING EVERY ONE
    OF THESE BLOCKS — of which there are now eleven, scattered.

✅ WITH POLYMORPHISM
  every channel implements the same shape: `send(message)`
  the calling code does:  channel.send(message)
  ⇒ ADDING A CHANNEL MEANS ADDING ONE CLASS AND CHANGING NO
    EXISTING CODE.

⭐ THAT PROPERTY — EXTEND BY ADDING RATHER THAN BY EDITING — IS
  THE WHOLE POINT, and it is the "O" in SOLID (reflex 28).
Foundations 27

Composition versus inheritance

The most useful design judgement in this part
INHERITANCE  "IS-A"      a Manager IS AN Employee
COMPOSITION  "HAS-A"     an Employee HAS A Salary, HAS A
                         ContactDetails

⚠ WHY INHERITANCE GOES WRONG SO OFTEN
  ①  IT IS PERMANENT. A class has one parent, chosen at
     design time, and changing it later touches everything.
  ②  ⭐ IT COUPLES YOU TO THE PARENT'S INTERNALS. A change in
     the base class can break every subclass — sometimes
     subtly, and at a distance. This is called the fragile base
     class problem, and it is real.
  ③  DEEP HIERARCHIES BECOME UNREADABLE. To understand one
     method you open five files.
  ④  IT IS USED FOR CODE REUSE WHEN THERE IS NO REAL "IS-A".
     ⚠ THE CLASSIC: `class Employee extends DatabaseRecord`.
       An employee is not a database record; it HAS a
       representation in a database.

⭐ THE RULE THE FIELD SETTLED ON: FAVOUR COMPOSITION OVER
  INHERITANCE.
  build behaviour by assembling small pieces rather than by
  extending a chain. It is more flexible, changeable at run
  time, and each piece is testable alone.

WHEN INHERITANCE IS GENUINELY RIGHT
  · a true, stable "is-a" that will not be re-classified
  · implementing an INTERFACE — ⭐ which is inheritance of a
    CONTRACT rather than of implementation, and carries almost
    none of the problems above
  · the framework requires it (extending a base controller)

⭐ THE TEST: WOULD "IS A" BE TRUE TO A DOMAIN EXPERT WHO HAS
  NEVER SEEN CODE? If you have to explain why it is technically
  true, it is composition.
Foundations 28

SOLID

PrincipleIn plain terms
SSingle responsibilityA class should have one reason to change. If two different stakeholders can request changes to it, it is doing two jobs.
OOpen/closedOpen to extension, closed to modification — add a new case by adding code, not by editing a chain of conditionals. This is reflex 26's payoff.
LLiskov substitutionAnywhere the parent works, a child must work. If a subclass throws where the parent returned, or narrows what it accepts, the hierarchy is wrong.
IInterface segregationMany small interfaces beat one large one. Nobody should be forced to implement methods they do not use.
DDependency inversionDepend on an abstraction, not a concrete thing. A service should ask for "a way to store reports", not construct a specific database client — which is exactly what dependency injection does in NestJS and FastAPI.
How to actually use these

As diagnostic questions, not as a checklist. Nobody designs by reciting SOLID. But when a piece of code is painful to change, one of these five is usually the reason — and naming it turns "this feels wrong" into a specific, fixable observation.

The two with the most day-to-day value are S (this class does too much) and D (this class built its own dependencies and is now untestable).

Foundations 29

Design versus architecture, and why model at all

Two altitudes of the same activity
ARCHITECTURE   the decisions that are EXPENSIVE TO REVERSE
               ⇒ how many services · what the data model is ·
                 which database · synchronous or asynchronous ·
                 where the trust boundary sits
DESIGN         the decisions INSIDE those
               ⇒ which classes, which functions, which
                 patterns, how this module is organised

⭐ THE PRACTICAL DIFFERENCE IS COST OF CHANGE. Renaming a class
  is an afternoon. Splitting a database is a quarter. ⇒ SPEND
  YOUR CARE PROPORTIONALLY: think hard, and briefly, about the
  first; iterate freely on the second.

WHY MODEL BEFORE BUILDING — four honest reasons
  ①  ⭐ IT IS THE CHEAPEST PLACE TO BE WRONG. Reflex 04's chart:
     a design error costs ~5×, the same error found in
     production ~100×.
  ②  IT SURFACES DISAGREEMENT. Two people who "agree" on a
     feature often discover, at the whiteboard, that they
     imagined different systems.
  ③  IT REVEALS THE MISSING QUESTIONS. Drawing the boxes makes
     you ask what happens when one of them is unavailable.
  ④  IT COMMUNICATES. A diagram explains in a minute what
     prose takes a page to convey.

⚠ AND WHY NOT TO OVER-MODEL: A DESIGN IS A PREDICTION, AND
  PREDICTIONS DECAY. A complete design for a six-month project
  will be wrong in its details, and the detailed parts are the
  parts that will be wrong.
  ⭐ SO: MODEL THE EXPENSIVE DECISIONS AND THE INTERFACES; LEAVE
    THE INSIDES TO BE DISCOVERED WHILE BUILDING.
Foundations 30

The UML diagrams that earn their keep

DiagramShowsWorth it when
Class diagramClasses, their fields, and the relationships between themWorking out the domain model. The most used of the structural diagrams.
ER diagramTables, columns, keys and cardinalityGenuinely essential. The database schema is the most permanent decision in a system — draw it before creating it.
Sequence diagramMessages between participants, in time orderThe highest-value behavioural diagram. Tracing a login, a payment, or a report request across services — it exposes every round trip and every failure point.
Use-case diagramActors and what they can doScoping a feature with non-technical stakeholders. Deliberately simple.
State diagramStates and the legal transitions between themUnderused and very valuable — anything with a lifecycle (draft → submitted → approved → paid). It makes the illegal transitions visible, which is where the bugs are.
Activity diagramA process, with decisions and parallel pathsA business workflow that must be validated by the people who run it.
Component diagramThe deployable pieces and their interfacesMostly superseded by C4's container view — reflex 31.
The honest position on UML

Full UML is a formal notation with fourteen diagram types, and almost no team uses it formally. What survives in practice is four or five of the above, drawn loosely on a whiteboard to have a conversation — and then usually thrown away.

That is not a failure of UML; it is the correct use of it. The value was in the conversation, not in the artefact. Keep a diagram only when someone will read it later — and then accept that you have taken on the obligation to keep it true.

Architecture styles

StyleShapeTrade-off
MonolithOne application, one deployable, one databaseSimple to build, deploy, debug and reason about. One codebase, one log. The right starting point for almost everything. Its limit: everything scales together, and one language for all of it.

                        INSIDE the code
                        ⇒ factory · strategy · adapter ·
                          observer · decorator

  design pattern is a refactor; swapping an architectural
  pattern is a project. ⇒ WHICH IS EXACTLY WHY THE FIRST IS A
  DESIGN DECISION AND THE SECOND IS AN ARCHITECTURE DECISION

Foundations 33

Choosing an architecture

The advice the whole field converged on

Start with a monolith. Give it clean internal boundaries. Split off a service only when you can state the specific reason in one sentence.

Good reasons: this part needs a different language (Python for machine learning); this part must scale independently; this part has a genuinely separate team and lifecycle. Bad reasons: microservices are modern; a large company does it; we might need to scale one day.

The deployment environments this all runs through
LOCAL       your machine. Fast iteration.
DEVELOPMENT a shared integrated environment.
STAGING     ⭐ AS CLOSE TO PRODUCTION AS YOU CAN AFFORD — its
            value is exactly proportional to how similar it is.
            A staging environment with 200 rows and different
            configuration tells you almost nothing.
PRODUCTION  real users, real data. The only one that matters.

⭐ TWO RULES THAT PREVENT REAL INCIDENTS
  ①  SEPARATE CREDENTIALS PER ENVIRONMENT. A shared secret
     means a test script can reach production.
  ②  ⚠ DO NOT COPY PRODUCTION DATA INTO LOWER ENVIRONMENTS.
     Salaries, reviews and health information in an
     environment with weaker access control is a breach waiting
     to be found. ⇒ ANONYMISE OR GENERATE.

WHAT A PRODUCTION SYSTEM CONTAINS BEYOND YOUR CODE
  a load balancer · TLS certificates · a managed database with
  tested backups · a cache where it earns its place · a queue
  for unbounded work · monitoring, logs and alerts · a
  deployment pipeline · secrets management · and a way to turn
  a feature off without deploying.
  ⭐ THE POINT OF THE LIST IS NOT TO ADOPT ALL OF IT — IT IS TO
    KNOW WHICH LINE YOU ARE MISSING, AND WHETHER THAT IS A
    CHOICE OR AN OVERSIGHT.

Threats, controls, review. High leverage, and it requires thinking like an attacker.

QA / test engineering

Test strategy and automation. Underrated, and it is where "how would this break?" is a full-time skill.

On choosing, and on the effect of AI

Depth in one area plus competence across many is the shape that holds up — the depth earns trust, the breadth lets you see how the pieces meet. And the most valuable depth is often an intersection you already have: a domain background plus engineering skills is rarer, and harder to replace, than a second framework.

On AI specifically: it has made producing code much cheaper, which moved the bottleneck to deciding what to build, judging whether it is correct, and owning the outcome. Those were always the senior skills — the change is that they are now the main skills rather than the ones you grew into.

Why ethics is part of engineering

Not a separate subject
⭐ SOFTWARE DECIDES THINGS ABOUT PEOPLE WHO NEVER AGREED TO IT
  AND USUALLY CANNOT SEE IT. Whether someone gets a loan, an
  interview, a shift, a diagnosis, a benefit. Whether their
  data is safe. Whether a system works for them at all.

  ⇒ AND THE ENGINEER IS OFTEN THE ONLY PERSON WHO UNDERSTANDS
    WHAT THE SYSTEM ACTUALLY DOES. That asymmetry is the
    source of the obligation: nobody else is positioned to
    notice.

THE ACM/IEEE CODE OF ETHICS — its eight principles, compressed
  ①  THE PUBLIC INTEREST comes first ⭐ AND IT IS FIRST
     DELIBERATELY — ahead of the client and the employer
  ②  act in the best interests of CLIENT AND EMPLOYER,
     consistent with ①
  ③  ensure the PRODUCT meets professional standards
  ④  maintain integrity in JUDGEMENT
  ⑤  MANAGERS shall promote an ethical approach
  ⑥  advance the INTEGRITY OF THE PROFESSION
  ⑦  be fair and supportive to COLLEAGUES
  ⑧  maintain your own COMPETENCE — ⭐ staying current is
     framed as an ethical duty, not just career development

⭐ THE ORDERING IS THE MOST IMPORTANT THING ABOUT IT. When
  the public interest and your employer's interest conflict,
  the code is explicit about which wins. That is what makes it
  a professional code rather than a policy.

AND THE PRACTICAL FORM IT TAKES
  COMPETENCE   ⭐ do not take on work you cannot do safely —
               and say so early rather than late
  HONESTY      about estimates, risks, capabilities and what
               you do not know
  PRIVACY      collect what the purpose needs, protect it,
               delete it when done
  SECURITY     ⭐ AN ETHICAL DUTY, NOT A FEATURE REQUEST. The
               people harmed by a breach are not the people who
               deprioritised it.
  ACCESSIBILITY excluding people is a defect, not a
               nice-to-have
Foundations 38

The code, applied to this kind of system

HR software concentrates almost every ethical question at once
THIS PROJECT PREDICTS WHETHER IDENTIFIABLE PEOPLE WILL LEAVE
THEIR JOBS. That makes the following engineering requirements,
not principles.

PRIVACY AND MINIMISATION
  □  collect what the stated purpose needs — ⚠ "it might be a
     useful feature" is not a purpose
  □  ⭐ PURPOSE LIMITATION: data gathered for payroll may not
     silently become model input
  □  a retention period, and an erasure path that reaches
     backups, logs, exports and training sets

ACCESS AND PROPORTIONALITY
  □  ⭐ PER-FIELD PERMISSIONS. Salary, sickness, disciplinary
     notes and a name have completely different audiences —
     one endpoint returning "the employee" returns all of it
  □  a minimum cell size on every aggregate. ⭐ "ENGAGEMENT IN
     THE LISBON TEAM" WHERE THE TEAM IS THREE PEOPLE
     IDENTIFIES THREE PEOPLE — aggregation is not
     anonymisation
  □  audit who viewed whose record

FAIRNESS
  □  exclude protected attributes AND their proxies — ⚠
     postcode, school, name, working pattern and career gaps
     all correlate with them, so removing the obvious column
     is not sufficient
  □  ⭐ EVALUATE PER SUBGROUP, not only in aggregate. An
     overall score can hide a model that works well for one
     group and badly for another.
  □  a model that faithfully reproduces a biased history is a
     machine for perpetuating it at scale, with an appearance
     of objectivity

TRANSPARENCY AND RECOURSE
  □  a human who can genuinely override — ⚠ a rubber stamp is
     not human review
  □  an explanation the person affected could be given
  □  ⭐ FRAME A PREDICTION AS A PROMPT, NOT A VERDICT. With a
     low base rate, most people a good model flags will not
     leave. Presenting a flag as a fact is both wrong and
     unfair.

⭐ AND THE QUESTION THAT COVERS ALL OF IT: WOULD WE BE
  COMFORTABLE EXPLAINING THIS SYSTEM, IN DETAIL, TO THE PEOPLE
  IT SCORES? If not, that discomfort is the finding.
Foundations 39

Everyday dilemmas

The situationWhat professional judgement looks like
You are asked to ship with a known security flaw to hit a dateState the risk in writing, with the consequence. Offer the smallest mitigation that fits the time. If overruled, the written record matters — and if it is severe enough, escalate rather than comply.
You are told to log data "just in case it's useful later"That is collection without a purpose. Ask what decision it serves; log the minimum, with a retention period.
Your estimate was wrong and the date will slipSay so immediately. A revised date is manageable; a missed date discovered on the day is a trust failure — and trust is what seniority is made of.
You find a bug in production that nobody has noticedReport it. Silence is a decision that the people affected did not get to make.
You are asked to build something you believe is harmful to usersSay why, once, clearly, with an alternative. This is the case the code of ethics puts the public interest first for.
You do not understand the code you are about to changeSay so and take the time. Changing code you do not understand is how outages happen, and "I need an hour to read this" is always cheaper.
You copied a solution you cannot explainUnderstand it before merging. You now own it, and you will be the one debugging it at 3 a.m.
The pattern in the right-hand column

Every one is a version of the same two acts: make the consequence visible to the person entitled to decide, and put it in writing.

Almost no engineering ethics dilemma requires heroism. It requires saying the uncomfortable thing once, clearly, early — and that is a learnable professional habit rather than a character trait.

Term bank

The vocabulary this primer has introduced
SDLC
The seven phases every piece of software goes through, however often you cycle through them.
waterfall
Each phase once, in order, signed off. Right only when requirements are genuinely fixed.
agile
Short cycles, working software each cycle, plan adjusted as you learn.

A cap on work in progress. Forces finishing over starting.
functional requirement
What the system must do. Testable as a yes/no.
non-functional requirement
How well — speed, scale, availability, security. Where architectures are decided.
user story
"As a … I want … so that …". The "so that" is the valuable part.
semantic versioning
MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH — a version number as a promise about breakage.
regression test
A check that a change did not break something that already worked.
squad
A cross-functional team with the roles needed to ship end to end.
client–server
The client asks, the server answers, and the server never trusts the client.
stateless
The server keeps nothing between requests, so each must carry its own identity.
API
A published contract of operations one program offers another.
IaaS / PaaS / SaaS
How much of the stack you still manage yourself.
stack
The set of technologies chosen to work together across the four layers.
runtime
The program that executes your program.
compiled / interpreted
Translated before running, or read and executed as it goes. Decides when you learn you were wrong.
transpile
Translate to another language — TypeScript to JavaScript, with the types deleted.
paradigm
How you organise instructions: imperative, object-oriented, functional, declarative.
null
The absence of a value. Not zero, and not empty.
pure function
Depends only on its inputs, changes nothing outside. Easy to test and reuse.
side effect
Anything a function does beyond returning a value.
encapsulation
Data private, access through methods that enforce the rules.
polymorphism
Different types answering the same call their own way. Extend by adding, not editing.
composition over inheritance
Assemble behaviour from parts rather than extending a chain.
SOLID
Five diagnostic principles for why code is painful to change.
architecture vs design
Decisions expensive to reverse, versus decisions inside them.
C4
Four zoom levels, one diagram each. The container view is the most useful diagram in software.
modular monolith
One deployable with strong internal boundaries. Often the right answer.
staging
A pre-production environment whose value equals how similar it is to production.
minimum cell size
The floor below which an aggregate identifies individuals.
purpose limitation
Data collected for one purpose may not silently be used for another.
