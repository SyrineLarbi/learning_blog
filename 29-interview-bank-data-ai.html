The single most useful thing in this volume

Almost every strong answer here names a way the result could be wrong. In data interviews, the difference between mid and senior is rarely knowledge of an algorithm — it is whether you volunteer the leakage risk, the base-rate problem or the missing baseline before being asked.

Part I

Statistics

Prep file 03 — describing data, inference, causation, base rates.

Topic 01

Describing data

Junior · a warm-up that still filters people

Mean or median for salary, and why?

Weak

"Mean, because it uses all the data."

Strong

"Median. Salary is right-skewed — a few very high values drag the mean upward, so the mean describes nobody. I would report both, plus the distribution or the percentiles, because a mean alone hides whether everyone is at four years' tenure or half are at one and half at eight. And I would never report a mean without saying what the spread is."

Follow-up

"What about an average satisfaction score of 1–5?" — that assumes the gap from 1 to 2 equals the gap from 4 to 5. It does not, so "3.7 average satisfaction" is a number with no defined meaning.

Topic 02

Inference

Mid · the question that reveals statistical maturity

Attrition went from 12% to 15%. Is that a problem?

Strong

"I cannot say yet, and there are three separate questions. First, is it outside normal variation — every process wobbles, so I would plot twenty-four months rather than two before deciding this is a change at all. Second, how big is the sample: three points on 800 people is worth investigating, on 20 people it is two extra leavers. Third, is it material — significance, materiality and stability are three different things, and with enough data almost everything is significant."

Follow-up

"What is a p-value?" — how surprising the data would be if there were no effect. Not the probability the effect is real, and not a measure of size.

Red flag

Slicing to a dramatic subgroup without mentioning cell size. Slice a few thousand employees three ways and you have cells of nine, where every big percentage is noise.

Topic 03

Correlation and causation

Senior · testing whether you can be trusted with a claim

Low satisfaction correlates with leaving. Can we say it causes it?

Strong

"No, and I can say why not, which matters more than the refusal. There are four alternatives. Reverse direction — the decision to leave may come first and depress the score. Confounding — department drives both, and that is the common case. Selection — surveys only contain people who responded, and leavers are systematically absent. And chance, with enough columns. So the defensible phrasing is 'employees with low satisfaction are 2.3 times more likely to leave in this data', and if we need the causal answer we need an experiment or a much more careful design."

Follow-up

"We targeted the worst teams and they improved — did our intervention work?" — regression to the mean. Extremes are partly luck and luck does not persist, so they would likely have improved anyway. That is what a control group is for.

Topic 04 · print this one

The base rate

Senior · the highest-signal question in the whole bank

Your model catches 90% of leavers and wrongly flags 10% of stayers. It flags someone. What is the chance they leave?

Weak

"90%." — the most common answer, and it confuses recall with precision.

Strong

"It depends entirely on the base rate. At 5% attrition, take 1,000 employees: 50 leave and 45 are caught; 950 stay and 95 are wrongly flagged. So 140 flags, of which 45 leave — about 32%. The model is good, and two thirds of the people it flags will stay. Nothing is wrong with it; the rare event does that. Which is exactly why a flag has to be framed as 'worth a conversation', never as 'will leave' — and why I would not report accuracy on this problem at all."

Follow-up

"So which metric?" — PR-AUC for ranking quality, and precision at the number of people a manager can actually talk to. That number is the metric's k.

Part II

Machine learning

Prep file 04 — framing, validation, leakage, evaluation, deployment.

Topic 05

Framing

Senior · the opener that sorts candidates immediately

HR asks you to "predict which employees will leave". Where do you start?

Weak

"I'd pull the HR data and try a few classifiers."

Strong

"By turning that into a decision. 'Predict attrition' is not a problem statement — 'give each manager five people worth a conversation this month' is, and it changes everything: it makes this a ranking problem rather than classification, it sets the metric to precision at five, and it tells me the cadence is monthly so batch scoring is fine. I would also ask what happens today without it, which is my baseline, and which error costs more. And I would check whether a rule — low satisfaction plus no promotion in three years — already solves most of it, because if it does, the model has to beat that and justify its maintenance."

Follow-up

"What if they say they want to know why people leave?" — that is a different problem entirely: explanation, not prediction. Feature importances are not causes.

Topic 06

Splits and validation

Mid · testing discipline rather than knowledge

Why three sets rather than two?

Strong

"Training to fit, validation to choose hyperparameters, and test to be touched once at the very end. The reason for the third is that every time you look at a set and change something, you leak information about it into your choices — do it ten times and the score is an optimistic fiction, because you have tuned to that sample. It is a discipline problem rather than a technical one, and it is the most commonly broken rule in applied ML."

Follow-up

"When must the split not be random?" — three cases: time-ordered data, grouped data where one person has many rows, and imbalanced classes, which need stratifying.

Topic 07 · the one that ends projects

Leakage

Senior · asked in every serious ML interview

You get 0.99 AUC predicting attrition. What is your first thought?

Strong

"That I have leaked something. On a human-behaviour problem that number is not a success, it is a bug report. The usual culprits in HR data are post-outcome fields — exit interview completed, handover uploaded, access revoked date, last day. All perfect predictors, and all known only after the decision. So I apply one test to every feature: would this value have existed, with this value, at the moment I need the prediction? Then I check feature importance — if one feature dominates absurdly, that is the leak."

Follow-up

"Name a leak that is not a feature." — fitting a scaler or an imputation median before splitting; a random split on time-ordered data; and the same employee appearing in both train and test.

Red flag

Being pleased with the number. That single reaction tells an interviewer how much production ML you have actually shipped.

Topic 08

Bias and variance

Mid · a classic, and easy to answer badly

Training accuracy 0.97, test accuracy 0.71. Diagnose it.

Strong

"Overfitting — the gap is the diagnosis. The model has memorised the training data including its noise. I would reach for more data first if it is available, then fewer features, regularisation, a simpler model, or early stopping, which is the simplest and most effective on a boosted model. And I would check that the split is honest before concluding anything, because a leaky split can produce the opposite pattern."

Follow-up

"What if test were higher than train?" — suspicious. Usually a leak, or an easier test split. Worth investigating rather than celebrating.

Topic 09

Algorithm choice

Mid–senior · testing judgement, not encyclopaedic recall

Tabular HR data, a few thousand rows, binary outcome. Which model?

Strong

"Logistic regression as the baseline, then gradient boosting — and on tabular data of that size boosting almost always wins, trains in seconds and tells me which features mattered. Not deep learning: with a few thousand rows it would be slower, worse and less explainable, because there is no hierarchical structure for it to discover. And the gap between the regression and the boosted model tells me whether the extra complexity is buying anything at all."

Follow-up

"Bagging versus boosting?" — many independent models averaged cuts variance (random forest); sequential models each fixing the last's errors cuts bias (XGBoost).

Red flag

Proposing a neural network for small tabular data, or an ensemble of five models for half a point of AUC that nobody can explain to the person it affects.

Topic 10

Evaluation

Mid · and a very common failure

Your model is 95% accurate. Is that good?

Strong

"Probably meaningless. If 95% of employees stay, predicting 'stays' for everyone scores 95% and is useless — so accuracy is the wrong metric on imbalanced data, and the first thing I need is the baseline. I would use PR-AUC rather than ROC-AUC because ROC looks flatteringly high when true negatives are abundant, and then precision and recall at a chosen threshold. And I would check calibration if a human is going to read the probability."

Follow-up

"Who picks the threshold?" — the business, from the cost asymmetry. Leaving it at 0.5 is not neutral; it is an unexamined choice.

Red flag

Reporting F1 alone — it hides which side you are failing on — or R² with no context.

Topic 11

Deployment and drift

Senior · separates notebook work from production experience

The model has been live for six months. How do you know it still works?

Strong

"I monitor four layers, and most teams only do the first. The service — latency and errors. The inputs — data drift, which is detectable immediately and without labels, so it is the most valuable monitor there is: a reorganisation changes every department code and the model degrades silently. The outputs — if suddenly 40% are high-risk where it was 8%, something upstream changed. And the outcomes — concept drift, which I only learn months later because attrition takes time to confirm, so accuracy is always measured in arrears."

Follow-up

"What is special about a retention model?" — it changes behaviour. Managers act on the flags, so future data reflects the interventions, and naive retraining slowly models your own actions. You need a holdout that was not acted on.

Red flag

"An ML system fails loudly like any service." It does the opposite: nothing errors, the dashboard is green, and the predictions are worthless.

LLMs, RAG and agents

Prep file 05 — mechanism, prompting, retrieval, tools, cost, trust.

Topic 12

What an LLM is

Mid · testing whether you understand the mechanism

Why do LLMs hallucinate, and can you stop it?

Strong

"Because the model produces plausible continuations. It has no lookup table, no truth flag and no reliable signal for 'I don't know' — an invented citation and a real one are produced by the same process and look identical to it. So it is structural, and you cannot prompt it away. You engineer around it: compute anything auditable elsewhere, ground it in retrieved context, constrain the output to a schema so there is no room to invent, verify claims programmatically, permit 'I don't know' explicitly, and show sources so a human can check."

Follow-up

"Why can't it count the letters in a word?" — it sees tokens, not characters. Not carelessness; it structurally cannot see what you are asking about.

Red flag

"We tell it to be accurate." The model is not withholding accuracy; it has no mechanism to comply.

Topic 13

Prompting

Mid · testing whether you know where the leverage is

The output is bad. What do you change first?

Strong

"Read the fully rendered prompt — the actual bytes sent, every variable interpolated. A surprising share of prompt bugs are visible immediately: an empty variable, a truncated document, retrieved chunks that are all irrelevant. Then I ask whether a competent human could do this task with only that context, because most bad output is missing information, not bad wording. Context is the biggest lever, then a constrained output shape, then examples for the edge cases. Rewording is the smallest lever and gets the most attention."

Follow-up

"What about 'think step by step'?" — obsolete on current models, which reason by default; explicit don't-think instructions actively degrade output.

Topic 14

Embeddings and RAG

Senior · the most useful RAG question there is

Your RAG system gives a wrong answer. How do you debug it?

Strong

"By separating four possible causes, because without that split it is guesswork. One — was the information in the corpus at all? Coverage. Two — was it in the retrieved set? Recall. Three — did it survive reranking? Precision. Four — did the model use what it was given? Faithfulness. Teams spend weeks tuning chunk size when the document was never ingested. And the cheapest quality win is unrelated to any of that: prepend the document title and section heading to every chunk, because a chunk saying 'must be approved by HR' is useless without knowing which policy it belongs to."

Follow-up

"When would you not build RAG?" — when the corpus fits in a cached prompt. No vector store, no chunking, no retrieval bugs. RAG earns its complexity when the corpus far exceeds the window, changes constantly, or needs per-user filtering — which HR documents do.

Red flag

Not knowing that embeddings fail on negation and exact identifiers — "invoices NOT paid" retrieves paid invoices, and a product code is semantically meaningless. That is why hybrid search exists.

Topic 15

Agents and tools

Senior · testing restraint as much as knowledge

When would you build an agent?

Strong

"Rarely, and the test is one question: can I enumerate the steps in advance? If yes, it is a pipeline — and I get determinism, per-step evaluation and lower cost for free. Only if the sequence genuinely cannot be enumerated does an agent earn its place, and then it needs an iteration cap, a token budget, a full trace, a context strategy because tool results accumulate, and tools scoped to the caller. Agents are the most over-applied pattern in the field; most of what people build as agents are pipelines wearing a costume."

Follow-up

"Does the model execute the tool?" — no. It requests a call; your code decides whether to comply. That gap is where all the security lives.

Topic 16

Cost and evaluation

Mid–senior · almost nobody has a good answer

How do you know a prompt change improved anything?

Strong

"A graded example set — twenty to fifty cases with programmatic checks where possible — run on every prompt, model or retrieval change, producing a score. Without it you cannot tell an improvement from a regression, so eventually nobody dares touch the prompt and a superstition stays frozen in production for a year. It is a day's work and it is the single biggest rung transition in AI engineering."

Follow-up

"And cost?" — output tokens cost several times input and dominate latency, so "answer in one sentence" is a real control. Caching a stable prefix is the first lever; a stateless API means a chat resends the whole history every turn, so cost grows quadratically.

Red flag

Verifying generated output with generated tests. Both encode the same guess and agree with each other.

Topic 17

Injection and trust

Senior · the security question for AI features

A candidate writes instructions into their CV and your screening tool reads it. What protects you?

Strong

"Not the prompt. Anything in the context can influence the output, so once a CV, a support ticket or a retrieved document enters the window, its author is speaking to my model. The only real defence is the trust boundary: whatever the model asks to do runs with the permissions of the person in the session, enforced in code. Then an injection that succeeds completely still cannot do anything that user could not already do. 'Ignore instructions in the document' is a mitigation, never the control."

Follow-up

"And what about the CV data itself?" — it left our boundary the moment it entered a prompt. For HR data that is a contractual and legal question: what is retained, where, for how long, and is it used for training.

Vocabulary

Prep file 14 — the compressed language that marks experience, and the emerging tech worth being able to discuss.

Topic 18

Terms they listen for

Why this is a technical skill

A term is compressed experience. Saying "that's data drift" transmits a whole failure mode, its cause and its detection in three words — to anyone who has the word. Without the vocabulary you cannot participate at speed even when you understand the concept perfectly, and that is most of what "sounds junior" means.

Used correctly, once, in a real answer — that is how they stick
base rate
How often the outcome actually occurs. Governs metric choice and every interpretation.
leakage
Training information unavailable at prediction time. Great scores, useless model.
data drift
Input distributions moving away from training. Detectable without labels.
concept drift
The relationship itself changing. Only visible once outcomes arrive.
training/serving skew
A feature computed differently in training and production.
point-in-time correctness
Using a feature's value as at the event, not as at now.
calibration
Whether a predicted 0.7 really means 70%. Required if a human reads it.
PR-AUC
The ranking metric for a rare positive class.
stratify
Preserving class proportions when splitting imbalanced data.
bagging / boosting
Averaging independent models (cuts variance) versus sequential correction (cuts bias).
uplift
Modelling who an intervention would change, not who will churn.
trust boundary
Model-requested actions running with the session user's permissions, in code.
eval set
Graded examples run on every change. The difference between engineering and vibes.
hybrid search
Keyword plus vector, because embeddings miss exact identifiers.
faithfulness
Whether the answer actually used the retrieved context.
minimum cell size
The privacy floor below which an aggregate identifies individuals.
Topic 19

Emerging tech

Senior · a "what are you tracking" question

Which emerging techniques matter for a system holding employee data?

Strong

"Two that pay off now and three worth tracking. Synthetic data is the immediately practical one — it lets us populate staging and demos without real employee records, which removes a genuine breach risk. And differential privacy, or at minimum enforced minimum cell sizes, on any published aggregate, because aggregation is not anonymisation: engagement for a three-person team identifies three people. Worth tracking but not building: federated learning, which is for parties who cannot pool data at all; homomorphic encryption, still too slow; and secure enclaves, which are available now and relevant if even the operator should not see the data."

Follow-up

"Quantum?" — one concrete implication: harvest-now-decrypt-later. HR data — health, disciplinary, salary history — needs confidentiality for decades, so the useful action is knowing where TLS is used, preferring managed TLS so the migration happens to us, and keeping cryptographic agility.

Red flag

Enthusiasm with no cost attached. Naming what you would not adopt, and why, is the senior half of this answer.

The red flags, collected

Celebrating 0.99

A suspiciously good result is a bug report. Being pleased with it is the single most revealing reaction in a data interview.

Accuracy on imbalanced data

Predict the majority class and score 95%. Quoting accuracy here suggests you have not shipped one.

Confusing recall with precision

"It catches 90%, so a flag is 90% likely" — the base-rate error, and it is extremely common.

Causal language from observational data

Especially when the stakeholder wants a cause. Name the four alternatives instead.

No baseline

Any model result quoted with nothing to compare it against.

Deep learning by default

Proposed for small tabular data. Slower, worse, less explainable.

Prompting away hallucination

"We tell it to be accurate." There is no mechanism to comply.

Prompt-level injection defence

Offering an instruction rather than a trust boundary enforced in code.

Agent-first design

Reaching for an agent where the steps can be enumerated.

No eval set

Improving a prompt "by feel". You cannot distinguish progress from regression.
