Topic 43

SQL for analysis: cohorts, funnels and time

The three shapes that answer most business questions
⭐ THE DISTINCTION FROM APPLICATION SQL: application queries
  FETCH ROWS; analytical queries RESHAPE THEM. Which is why
  window functions and CTEs matter far more here than joins do.

①  COHORTS — group by when they started, measure over time
   the shape: assign each person a cohort (hire month), then
   compute a metric per cohort per period since.
     · retention by tenure month
     · attrition by hire cohort — ⭐ which is how you separate
       "we got worse" from "we hired differently"
   ⚠ THE TRAP: an incomplete final cohort. The people hired
     last month cannot have twelve-month retention, so the
     latest cohort always looks different. Truncate or annotate.

②  FUNNELS — ordered stages with drop-off
   the shape: one row per person per stage reached, then count
   distinct at each.
   ⚠ THE TRAPS:
     · ORDER MATTERS AND PEOPLE SKIP STAGES. Counting each
       stage independently overstates completion.
     · THE TIME WINDOW. Someone who applied yesterday has not
       failed to be hired — ⭐ RIGHT-CENSORING is the most
       common funnel error.

③  TIME SERIES — change over periods
   the shape: `LAG()` for period-over-period, a rolling window
   for smoothing, a date spine so missing periods appear as zero
   rather than vanishing.
   ⚠ THE TRAPS:
     · ⭐ THE CLIFF AT THE END — the incomplete current period,
       the single most common false alarm in reporting
     · timezone boundaries moving events across days
     · comparing a 28-day month to a 31-day one without
       normalising

⭐ THE TOOLS THAT DO THE WORK, AND THE ONE GOTCHA
  ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY … ORDER BY …) — the standard
    way to take "the latest row per group", replacing a slow
    correlated subquery.
  LAG / LEAD — period-over-period without a self-join.
  SUM() OVER — running totals.
  ⚠ WINDOW FUNCTIONS RUN AFTER `WHERE`, so you cannot filter on
    a window result in the same clause. Wrap it in a CTE and
    filter outside. This catches everyone exactly once.

⚠ AND THE ARITHMETIC ERROR THAT PRODUCES CONFIDENT WRONG
  NUMBERS: FAN-OUT. Joining to a many-side multiplies rows, so
  any sum afterwards is inflated. ⭐ AGGREGATE THE MANY-SIDE
  FIRST, THEN JOIN — and sanity-check the row count before and
  after every join.
Topic 44

The statistics an analyst is asked about

The layer this adds

Not a statistics course — the six ideas that come up in analyst conversations, and the phrasing that makes each defensible.

The six
  • Signal versus noise. Every process varies. Before calling a move a change, plot enough history to know the normal range — you cannot answer "is this real" without knowing the ordinary variation.
  • Sample size and subgroup cells. Three points on 800 people is worth investigating; on 20 people it is two extra leavers. And slicing a few thousand employees three ways gives cells of nine, where every dramatic percentage is noise.
  • Significant, material and stable are three questions. With enough data almost everything is significant; only some of it is worth acting on; less of it is still there next month.
  • Effect size is what a decision uses. Three percentage points, eleven people, roughly £40,000 of replacement cost — not "significant at p < 0.01".
  • Simpson's paradox. The aggregate moves one way while every subgroup moves the other, because the mix changed. Report the aggregate alone and you can be wrong in front of a board. The fix is a rate-versus-mix decomposition.
  • Regression to the mean. Target the worst-performing teams, intervene, measure again — they improve, and so would they have. This is why a comparison group matters even when it feels unnecessary.
Where it goes wrong
  • Reporting a mean alone on skewed data — salary, tenure, duration are all right-skewed, so the median and the distribution are the honest summary.
  • Describing a p-value as the probability the effect is real.
  • Averaging an ordinal satisfaction score and treating the result as a quantity.
  • Slicing until something looks dramatic, then presenting it.
The question you must be able to answer

"Attrition is up three points. Is that a problem?" — the strong answer is three questions, not a yes: is it outside normal variation, is the sample big enough, and is it material. Then the honest offer: here is what I would look at to find out.

Topic 45

Metric definition, and the numbers that disagree

The layer this adds

The most common analytics failure is not a wrong calculation. It is two dashboards showing different numbers for the same thing, and nobody able to say which is right — which destroys trust in the whole reporting layer.

What a definition must pin down
  • The numerator and the denominator. Attrition rate over what — headcount at the start, the average over the period, or the end? All three are used, and they differ.
  • The time window and the timezone. A leaver on the 31st at 23:00 in one timezone is next month's in another.
  • The inclusions and exclusions. Contractors, interns, transfers, people who left in probation, regretted versus non-regretted. The regretted split is usually the whole point — total turnover and "people we wanted to keep" are different numbers with different actions.
  • The grain. One row per what — per employee, per employment spell, per month?
  • The as-at date. Whether the number is recomputed from current data or frozen when published. If it is recomputed, last quarter's report changes after a reorganisation — which is a data-integrity failure that presents as a reporting bug.

And then it lives in one place. Defined once in the modelling layer and built on, not re-implemented in four dashboards — because four implementations will eventually be four definitions.

When two numbers disagree — the reconciliation order
  1. Compare the definitions before the data. Most disagreements are definitional, and checking that first saves hours.
  2. Compare the row counts, then the grain. A fan-out join explains a surprising number of inflated figures.
  3. Compare the time windows and timezones.
  4. Compare the filters — especially a soft-delete flag one query applied and the other did not.
  5. Reconcile to a source nobody in the argument controls — payroll, the HRIS itself. A number that matches an independent system is defensible; one that matches only your own pipeline is not.
The question you must be able to answer

"Finance says headcount is 4,012 and your dashboard says 3,987. Who is right?" — the answer is a process, not a number: compare definitions, then grain, then filters, then reconcile to the system of record. And the outcome should be one agreed definition, not a winner.

Topic 46

Power BI for a SQL-fluent engineer

The model that makes DAX comprehensible
⭐ THE ONE CONCEPT: EVERYTHING IN POWER BI IS ABOUT FILTER
  CONTEXT. A measure is not a value — it is a calculation
  evaluated against whatever filters apply at that point in the
  visual. Once that clicks, DAX stops being arbitrary.

THE MODEL, IN SQL TERMS
  · TABLES with RELATIONSHIPS — a star schema, with fact tables
    and dimension tables. ⭐ AND THE DEFAULT RELATIONSHIP IS
    ONE-DIRECTIONAL, which is why a filter sometimes does not
    propagate the way you expect.
  · A DATE DIMENSION is not optional. Time intelligence
    functions require one, and its absence is why
    year-over-year comparisons behave oddly.
  · CALCULATED COLUMN vs MEASURE: a column is computed per row
    at refresh and stored; a measure is computed at query time
    against the filter context. ⚠ CHOOSING A COLUMN WHERE YOU
    NEEDED A MEASURE IS THE MOST COMMON MODELLING MISTAKE, and
    it produces totals that do not aggregate correctly.

THE FUNCTIONS THAT DO THE WORK
  CALCULATE     ⭐ the central one: evaluate an expression with
                a modified filter context. Almost every
                non-trivial measure is a CALCULATE.
  ALL / REMOVEFILTERS  ignore filters — how you compute a
                percentage of total.
  FILTER        row-by-row restriction; powerful and slower.
  time intelligence — same period last year, year to date,
                rolling periods. Needs the date dimension.

IMPORT vs DIRECTQUERY
  IMPORT        data copied into the model. Fast, and stale
                until refresh.
  DIRECTQUERY   queries the source live. Current, and every
                interaction is a query — ⭐ SO YOUR INDEXES AND
                YOUR QUERY SHAPES ARE NOW A DASHBOARD
                PERFORMANCE PROBLEM.
  ⇒ for HR reporting, import with a scheduled refresh is
    usually right: the data changes daily at most.

⚠ AND THE THING THAT MATTERS MOST IN AN HR CONTEXT:
  ROW-LEVEL SECURITY. Defined in the model, so a regional
  manager's report shows their scope and not the company's.
  ⭐ WITHOUT IT, PUBLISHING A DASHBOARD IS PUBLISHING THE WHOLE
    DATASET — and a filter is not a permission.
  ⇒ and the minimum-cell-size rule applies here too: a
    three-person team's engagement score identifies three
    people.
Topic 47

Experiments and causal claims

The layer this adds

The line between "we observed" and "we caused" — and what you can honestly claim without an experiment, which in HR analytics is usually where you are.

The mechanism
  • Randomisation is what buys causation. It makes the groups comparable on everything, including things you did not measure. Without it, any difference has three other explanations.
  • The four alternatives to causation, always: reverse direction, a confounder, selection in the data, chance. Being able to rule them out — or admit you cannot — is causal honesty.
  • Decide the analysis before looking. The metric, the duration, the stopping rule, the subgroups. Stopping as soon as it reaches significance biases the result upward, and testing twenty things guarantees roughly one false positive.
  • A holdout group, even when it feels unnecessary. Without one you cannot claim the effect, and someone will eventually ask you to.
  • When you cannot randomise — which is most HR interventions — the honest options are a difference-in-differences against a comparable group, or a before/after with the seasonality and the regression-to-the-mean risk explicitly stated. Both are weaker, and saying so is the point.
Where it goes wrong
  • Targeting the worst performers, seeing improvement, and claiming the intervention worked.
  • Selection: surveying current employees to explain why people left — those people are gone.
  • Choosing the subgroup after seeing the results.
  • Claiming causation because the stakeholder wants a cause, which is the pressure that produces most overclaiming.
The question you must be able to answer

"We ran the retention programme and attrition fell. Did it work?" — the strong answer names what else changed, asks whether there was a comparison group, and offers the honest phrasing: "attrition fell by X in the treated group and Y in the untreated; that difference is what we can attribute."

Topic 48

The analyst interview and positioning

The positioning that works

An engineer who can do analysis is a rarer and more valuable profile than either alone — you can build the pipeline, define the metric and defend the number. Lead with the intersection, not with a weaker version of one side.

The SQL round

Cohorts, funnels, window functions. Say the fan-out risk before it happens and name the incomplete-period trap unprompted. Those two remarks do more than any query.

The case round

"Attrition is up — what do you do?" They want the process: define the metric, check the period is complete, decompose rate versus mix, size the effect, then a recommendation with its uncertainty stated.

The communication round

Answer first, in people and money. One annotated chart. What you are not claiming. And a recommendation, because options without one is abdication.

The domain advantage

Supply chain plus HR plus engineering is an unusual combination. The intersection is where you are hard to replace, and it is something you already have rather than something to acquire.

The red flag to avoid

Presenting the journey — the cleaning, the models tried — instead of the answer. And a confusion matrix on a slide for a non-technical audience.

Topic 40

The legacy migration playbook

The layer this adds

The Retool migration is the CV claim, and the question it invites is how you replace a live system without a freeze and without a big-bang cutover — because that is the only version of this that works.

The playbook
  1. Inventory what it actually does, including the undocumented behaviour people depend on. The ugliness of a legacy system is mostly accumulated knowledge — every strange conditional is a bug someone hit or a regulation.
  2. Find the seam. Route traffic through something you control — a proxy, a facade, a feature flag — so you can redirect one path at a time.
  3. Strangler fig, route by route. Move the least risky path first, verify, then the next. Incremental, and reversible per route.
  4. Run both and compare. Shadow the new implementation, log both outputs, diff them. This is the step that finds the undocumented behaviour, and it is the one people skip.
  5. Migrate data with a reconciliation, not a trust exercise: counts, sums, and spot checks against the old system.
  6. Decommission deliberately, with a date and a rollback path, and only after the old path has been unused for a while.
Why rewrites fail
  • You must maintain two systems while the new one catches up to a moving target — and the feature freeze that would prevent it is usually politically impossible.
  • The specification does not exist; the old system is the specification, and it is only readable by running it.
  • The last 10% of edge cases is more than half the work, and it is invisible at the start.
  • A big-bang cutover has no rollback that anyone would actually take.
The question you must be able to answer

"How did you know the new system behaved the same?" — the strong answer is the shadow-and-diff step, and a specific example of a behaviour it caught that nobody had documented.

Topic 41

Capacity math and runnable trade-offs

The arithmetic that stops you over-building
⭐ DO THE NUMBERS BEFORE THE ARCHITECTURE, EVERY TIME. The
  number chooses the design, and most systems are far smaller
  than they feel.

THE FOUR CALCULATIONS
  ①  REQUESTS PER SECOND AT PEAK
     a day is ~90,000 seconds. 4,000 employees, most opening a
     dashboard once a day, is single-digit requests per second
     — with a working-day spike of maybe 5–10×, because
     everyone opens it at 9 a.m.
     ⇒ ⭐ THAT IS A SMALL NUMBER. One ordinary database handles
       it, and knowing this is what stops you proposing
       Kubernetes.

  ②  DATA PER YEAR
     rows × bytes. The point is the order of magnitude:
     megabytes (irrelevant), gigabytes (fine), terabytes (now
     storage layout matters).
     ⇒ 4,000 employees with monthly history is thousands of
       rows a year. It fits comfortably in Postgres.

  ③  THE READ-TO-WRITE RATIO
     ⭐ DETERMINES MORE OF THE ARCHITECTURE THAN ANY OTHER
       NUMBER, and it is the one nobody asks for. Thousands of
       reads per write means caching and precomputation are the
       whole answer.

  ④  COST PER OPERATION, FOR ANYTHING METERED
     an LLM call over every employee has a price. ⭐ "$0.004
     PER REQUEST, $240 A MONTH AT CURRENT VOLUME" IS REAL
     ENGINEERING and almost nobody brings it.

THE LATENCY INTUITIONS TO CARRY
  memory                nanoseconds        free
  local network hop     well under 1 ms    cheap
  simple DB query       1–10 ms            counts
  cross-region trip     50–150 ms          expensive
  LLM call              1–5 s              ⭐ A DIFFERENT UNIT
  ⇒ AND THE ONE THAT EXPLAINS MOST SLOWNESS: 50 sequential
    queries at 5 ms is 250 ms of pure waiting. ROUND TRIPS,
    NOT CPU, make ordinary software slow.

⭐ THE SENTENCE THAT SCORES: "At our volume this is one
  Postgres and one container. Here is the arithmetic. What
  would change my mind is X." — a number, a design, and a
  named condition for revisiting it.
Topic 42

From manager to tech lead

Why this topic is here

A prior management role is an unusual asset and an easy thing to present badly. The tech-lead role is not "manager who codes" — it is technical judgement exercised through other people, and the transferable half of management experience is specific.

What transfers, and what does not
  • Transfers well: running a decision to a conclusion rather than letting it drift · translating between a business need and a technical constraint · giving feedback without drama · noticing when someone is stuck before they say so · estimating with conditions attached · writing so people act.
  • Does not transfer: authority. A tech lead has influence and technical credibility, not a reporting line — so every decision has to be argued rather than announced, and that is a genuinely different skill.
  • Has to be rebuilt: hands-on depth. The credibility is technical, and it is why the other nine volumes exist.
  • The two moves that define the role: making other people's work better through review and design conversations, and building the thing that makes everyone faster and safer — the shared eval harness, the one path every query goes through, the constraint that removes a bug family permanently.
How it gets presented badly
  • Leading with the management title for an engineering role, which invites the question of whether you still build.
  • Describing team outcomes without your own contribution — "we" with no "I", which cannot be scored.
  • Treating the move as a step down, which it is not: individual contributor and management are parallel tracks, not a sequence, and moving between them is normal in both directions.
  • Not naming what you deliberately stopped doing to go deep technically.
The question you must be able to answer

"You managed people, and now you want a hands-on role. Why?" — a direct answer about where you want your judgement applied, plus evidence of the technical depth rebuilt. The evidence is the answer; the narrative alone is not.
