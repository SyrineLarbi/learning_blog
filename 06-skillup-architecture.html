Part I

System design

Not a talent you either have or don't. A process with an order, and the order is most of the skill.

Design 01

The process

① REQUIREMENTS — and write them where everyone can see them FUNCTIONAL what must it do? three to five things. NON-FUNCTIONAL how many users · how fast · how fresh · how available · how private ⭐ AND EXPLICITLY: WHAT IS OUT OF SCOPE. Cutting scope in the first five minutes is the most senior move available, and the one most often skipped. ② ESTIMATE — before you draw anything users → requests per second → data per day → data per year. Round violently and say you are rounding. ⭐ THE NUMBER CHOOSES THE DESIGN. Fifty requests a second is one boring database. Getting this backwards — design first, justify later — is how teams build distributed systems for problems that fit on a laptop. ③ THE BOX DIAGRAM — start small, add under pressure client → api → store, plus only what is needed. ⚠ eleven boxes in minute four, none of which you can justify, reads as inexperience rather than ambition. ④ THE DATA MODEL — ⭐ the highest-signal part the entities, the relationships, and the ACCESS PATTERNS. "we read this a thousand times per write" changes the design more than any box on the diagram. ⑤ THE MAIN FLOW — walk ONE request end to end, in words what is written, what is read, what is cached, what is queued, and what the user sees at each moment. ⑥ FAILURE — ⭐ where seniority is most visible what happens when each box is down or slow? which parts must be correct and which may be stale? what does the user experience during a partial outage? ⚠ this is also where people run out of time. Protect it. ⑦ SCALE THE BOTTLENECK — the one thing that breaks first not everything. Scaling everything is not a design, it is a list of technologies. ⑧ TRADE-OFFS AND WHAT'S NEXT what you would build first, what you deliberately left out, what breaks at 10× and what you would do then. ⭐ THE STRUCTURE IS THE SCORE — in an interview and in a design review. Two engineers with identical knowledge produce different outcomes because one followed this order and one free-associated.
Design 02

The building blocks

BlockSolvesCosts
Load balancerSpreads traffic; removes a single point of failure at the app tierSticky sessions become a problem; health checks must be honest, or it routes to a broken instance forever.
CacheRead speed and load reductionStaleness and invalidation — a correctness problem you have chosen to take on. Ask first: how wrong may this be, and for how long?
Read replicaRead capacityReplication lag. Write then immediately read, and the user does not see their own change. The classic ghost bug.
QueueAbsorbs spikes, decouples, enables retriesThe work is now asynchronous: no immediate answer, no guaranteed order, possible double delivery, and you must design the dead-letter path and the progress UI.
Object storageLarge files, cheaply, foreverNot a filesystem. And a URL is a credential — see Volume VII on access.
CDNLatency for cacheable contentInvalidation, and it does nothing for personalised or authenticated responses.
Search indexText queries a database does badlyA second copy of your data to keep in step. Always eventually consistent with the source.
Worker poolLong jobs outside the request cycleNow you own scheduling, retries, idempotency, visibility and cost.
The sentence that scores

"I'd add a cache here — reads outnumber writes heavily and this data may be a minute stale. What it costs me is a stale window after an edit, so I'd invalidate on write and accept the rare race."

Lever · reason · cost · mitigation. Four clauses. Naming the cost is what turns a default into a decision, and it is the single most reliable marker of experience in a design conversation.

Design 03

The trade-offs

Five axes, and neither end is "correct"
CONSISTENCY  ←→  AVAILABILITY
  must every reader see the latest write, or may some see a
  slightly old value in exchange for staying up?
  ⇒ money and permissions: consistency. A dashboard count:
    availability. ⭐ ASK PER FEATURE, not per system.

LATENCY  ←→  THROUGHPUT
  batching raises throughput and raises latency. Small requests
  do the reverse. You cannot maximise both.

SPACE  ←→  TIME
  indexes, caches, precomputed aggregates and denormalised
  copies all spend storage to buy speed.

SIMPLICITY  ←→  FLEXIBILITY
  ⭐ THE MOST OFTEN MISJUDGED. Every abstraction added "for
    future flexibility" is complexity paid today for an option
    that usually expires unused. Pay it when the second real
    case arrives, not the first imagined one.

BUILD  ←→  BUY
  building costs engineering time forever, not once. Buying
  costs money and control. ⚠ and the honest question is not
  cost — it is whether this thing is your differentiator.

⭐ THE META-POINT: A DESIGN IS A SET OF CHOSEN COSTS. When
  someone asks "is this a good architecture?", the answerable
  version is "good for what?" — and the design document should
  say which trade-offs it took and why, because the next person
  will otherwise re-litigate them from scratch.
Design 04

Designing for scale

Saturates firstSymptomWhat helps
Database connectionsEverything slows at once, even trivial endpointsPooling, shorter transactions, moving long work out of the request. Adding app servers makes it worse — the counter-intuitive one worth knowing.
One queryOne endpoint slow, rest fineAn index or a rewrite. Cheapest fix available; look here first.
Round tripsSlowness proportional to items on screenFetch in one query, or in parallel. The N+1 pattern.
App CPUDegrades smoothly with trafficAdd instances. The easy case, and genuinely rare in business software.
A downstream serviceYour latency tracks theirs exactlyTimeouts, circuit breaker, cache, or degrade without it. You cannot fix their speed; you can stop it becoming your outage.
Lock contentionFine, then collapses past a threshold; worse with more workersShorter transactions, finer locks, remove the shared row.
Queue depthNothing is slow — things are just late, and getting laterMore consumers or less work per item. Arrival rate exceeds service rate; waiting never fixes it.
Vertical before horizontal, always

A bigger machine is one afternoon. A distributed system is a permanent tax — on debugging, on transactions, on deployment, on every new engineer. Modern hardware is enormous: a single well-indexed Postgres handles workloads most teams never approach.

So the honest sequence is: fix the query, add the index, cache the hot read, get a bigger box, add a read replica — and only then consider splitting anything. Sharding proposed before estimating is a tell.

Design 05

Drawing and communicating it

A diagram is an argument, not decoration
THE THREE ZOOM LEVELS, AND ONE DIAGRAM PER LEVEL
  CONTEXT     your system as one box, with the users and the
              external systems around it. For non-technical
              stakeholders.
  CONTAINERS  ⭐ THE MOST USEFUL ONE. The deployable pieces —
              frontend, backend, AI service, database — and the
              protocols between them. This is the diagram your
              team actually needs, and the one Volume II of the
              onboarding handbook draws.
  COMPONENTS  inside one container. Only for the part under
              discussion.

⚠ THE MISTAKE: ONE DIAGRAM AT ALL THREE LEVELS. It becomes
  unreadable and is never updated, so it becomes wrong, and then
  it actively misleads.

WHAT MAKES A DIAGRAM USEFUL
  · LABEL THE ARROWS with the protocol AND the direction —
    "HTTP/JSON, sync" versus "event, async" is the most
    information-dense thing on the page
  · mark what is SYNCHRONOUS: ⭐ every sync arrow is a
    dependency that can make you slow or down
  · mark TRUST BOUNDARIES — where data crosses from a system you
    control to one you do not
  · include the things that fail: the queue, the third party,
    the cache
  ⚠ omit: every class, every table, and anything that will be
    stale next week

⭐ AND THE HABIT THAT MATTERS MOST: WRITE THE DECISION DOWN
  ALONGSIDE IT. A diagram shows what you built; only prose can
  say why, and what you rejected. That is reflex 15.
Part II

Design patterns

Named solutions to recurring problems. Useful as vocabulary; dangerous as a shopping list.

Patterns 06

What they are, and are not

The honest framing

A pattern is a name for a shape that keeps appearing. Its value is almost entirely communication: "that's a strategy, not a switch statement" replaces ten minutes of explanation.

What patterns are not: a checklist of things a good codebase contains, or a quality signal. Recognising a pattern that already exists in your code is a senior skill. Reaching for one before the problem has appeared twice is the classic mid-level failure — and generated code is unusually prone to it.

Patterns 07

The ones worth knowing

PatternThe problem it solvesRecognise it by
FactoryChoosing which implementation to create, without callers knowing the optionsA function that returns one of several types based on input. In this stack: picking a notification channel.
SingletonExactly one instance of an expensive shared thingA connection pool, a loaded model. Your framework already does this — NestJS providers are singletons, which is why you never call new PrismaClient().
StrategySeveral interchangeable algorithms chosen at runtimeSwappable scoring rules, pricing rules, export formats. Replaces a growing switch statement.
AdapterMaking a third party fit your interfaceThe most valuable one in practice. Wrap the LLM provider, the storage client, the payment gateway — then swapping vendors touches one file.
DecoratorAdding behaviour without changing the thingLogging, caching, retries around a call. Literally the @decorator syntax in NestJS.
ObserverMany things reacting to one eventEvent emitters, WebSocket broadcasts, React state subscriptions.
RepositoryIsolating data access behind an interfaceA service that owns all queries for one entity. Partly redundant with an ORM — Prisma is already this layer, so a repository over it is often ceremony.
FacadeOne simple entry point over a complicated subsystemA service method that orchestrates four others so callers need not know the sequence.

Patterns 09

Anti-patterns

God object

One class or service that does everything. The symptom: every change touches it, and two people always conflict there.

Anaemic domain

Data classes with no behaviour, and all logic in one "manager". Common with ORMs, and it scatters the rules that belong together.

Premature abstraction

An interface with one implementation, a factory for one type, config nobody sets. Duplication is cheaper than the wrong abstraction — wait for the third case.

Shotgun surgery

One conceptual change requires edits in fifteen files. The real measure of coupling, and the health metric worth watching.

Magic strings and numbers

status === 'APPROVED_2'. A literal union or an enum makes the typo a compile error instead of a silent permission failure.

Stringly typed

Passing ids, dates and money as bare strings. Distinct types make the wrong argument order unrepresentable.

Patterns 10

When not to use one

Three tests before adding a pattern
①  HAS THE PROBLEM ACTUALLY HAPPENED — TWICE?
   ⭐ once is a case. Twice is a pattern. Zero times is a
     prediction, and predictions about future flexibility have
     a poor record.

②  DOES IT MAKE THE CODE EASIER TO READ FOR SOMEONE NEW?
   if a reader must now open four files to follow one request,
   the pattern has cost more than it bought. ⚠ INDIRECTION IS A
   REAL COST paid by every future reader.

③  COULD A PLAIN FUNCTION DO THIS?
   very often, yes. A named function is a pattern with no
   ceremony, and it is the right answer more often than any
   named pattern.

⭐ THE JUDGEMENT, PLAINLY: PATTERNS ARE FOR MANAGING VARIATION
  THAT EXISTS. Where nothing varies, a pattern is decoration
  with a maintenance cost — and a codebase full of decorative
  patterns is harder to change than one written plainly, which
  is the exact opposite of the intent.
Part III

Architecture paradigms

Whole-application shapes. Each buys one specific thing — and the skill is knowing what, and whether you need it.

Paradigms 11

Layered

controller ← HTTP only ▼ service ← business rules ▼ repository ← data access ▼ database ⭐ WHAT IT BUYS: predictability. Everyone knows where anything goes, and a new feature is a copy of an existing shape rather than a design decision. This is what NestJS imposes, and it is the correct default for most applications. THE ONE RULE THAT MAKES IT WORK ⭐ DEPENDENCIES POINT DOWNWARD ONLY. A service never knows about HTTP; a repository never knows about business rules. ⇒ the test from the onboarding handbook: could this service method be called from a cron job with no request in sight? If not, HTTP has leaked downward. ⚠ WHERE IT STRAINS · business logic drifts into controllers (thin controllers is a discipline, not a guarantee) · a "service" that is really four features becomes a god object · with an ORM, the repository layer is often redundant ceremony over Prisma ⭐ AND THE HONEST NOTE THAT FRAMES THE REST OF THIS PART: LAYERED IS ENOUGH FOR THE LARGE MAJORITY OF APPLICATIONS. Everything below is a response to a specific pain. If you do not have the pain, the paradigm is cost without benefit.
Paradigms 12

Clean and hexagonal

Same core idea, two names
THE IDEA: INVERT THE DEPENDENCY ON INFRASTRUCTURE.

  layered:      business logic → database
  clean/hex:    business logic ← database
                (the database implements an interface the
                 business logic defines)

              ┌─────────────────────────┐
   HTTP ──────┤  adapter                │
   CLI  ──────┤   ↕                     │
              │  DOMAIN (pure rules)    │
              │   ↕                     │
   Postgres ──┤  adapter                │
   S3       ──┤                         │
              └─────────────────────────┘
  "ports and adapters": the domain declares PORTS; infrastructure
  provides ADAPTERS.

⭐ WHAT IT BUYS
  · the rules are testable with no database, no HTTP, no
    framework — genuinely fast, genuinely isolated tests
  · infrastructure becomes swappable: a different store, a
    different provider, touches one adapter
  · the domain does not age with the framework

⚠ WHAT IT COSTS — and this is why it is not the default
  · MORE FILES AND MORE INDIRECTION for the same feature
  · mapping between domain objects and database rows, by hand,
    forever — which duplicates what your ORM already does
  · a team that half-adopts it produces the worst of both: the
    ceremony without the isolation

⭐ THE HONEST TEST: DO YOU HAVE COMPLEX RULES THAT ARE PAINFUL
  TO TEST BECAUSE OF INFRASTRUCTURE? If yes, this pays for
  itself quickly. If your services are mostly validate-and-save,
  it is pure overhead — and CRUD dressed as hexagonal
  architecture is a common and expensive mistake.
Paradigms 13

Domain-driven design

The parts that are useful without adopting the whole thing
⭐ UBIQUITOUS LANGUAGE — the highest-value idea in DDD, and it
  is free.
  the code uses the words the business uses. If HR says
  "regretted attrition", the field is not `left_flag`.
  ⇒ this eliminates an entire class of bug: the translation
    error between what a stakeholder asked for and what was
    built. Adopt this even if you adopt nothing else.

BOUNDED CONTEXT — the second most useful
  the same word means different things in different parts of the
  business. An "employee" to payroll (a cost, a tax code) is not
  an "employee" to recruiting (a candidate who converted).
  ⭐ FORCING ONE MODEL ACROSS BOTH PRODUCES A TABLE WITH FORTY
    NULLABLE COLUMNS AND NO CLEAR OWNER. Two contexts, each with
    its own model, is the answer — and it is also the only
    defensible basis for splitting services.

ENTITY vs VALUE OBJECT
  ENTITY        has identity that persists through change — an
                employee is the same employee after a rename
  VALUE OBJECT  defined entirely by its values — money, a date
                range, an address. Interchangeable if equal.
  ⇒ practical payoff: value objects can validate themselves, so
    an invalid date range becomes unrepresentable.

AGGREGATE
  a cluster of objects changed together, with one root as the
  only entry point. ⭐ THE AGGREGATE IS THE TRANSACTION BOUNDARY
  — which is the genuinely useful part: it tells you what must
  be consistent at once and what may be eventually consistent.

⚠ WHAT TO SKIP UNLESS THE DOMAIN IS GENUINELY COMPLEX: the full
  apparatus — repositories per aggregate, domain events for
  everything, a separate application layer. DDD earns its
  ceremony on complicated business rules, not on CRUD.
Paradigms 14

Event sourcing and CQRS

Event sourcingCQRS
The ideaStore the events, not the current state. Current state is derived by replaying them.Separate the write model from the read model. Different shapes, possibly different stores.
Buys youA complete audit trail by construction · time travel ("what was true then?") · the ability to answer questions you had not thought of yetReads and writes scale and are modelled independently. Complex reporting stops distorting the write schema.
Costs youA great deal. Every query needs a projection · events are immutable so a mistake is permanent · schema evolution of old events · no simple "just look at the table"Eventual consistency between the two models — the user writes and does not immediately see it. Plus two models to keep in step.
Worth it whenThe history is the product: finance, audit, compliance, anything where "how did we get here" is a required question.Read and write loads are genuinely different in shape and volume — reporting versus transactions.
The realistic middle

You almost certainly want an append-only audit log alongside normal tables, not event sourcing. That gives you the history and the "what was true then" answer, while keeping the ability to read current state with one obvious query.

Similarly: a read replica, a materialised view or a reporting table gives you most of CQRS's benefit without maintaining two models. Take the property you need, not the paradigm's name.

Paradigms 15

Architecture decision records

One page per decision, written when you make it
TITLE     ADR-014: Use Prisma rather than raw SQL for the API
STATUS    accepted | superseded by ADR-031 | deprecated
CONTEXT   what was true when we decided — the constraints, the
          team, the deadline, what we knew and did not know
DECISION  what we chose, in one paragraph
ALTERNATIVES  what else we considered, and why not
CONSEQUENCES  ⭐ THE MOST VALUABLE SECTION: what this makes
          easy, what it makes hard, and what we accept

⭐ WHY THIS IS WORTH THE FIFTEEN MINUTES
  the code shows WHAT you built. Only this shows WHY, and
  crucially WHAT YOU REJECTED. Without it, every new engineer
  re-litigates settled decisions from scratch — and sometimes
  reverses one for a reason that was already considered and
  rejected.

⭐ AND THE PART PEOPLE MISS: A SUPERSEDED ADR IS NOT DELETED.
  It is marked superseded and linked to its replacement. The
  record of a decision you later reversed is often the most
  useful document in the repository, because it explains why the
  obvious approach did not work.

WHEN TO WRITE ONE
  · you chose between real alternatives
  · you accepted a known cost deliberately
  · someone will ask "why is it like this?" in six months
  ⚠ NOT for every commit. An ADR for a naming choice devalues
    the format.
Part IV

API design

An API is a promise you must keep after you have forgotten making it — and after the people relying on it have forgotten too.

API 16

Beyond REST basics

The questions the basics don't cover
①  IS IT SAFE TO CALL TWICE?
   ⭐ THE MOST IMPORTANT API QUESTION. Reads are naturally safe;
     creates are not. Anything that moves money, sends a
     message or triggers a job needs a CALLER-SUPPLIED
     IDEMPOTENCY KEY, so the second attempt returns the first
     result rather than doing it again.
   ⚠ NETWORKS RETRY WHETHER OR NOT YOU DESIGNED FOR IT. Users
     double-click, phones re-send, proxies repeat. This is not
     a hypothetical.

②  WHAT DOES PARTIAL SUCCESS LOOK LIKE?
   a bulk endpoint that updates 40 of 50 records must be able to
   SAY SO. Returning 200 with no detail, or 500 after committing
   40, are both lies.
   ⇒ return per-item results, or make it all-or-nothing. Choose,
     and document which.

③  WHAT CAN NEVER CHANGE?
   removing a field · renaming one · narrowing a type · making
   an optional field required → all BREAKING.
   adding an optional field → not breaking.
   ⭐ KNOW WHICH CATEGORY YOU ARE IN BEFORE SHIPPING. It decides
     whether this is a deploy or a migration with notice.

④  WHOSE DATA CAN THIS REACH?
   every endpoint taking an id must answer: what if the id
   belongs to someone else? In code, on every path.

⑤  HOW LONG CAN IT TAKE?
   anything unbounded — a report, an import, a model call over
   many records — does not belong in a request. Accept it,
   return a handle, do the work elsewhere.

⑥  WHAT DOES AN ERROR LOOK LIKE?
   a stable machine-readable code, a human message, and which
   field was wrong. ⚠ errors are part of the interface; changing
   an error code breaks callers as surely as removing a field.
API 17

Pagination

StrategyHowTrade-off
Offset / limit?page=3&size=20Simple, jumpable, and wrong under writes — an insert shifts everything, so page 2 repeats or skips rows. Also gets slower the deeper you go: the database must count past every skipped row.
Cursor / keyset?after=<opaque>Stable and fast at any depth. No page numbers and no jumping to page 47. The right default for feeds and large lists.
Page tokensAn opaque token encoding position and filtersCursor's benefits, and you can change the internals later without breaking callers. Slightly more work.
The decision you cannot defer

The response shape is a contract. An endpoint that returns a bare array cannot later gain a { data, nextCursor, total } wrapper without breaking every caller.

So paginate from the first commit, even when there are nine rows. Return the envelope on day one. Retrofitting pagination is one of the most common breaking changes teams are forced into, and it is entirely avoidable.

API 18

Versioning

Three approaches, and the one that matters more than the choice
URL PATH        /v1/employees · /v2/employees
                ⭐ obvious, cacheable, greppable, trivially
                  testable in a browser. THE PRACTICAL DEFAULT.
HEADER          Accept: application/vnd.api.v2+json
                cleaner URLs, and invisible — easy to get wrong
                and hard to debug from a browser.
QUERY PARAM     ?version=2
                easy, and easy to forget. Weakest option.

⭐ AND THE THING THAT MATTERS MORE THAN WHICH: HAVING A
  DEPRECATION POLICY AT ALL.
  · announce · a date · a Deprecation or Sunset header on
    responses · monitoring of who is still calling the old
    version · then remove it
  ⚠ WITHOUT REMOVAL YOU HAVE NOT VERSIONED — you have
    accumulated. Three live versions means every bug fixed three
    times, and it is how an API becomes unmaintainable.

⭐ THE BETTER STRATEGY: DON'T VERSION. ADD.
  most changes can be additive — a new optional field, a new
  endpoint alongside the old. ⇒ RESERVE A VERSION BUMP FOR A
  GENUINELY BREAKING CHANGE, and you may never need v2.

FOR AN INTERNAL API — the honest note
  when the frontend and backend deploy together, versioning is
  often ceremony. What you need instead is the DISCIPLINE OF
  KNOWING which changes are breaking — and a plan for the window
  where old clients are still running during a rollout.
API 19

Rate limiting

Protecting the service from its callers, including yourself
WHY: one buggy client, one retry storm, or one enthusiastic
integration can consume the capacity of every other caller. Rate
limiting is how a shared service stays fair.

THE ALGORITHMS, briefly
  FIXED WINDOW    "100 per minute". Simple. ⚠ allows a burst of
                  200 across a window boundary.
  SLIDING WINDOW  smoother, more bookkeeping.
  TOKEN BUCKET    ⭐ the usual right answer: a steady refill
                  rate plus a burst allowance, which matches how
                  real clients behave.

WHAT TO RETURN — and this is the part people get wrong
  429 Too Many Requests
  + Retry-After: 30
  + the limit, the remaining count and the reset time in headers
  ⭐ A LIMIT WITHOUT A Retry-After TEACHES CLIENTS TO HAMMER.
    You have created the retry storm you were defending against.

WHAT TO LIMIT ON
  per API key or user, not per IP where possible — ⚠ an office
  behind one NAT shares an IP, so IP limits punish a whole
  company for one user.
  and limit EXPENSIVE endpoints separately: a report generation
  or an LLM call is not equivalent to a health check.

⭐ THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET: LIMIT YOUR OWN OUTBOUND CALLS TOO.
  your backend calling an LLM provider or the AI service needs a
  concurrency cap, or a queue drain becomes a self-inflicted
  denial of service — and, with a metered provider, an
  unexpected invoice.
API 20

GraphQL and gRPC, in context

Buys youCosts you
RESTUniversally understood, cacheable by default, debuggable in a browser, no tooling requiredOver-fetching and under-fetching; several round trips for one screen.
GraphQLThe client asks for exactly the fields it needs, in one request. Excellent for many varied clients.N+1 by default unless you add batching · caching is now your problem, not HTTP's · a query can be arbitrarily expensive, so you need depth and complexity limits · authorisation must be enforced per field, not per endpoint.
gRPCFast binary protocol, generated clients, streaming, a strict schemaNot readable by a browser without a proxy; harder to debug; a build step. Right for service-to-service, wrong for a public API.
For this project

REST between the frontend and NestJS, REST between NestJS and FastAPI. That is correct: one frontend, a small number of endpoints, and a team that benefits far more from browser-debuggable requests than from field-level query flexibility.

The senior version of this knowledge is not "GraphQL is better". It is being able to say which specific pain would justify the switch — many heterogeneous clients over-fetching badly — and noting that we do not have it.

API 21

APIs that last

Model nouns, not procedures

When you find yourself inventing verbs, you have missed a noun. "Cancel an order" is often better as a cancellation that gets created — then it has a time, an author and a reason.

Return what you created

A create should return the object, or where to find it. Otherwise every caller immediately does a second request, and you have designed an N+1 into your contract.

Be conservative in what you send

Every field you return is a field someone will depend on. A response that dumps the whole record makes every future change breaking — and leaks fields the caller should not see.

Generate the documentation

From the code, so it cannot drift. Swagger from DTOs, OpenAPI from Pydantic — one definition, three jobs.

Make errors part of the design

Codes clients can branch on, stable across versions. "500: something went wrong" turns every integration into a support conversation.

Think about the second caller

The first caller is your own frontend, and you can change both together. The second one you cannot. Design as if it already exists.

Part V

Distributed systems

What changes the moment your system spans two machines — which, with three services and a hosted database, yours already does.

Distributed 22

What actually changes

A function call and a network call are not the same thing
A FUNCTION CALL             A NETWORK CALL
  succeeds or throws          succeeds · fails · ⭐ OR NEITHER
  instant                     milliseconds to seconds
  ordered                     may arrive out of order
  exactly once                may arrive twice, or never
  same clock                  ⚠ different clocks
  same transaction            no shared transaction

⭐ THE THIRD OUTCOME IS THE WHOLE SUBJECT. "Neither" — you sent
  a request and never learned what happened. Did it fail before
  the work, or succeed and lose the response?
  ⇒ YOU CANNOT TELL. And that is why:
    · every write across a boundary needs to be IDEMPOTENT, so
      the retry is safe
    · every call needs a TIMEOUT, or you wait forever
    · "it timed out" NEVER MEANS "it didn't happen"

⚠ THE FALLACIES THAT CAUSE REAL OUTAGES — each one is an
  assumption people make without noticing:
  the network is reliable · latency is zero · bandwidth is
  infinite · the network is secure · topology doesn't change ·
  there is one administrator · transport cost is zero

⭐ AND THE CONCRETE VERSION FOR THIS PROJECT: when the backend
  posts to the AI service and the request times out, you do not
  know whether the prediction ran. If that call had side effects
  — a charge, a written row, a sent email — a naive retry
  duplicates them. THAT is why idempotency keeps appearing in
  this handbook.

CAP and consistency models

CAP, STATED CAREFULLY when the network PARTITIONS — some nodes cannot reach others — you must choose: CONSISTENCY refuse to answer rather than answer wrongly AVAILABILITY answer, possibly with stale data ⚠ THE COMMON MISSTATEMENT: "pick two of three." Partitions are not optional — they happen to you. So the real choice is C or A, and only DURING a partition. ⭐ THE MORE USEFUL FRAME: PICK PER OPERATION, NOT PER SYSTEM. · a permission check → consistency. Refuse. · an account balance → consistency. · a dashboard count → availability. Stale is fine. · a "last seen" timestamp → availability, obviously. the same application wants both, and mature systems mix them deliberately. THE CONSISTENCY MODELS YOU WILL MEET STRONG every read sees the latest write. Simple to reason about, costs latency and availability. EVENTUAL reads converge, given time. ⚠ "given time" is doing a lot of work in that sentence. READ-YOUR-WRITES ⭐ THE ONE THAT MATTERS MOST IN PRACTICE: a user always sees their OWN changes, even if others see them later. ⇒ this is what fixes the read-replica ghost bug: route a user's reads to the primary for a moment after they write. MONOTONIC READS you never see time go backwards. ⭐ THE DESIGN RULE: DECIDE WHAT THE USER MUST SEE IMMEDIATELY, and make only that strongly consistent. Everything else can be eventual, and saying which is which is the design.

Queues and events

What a queue buys, and the four things it makes you own
WHY: decouple a slow or unreliable step from the request. The
user gets an answer immediately; the work happens after.

⭐ WHAT YOU NOW OWN, ALL FOUR:
  ① AT-LEAST-ONCE DELIVERY. Messages can arrive twice.
    ⇒ CONSUMERS MUST BE IDEMPOTENT. "Exactly once" is
      effectively a marketing term; at-least-once plus
      idempotency is the real pattern.
  ② NO GLOBAL ORDERING. Two messages may be processed out of
    order, or concurrently. If order matters, you need a
    partition key — and then you have limited your parallelism.
  ③ FAILURE HANDLING. A message that always fails must not
    retry forever. ⭐ A DEAD-LETTER QUEUE IS NOT OPTIONAL, and
    neither is someone actually looking at it.
  ④ VISIBILITY. The user asked for something and got "accepted".
    They need a way to know it finished — a status, a
    notification, a WebSocket event.

EVENT-DRIVEN ARCHITECTURE — the same idea as a design style
  services publish facts ("employee.created"); others react.
  ✅ buys loose coupling and easy extension: adding a consumer
     requires no change to the producer.
  ⚠ costs: no single place shows the whole flow, debugging spans
     services, and ⭐ NOBODY KNOWS WHO IS LISTENING — so
     changing an event's shape breaks consumers you have
     forgotten exist.

⭐ COMMANDS vs EVENTS — a distinction worth keeping
  COMMAND  "do this" — one intended handler, may be rejected
  EVENT    "this happened" — a fact, any number of listeners,
           cannot be rejected
  ⇒ mixing them produces systems where a published "event" is
    secretly required to be handled, and a missing consumer is
    a silent bug.

Resilience patterns

PatternStopsDetail that matters
TimeoutWaiting forever on a hung dependencyThe single most important one. No timeout means one slow service exhausts your threads and takes you down with it. Set one on every outbound call.
Retry with backoffLosing a request to a transient blipExponential, capped, with jitter. Without jitter, every client retries in sync and you have built a thundering herd. And only retry idempotent operations.
Circuit breakerHammering a service that is already downAfter N failures, fail fast for a while, then try one probe. Gives the failing service room to recover instead of holding it under.
BulkheadOne slow dependency consuming all capacitySeparate connection pools or thread pools per dependency, so the AI service being slow cannot starve ordinary requests.
Graceful degradationAn optional component blanking a required pageRender without it and say what is missing. A design decision to make now, not during the provider's next outage.
Health checksTraffic routed to broken instancesMust be honest — a check that returns 200 while the database is unreachable is worse than none, because it hides the failure from the load balancer.
IdempotencyRetries causing duplicatesUnderpins every row above. Without it, retrying is unsafe and none of the others can be used freely.

Tracing across services

The one operational thing to add before you need it
THE PROBLEM: a request is slow. Three services and a database
were involved. Each log file says its own part took no time.

⭐ THE FIX, AND IT IS SMALL: A CORRELATION ID.
  generate one at the edge, pass it in a header to every
  downstream call, and log it with every line.

    frontend  →  X-Request-Id: 9f2c…
    NestJS    →  logs with 9f2c…, forwards the header
    FastAPI   →  logs with 9f2c…
    ⇒ now ONE grep reconstructs the entire request across all
      three services.

  this is perhaps twenty lines of middleware and it is the
  difference between debugging a distributed system and
  guessing at one.

DISTRIBUTED TRACING — the same idea, industrialised
  each step is a SPAN with a start, a duration and a parent, so
  you get a waterfall: which call took the time, and what ran in
  parallel versus in sequence.
  ⭐ THE WATERFALL ROUTINELY REVEALS THE ACTUAL BUG: three calls
    that could have run concurrently running one after another,
    or one unnoticed call being made forty times.

⭐ AND THE POINT THAT MAKES THIS PART WORTH READING FOR A SMALL
  TEAM: three services IS a distributed system. You already have
  timeouts you have not set, retries that are not idempotent,
  and failures you cannot correlate. The patterns in Part V are
  not for hyperscale — they are the difference between an
  incident you can explain and one you cannot.

Term bank

Volume II · architecture vocabulary
non-functional requirement
How fast, how many, how available, how private — as opposed to what it does.
bottleneck
The one resource that saturates first. Everything else is a symptom.
replication lag
The delay before a replica reflects a write. Cause of "I saved it and it's not there".
read-your-writes
A guarantee that a user always sees their own changes immediately.
eventual consistency
Reads converge given time. The "given time" is the design question.
idempotent
Safe to repeat. The precondition for every retry.
idempotency key
A caller-supplied id so a duplicate request returns the first result.
circuit breaker
Fail fast after repeated failures, so the broken service can recover.
bulkhead
Isolated resource pools so one slow dependency cannot starve the rest.
dead-letter queue
Where messages go after exhausting retries. Useless unless someone reads it.
thundering herd
Many clients retrying or expiring in sync. Prevented by jitter.
correlation id
One id logged by every service handling a request. The cheapest observability win.
span
One timed step in a trace, with a parent. Spans make the waterfall.
bounded context
A part of the business where a word has one consistent meaning.
ubiquitous language
Code using the words the business uses. The free half of DDD.
aggregate
Objects changed together behind one root. The transaction boundary.
value object
Defined entirely by its values — money, a date range. Can validate itself.
ports and adapters
The domain declares interfaces; infrastructure implements them.
projection
A read model derived from events. What event sourcing makes you build for every query.
ADR
A one-page record of a decision, its alternatives and its consequences.
keyset pagination
Paging by cursor rather than offset. Stable under writes, fast at depth.
breaking change
Removing or renaming a field, narrowing a type, or requiring an optional one.
