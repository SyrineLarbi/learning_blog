Problems before models. Translation is the whole job.

A business says a sentence. Your job is to turn it into a problem type, an approach, a target and a system — and sometimes into a decision not to build anything. Part I is that method. Part II is eight domain playbooks, because the method is generic and the vocabulary is not.

Part I

The framework

How a senior gets from a vague request to a defensible plan — and the five questions they ask before touching data.

Strategy 01

How a senior thinks

The beginner's mental modelWhy it is incomplete
"Data science is building models that predict things."Most of the value is in deciding what to predict, and a large share of good outcomes involve no model at all.
"Better accuracy is better work."Accuracy that changes no decision is worth nothing. A worse model that is acted on beats a better one that is not.
"The stakeholder tells me the problem."They tell you a symptom, usually phrased as a solution. Translating it is the skill.
"Done means the model works."Done means the decision improved, and someone can still tell in six months.
The five questions before touching data
①  WHAT DECISION CHANGES, AND WHO MAKES IT?
   ⭐ if you cannot name the person and the moment, there is no
     project yet — there is an interest.

②  WHAT HAPPENS TODAY, WITHOUT THIS?
   the current workaround is the baseline AND the specification.
   It is usually more honest than the request.

③  WHICH ERROR COSTS MORE, AND ROUGHLY BY HOW MUCH?
   this sets the metric and the threshold. It is a business
   input, and asking for it is not a technicality.

④  WHAT WOULD "GOOD ENOUGH TO ACT ON" LOOK LIKE?
   ⭐ A TARGET AGREED BEFORE YOU START IS THE ONLY DEFENCE
     AGAINST ENDLESS ITERATION. Without it, every result invites
     "can you make it better?"

⑤  WHAT IS THE SIMPLEST THING THAT COULD WORK?
   a rule, a report, a filtered view. If it solves 80%, the
   model needs to justify the other 20% plus its permanent
   maintenance cost.

⭐ AND THE MENTAL MODELS A SENIOR REASONS WITH
  · THE COMPLEXITY LADDER — rules → statistics → classical ML →
    deep learning → LLM. Climb only when the rung below fails,
    and be able to say why it failed.
  · BASE RATES — a rare event makes even a good model mostly
    false-positive, and that must be in the framing.
  · COST ASYMMETRY — the threshold is a business decision.
  · FEEDBACK LOOPS — acting on predictions changes the data you
    will later train on.
  · ⭐ VALUE = ACCURACY × ADOPTION × FREQUENCY. A brilliant model
    used once a quarter by one person is worth less than a crude
    one used daily by eleven.
Strategy 02

Translation — the whole job

The discovery interview, and worked translations
WHAT TO ASK, IN ORDER
  ①  "Walk me through what you do today."       ⭐ start here
  ②  "What decision do you make with it?"
  ③  "What does it cost you when it goes wrong — in either
      direction?"
  ④  "How often does this happen?"
  ⑤  "What would you do differently if you knew X?"
  ⑥  "Who else is affected?"     ⭐ in HR, the employee — and
      they are usually not in the room
  ⑦  "What have you already tried?"

⭐ QUESTION ⑤ IS THE ONE THAT PRODUCES THE PROJECT. If the
  answer is "nothing, really", you have found out early that
  there is no decision to improve.

WORKED TRANSLATIONS — sentence to problem

  "We're losing too many good people."
  ⇒ SYMPTOM. Decision: who does a manager talk to this month?
    ⇒ RANKING problem, not classification. Top-k per manager,
      evaluated on precision at k, with a monthly cadence.
    ⚠ NOT "predict who will leave" — nobody acts on a list of
      400 probabilities.

  "We want to know why people leave."
  ⇒ ⭐ A DIFFERENT PROBLEM ENTIRELY: explanation, not
    prediction. A predictive model's feature importances are
    not causes. This wants analysis, possibly a survey, and
    honest language about association.

  "Can you predict next quarter's headcount?"
  ⇒ FORECASTING, with a horizon and an interval — and the
    decision is a hiring plan, so the interval matters more
    than the point estimate.

  "Automate CV screening."
  ⇒ ⚠ STOP. Advisory or decisive? If it filters, this is a
    decision about employment: fairness, explainability,
    records and an appeal path, at a completely different bar.

⭐ THE TRANSLATION CHECKLIST
  □  I can state the problem type in one phrase
  □  I can name the decision and the decision-maker
  □  I know the cost asymmetry
  □  I know the cadence — real-time, daily, monthly
  □  I know what "good enough" is
  □  I have said out loud what I am NOT solving
Strategy 03

The problem taxonomy

Your core vocabulary. Naming the type correctly settles the model class, the metric and the evaluation in one move.

TypeThe questionMetric family, and the trap
Binary classificationWill this happen?PR-AUC on a rare class; precision/recall at a chosen threshold. Trap: accuracy on imbalanced data.
MulticlassWhich of these categories?Per-class recall, and a confusion matrix. Trap: a macro average hiding one terrible class.
Ranking / top-kWho should I attend to first?Precision@k, NDCG. Trap: optimising a global metric when only the top ten are ever seen.
RegressionHow much?MAE in real units; RMSE if large errors hurt more. Trap: R² quoted with no context.
ForecastingHow much, over time?Backtested error by horizon, with intervals. Trap: random splits, and ignoring seasonality.
Clustering / segmentationWhat natural groups exist?No accuracy score. Trap: clustering always returns clusters — validation is whether a domain expert finds them meaningful.
Anomaly detectionWhat is unusual here?Precision at the alert volume you can actually review. Trap: forgetting that anomalies are rare by definition, so precision will be low.
RecommendationWhat should we show next?Online metrics beat offline ones. Trap: popularity bias, and a feedback loop that narrows what anyone ever sees.
Causal / upliftWill the intervention help this person?Needs an experiment or a careful design. Trap: using a predictive model to answer it.
OptimisationWhat is the best allocation?Objective value against constraints. Trap: predicting when you should be optimising — a forecast is an input, not the decision.
Extraction / NLPWhat does this text say?Field-level accuracy; a schema. Trap: free-text output that nothing can validate.
The distinction people most often get wrong

Prediction versus causation versus optimisation. "Who will leave" is prediction. "Would a raise stop them" is causal. "Given a fixed retention budget, who should get one" is optimisation — and it needs the causal answer, not the predictive one.

Most attrition projects are asked as prediction, are acted on as if they were causal, and would be most valuable as optimisation. Naming which one you are actually doing is a senior act.

Strategy 04

Planning and roadmapping

How to start, and how to not run forever
⭐ START WITH THE THINNEST END-TO-END SLICE, NOT WITH THE DATA.
  get a crude prediction in front of one real user in two weeks,
  however embarrassing. ⇒ IT EXPOSES EVERY INTEGRATION PROBLEM,
  every missing field and every permission question while they
  are still cheap.
  ⚠ THE COMMON ALTERNATIVE — three weeks of exploration, then
    three weeks of modelling, then discovering the data you need
    is not collected — is how projects die at week seven.

THE LIFECYCLE, AS A PLANNING SKELETON
  frame → assess the data → thin slice → baseline → iterate →
  validate → ship → monitor → realise value

TIME-BOXING, WHICH IS THE PART THAT MATTERS
  · a SPIKE for anything unknown: "two days to find out whether
    the exit-interview data is usable", then estimate
  · ⭐ AN EXPLICIT DECISION POINT: "if we cannot beat the
    baseline by X after two weeks, we stop." Agreed in advance,
    this is a professional plan; proposed afterwards it sounds
    like failure.
  · milestones that are DECISIONS, not activities: "we know
    whether this is feasible" beats "feature engineering done"

THE RISK REGISTER — think like a pessimist, once, in writing
  DATA         is it collected? complete? accessible? is the
               label trustworthy?
  ⭐ LABEL RISK IS THE ONE PEOPLE MISS: "regretted attrition"
    may not be recorded consistently, and your target is only as
    good as its definition.
  SIGNAL       may there simply be no learnable signal? Say so
               as a possible outcome at the start.
  ADOPTION     will anyone act on it? ⭐ THE MOST COMMON REASON
               A TECHNICALLY SUCCESSFUL PROJECT DELIVERS NOTHING.
  ETHICS/LEGAL is this decision one we may make this way at all?
  DEPENDENCY   whose pipeline, whose approval, whose calendar?

ESTIMATING HONESTLY
  ⭐ DECOMPOSE UNTIL EACH PIECE IS A DAY OR LESS, and give a
    RANGE with the condition attached: "three to eight days;
    eight if the HR export is undocumented." The condition tells
    everyone what to resolve to narrow it.
Strategy 05

Choosing the approach

THE COMPLEXITY LADDER — climb only when the rung below fails, and be able to say why it failed. ① A RULE auditable · instant · free · explainable · often 80% as good ② DESCRIPTIVE STATISTICS a segment, a trend, a cohort table ③ CLASSICAL ML ⭐ tabular data with labels lives here, and gradient boosting wins ④ DEEP LEARNING images, audio, sequences — where features are hierarchical ⑤ AN LLM unstructured language, generation, extraction, fuzzy classification ⚠ EVERY RUNG ADDS: cost, latency, maintenance, and a loss of explainability. ⭐ SO THE QUESTION IS NEVER "WHAT IS THE BEST MODEL" — IT IS "WHAT IS THE LOWEST RUNG THAT MEETS THE TARGET". BUILD vs BUY vs LLM — the modern fork BUILD when it is your differentiator, you have the data, and you will maintain it BUY when it is a solved commodity — OCR, translation, speech. ⭐ Nobody was promoted for rebuilding one. LLM when the input is language and an occasional wrong answer is tolerable ⚠ AND THE HONEST COST OF "BUY": data leaves your boundary, which for HR data is a legal question and not a preference. CHOOSING THE WORKFLOW — how the model runs, which matters as much as which model BATCH scored nightly, read from a table ⭐ USUALLY RIGHT, AND USUALLY SKIPPED. An attrition risk changes monthly at most. REAL-TIME scored per request. For predictions that depend on the request itself. STREAMING continuous. For fraud, and little else here. HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP the model proposes, a person decides. ⭐ THE CORRECT DEFAULT FOR ANY DECISION ABOUT A PERSON. ⭐ THE WORKED DECISION, ON THIS PROJECT'S OWN PROBLEM: tabular · labelled · monthly cadence · explanation required · affects people ⇒ CLASSICAL ML (gradient boosting), BATCH scored, with HUMAN-IN-THE-LOOP presentation and per-prediction attributions. The LLM writes the narrative around a number it did not compute.
Strategy 06

System design for ML

THE REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE — every ML system is this shape sources ──► ingest ──► store ──► features ──► training │ │ │ ▼ │ registry │ │ ▼ ▼ serving ◄─── model │ ▼ consumer (app, dashboard) │ ▼ monitoring ──► back to training ⭐ THE BIGGEST ARCHITECTURAL FORK: BATCH vs REAL-TIME. it decides the storage, the feature computation, the serving, the monitoring and most of the cost. ⇒ DECIDE IT FIRST, and decide it from the cadence of the decision, not from what sounds modern. ⭐ THE TWO HARDEST PROBLEMS IN ML SYSTEMS, AND THEY ARE BOTH ABOUT FEATURES, NOT MODELS: ① TRAINING/SERVING SKEW — the feature computed one way in the notebook and another way in production. The model performs worse in production for no visible reason, and it is very hard to diagnose. ⇒ ONE DEFINITION, CALLED FROM BOTH. ② POINT-IN-TIME CORRECTNESS — using a feature's value as at the event, not as at now. Get it wrong and you have leakage baked into the architecture. ⇒ this is the genuinely hard thing a feature store solves; if you have one model, solve it with discipline instead. DESIGNING FOR THE QUALITIES THAT MATTER HERE REPRODUCIBILITY code version + DATA version + environment AUDITABILITY ⭐ every prediction records its model version and inputs. For an HR system this is a requirement, not an aspiration. REVERSIBILITY roll back a model as fast as code PRIVACY per-field access, retention, and an erasure path that reaches training sets ⚠ ANTI-PATTERNS: the notebook as the deployment artefact · a feature pipeline that exists only in someone's head · a model in production with no owner · real-time serving for a monthly decision.
Strategy 07

Measuring success and hitting targets

From model metric to money, and knowing when you are done
⭐ THE TWO-METRIC WORLD, AND CONFUSING THEM IS THE CENTRAL
  BEGINNER ERROR:
    THE MODEL METRIC     PR-AUC, MAE, precision@k
    THE BUSINESS METRIC  regretted attrition, cost per hire,
                         inventory held, revenue retained
  ⇒ YOU OPTIMISE THE FIRST AND ARE JUDGED ON THE SECOND, and
    the bridge between them is your job to build.

THE BRIDGE — worked, on this project
  the model flags 140 employees a month; ~32% of them would
  have left. If a conversation retains one in four of those, and
  replacing someone costs roughly half a salary, then:
    45 true flags × 25% retained ≈ 11 people
    11 × (replacement cost) = the annual value
  ⚠ AND EVERY NUMBER IN THAT CHAIN IS AN ASSUMPTION. ⭐ SO STATE
    THEM, and give a range rather than a point. A defensible
    range beats a precise fiction.

SETTING AN HONEST TARGET
  · anchor on the BASELINE, not on zero
  · express it in the decision's terms: "a manager's five
    conversations should contain at least two genuine risks"
  · ⭐ AGREE IT BEFORE MODELLING. Afterwards, any number can be
    argued into being disappointing.

VALIDATION — making sure the number is real
  □  a test set the tuning never touched
  □  ⭐ a genuinely LATER time period
  □  per-subgroup, not only aggregate
  □  compared against the baseline on the same split
  □  and the sanity question: is this too good? (reflex: a
     suspiciously good result is a bug report)

KNOWING WHEN YOU ARE DONE
  ⭐ WHEN THE AGREED TARGET IS MET AND THE NEXT INCREMENT COSTS
    MORE THAN IT RETURNS. Not when the metric stops improving —
    it never stops improving.

CLOSING THE LOOP — the step almost nobody does
  measure the BUSINESS metric after deployment, against a
  comparison group. ⚠ Without a holdout you cannot claim the
  effect, and someone will eventually ask you to.
  ⚠ AND EXPECT THE MEASUREMENT TO GET HARDER, not easier:
    acting on predictions changes the outcomes you are
    measuring.
Strategy 08

When not to use machine learning

Say it out loud, early

The most valuable sentence a senior data scientist says is often "this should not be a model" — and being right about it is what earns the trust to be asked next time.

Do not use ML when…Because
A rule worksAuditable, instant, free, explainable, and maintainable by someone who is not you.
The decision must be exactly reproducible and defensibleA model is a statistical statement. Some decisions need a stated rule, especially about people.
You have no labels, and cannot get themSupervised learning needs outcomes. Unsupervised methods answer a different question — do not pretend otherwise.
The label is untrustworthyIf "regretted attrition" is recorded inconsistently, you will faithfully model the recording process.
The base rate is tiny and the review capacity is smallMost of what you surface will be false positives, and the alert will be ignored within a month.
Nobody will act on the outputThen the accuracy is irrelevant. Fix the decision process first.
The real problem is data accessA model over data nobody can find solves the wrong bottleneck. A search tool or a clean table may be the whole answer.
The process it would automate is brokenAn AI layer over a distrusted process produces faster distrust.
Part II

Domain playbooks

Domains 09

How to read these

Why domain knowledge is not optional

The method in Part I is generic. The vocabulary, the data, the canonical problems and the pitfalls are not — and a data scientist who cannot speak a domain's language will translate its sentences wrongly, however good their modelling is.

Each playbook below answers the same eight questions: how the business thinks · what data exists · the canonical problems and their types · the models and workflow · the reference architecture · how success is measured · the domain-specific pitfalls · and a worked mini-case.

Domain 10

Sales and revenue

How the business thinks · the data

Pipeline, stages, quota, close rate, cycle length, ACV. A sales leader thinks in a funnel with a forecast attached, and their fear is a quarter that misses.

Data: a CRM (opportunities, stages, activities), marketing touch data, product usage, and — critically — who touched what and when. Usually messy, self-reported, and updated when convenient.

Canonical problems → type
  • Lead scoring → ranking (top-k for a rep's day), not classification.
  • Deal win prediction → binary classification, with the caveat below.
  • Pipeline forecasting → forecasting with intervals; the interval is the point.
  • Next-best-action → causal/uplift, though it is almost always attempted as prediction.
  • Churn on existing accounts → classification plus ranking for CSM attention.

Approach: gradient boosting on tabular CRM features, scored nightly, surfaced inside the CRM. A model that requires leaving the CRM will not be used.

Domain pitfalls
  • Self-reported stage is a leak and a lie. "Stage: verbal commit" is entered by the rep whose forecast it flatters — and it is often entered after the outcome is known.
  • Activity counts measure rep diligence, not deal quality. A model built on them predicts who updates the CRM.
  • Survivorship: closed-lost deals are frequently deleted or left stale, so the training data over-represents wins.
  • The feedback loop is immediate — reps work the leads the model ranks highly, which makes them close, which confirms the model.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: conversion rate on worked leads versus a holdout, cycle length, and forecast error by horizon — never by AUC alone.

Mini-case. "Our reps waste time on bad leads." Translated: rank each rep's inbound queue daily, top 20. Baseline: chronological order. Gradient boosting on firmographics plus product-usage signals, excluding CRM stage entirely. Result framed as "of your top 20, historically 6 convert versus 3 in the old order" — and a holdout of 10% of reps kept on the old order to prove it.

Domain 11

E-commerce and retail

How the business thinks · the data

Conversion rate, AOV, margin, return rate, CAC, repeat rate, stock availability. The tension is always growth versus margin, and availability versus holding cost.

Data: clickstream (large, noisy, rich), orders, catalogue, prices and promotions, returns, inventory. Clickstream is the most underused and the most seductive.

Canonical problems → type
  • Recommendation → recommendation; online metrics beat offline ones, always.
  • Demand forecasting per SKU → forecasting, with intermittent-demand problems for the long tail.
  • Price and promotion optimisation → causal, then optimisation. Not prediction.
  • Return prediction → classification; often more valuable than it sounds, because returns destroy margin.
  • Customer lifetime value → regression, or a probabilistic model.
  • Search relevance → ranking.
Domain pitfalls
  • Popularity bias: a recommender trained on clicks recommends what was already shown, narrowing the catalogue over time. The feedback loop is the product.
  • Promotions confound everything. A demand model that ignores them learns that December is magic.
  • Offline recommendation metrics correlate weakly with revenue. Only an online test settles it.
  • Out-of-stock periods look like zero demand in the data, and training on them teaches the model that the product is unwanted.
  • The long tail: most SKUs have too little history for per-SKU models, so hierarchical or pooled approaches are the honest answer.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: revenue per session, margin per order, stockout rate and holding cost together — optimising one alone moves another the wrong way.

Mini-case. "Recommend better products." Translated: raise revenue per session on the product page. Baseline: best-sellers in category — which is a strong baseline and frequently beats a first model. Ship as an A/B test, measure revenue per session, and explicitly monitor catalogue coverage so the recommender does not quietly collapse onto twenty products.

Domain 12

Marketing and growth

How the business thinks · the data

CAC, ROAS, channel mix, funnel stages, LTV:CAC, payback period. The recurring question is where the next unit of budget should go.

Data: ad platform exports, web analytics, email engagement, CRM. Fragmented across tools, with identity resolution as the hidden hard problem.

Canonical problems → type
  • Churn prediction and retention targeting → classification, then uplift, because you want people a message would change.
  • Attribution → causal. Not a classification problem, however often it is treated as one.
  • Budget allocation → optimisation, taking forecasts as inputs.
  • Segmentation → clustering, validated by whether marketers can act on the segments.
  • Send-time and content selection → ranking or bandits.
Domain pitfalls
  • Last-click attribution is not causation, and neither is any other heuristic rule. Attribution is a genuinely hard causal problem, and rule-based models mostly reallocate credit rather than discover it.
  • Targeting the most likely churners is the classic error. Some will churn regardless, and some would have stayed anyway — uplift identifies the persuadable, and it is a different model.
  • Selection: engagement data only contains the engaged, so a survey of active users cannot explain why people left.
  • Seasonality and campaign calendars confound almost every trend.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: incremental revenue against a holdout, CAC by channel, and retention lift — never by campaign open rates.

Mini-case. "Reduce churn." Translated: which subscribers would stay if contacted? Baseline: contact everyone at risk. An uplift model on treatment/control history, with a permanent holdout, reported as "contacting your top decile retains 4 more per hundred than contacting at random" — a sentence with an experiment behind it.

Domain 13

Science and chemistry

How the field thinks · the data

A different vocabulary entirely: assays, hits, potency, selectivity, ADMET, scaffolds, chemical series. The unit of progress is an experiment, and experiments are slow and expensive — which changes everything about how models are used.

Data: structures (SMILES, graphs), assay results, public databases. Small, heterogeneous, and with enormous class imbalance — actives are rare by design.

Canonical problems → type
  • Virtual screening → ranking. The output is "which 100 of these million should we test", not a probability.
  • Property prediction (QSAR/ADMET) → regression or classification on molecular representations.
  • Active learning / experiment selection → the highest-value pattern here: the model chooses the next experiment, and each round is expensive.
  • Generative design → generation with hard constraints, and synthesisability is the constraint people forget.
Domain pitfalls
  • Random splits massively overstate performance. Molecules in a chemical series are near-duplicates, so a random split puts siblings in train and test. Scaffold or time-based splits are mandatory, and the score will drop a lot — that drop is the truth.
  • Activity cliffs: a tiny structural change can abolish activity, so smooth models mislead near the boundary.
  • Assay data from different labs and protocols is not directly comparable, and pooling it silently adds noise you attribute to the model.
  • Negative results are systematically missing from public data, so "inactive" often means "never tested".
  • Applicability domain: a model asked about a chemical space it never saw will answer confidently and wrongly.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: enrichment — how many more hits per experiment than random selection — and by experiments saved, not by AUC.

Mini-case. "Help us screen faster." Translated: rank a library so the first 200 assays contain more hits. Scaffold-split validation, enrichment as the metric, and an active-learning loop where each assay round retrains. Reported as "5× enrichment over random in the first plate", which is the number a chemist can act on.

Domain 14

Finance and risk

How the business thinks · the data

Exposure, loss rate, expected loss, capital, false-positive cost, regulatory capital. And a constraint most domains do not have: models may need to be explainable to a regulator.

Data: transactions, account history, external bureau data, device and session signals. High volume, strongly imbalanced, and adversarial — the distribution changes because someone is trying to defeat you.

Canonical problems → type
  • Fraud detection → classification, real-time, with an extreme base rate and a hard latency budget.
  • Credit scoring → classification, with explainability as a legal requirement.
  • AML / suspicious activity → anomaly detection plus rules; alert volume is capped by review capacity.
  • Forecasting cash or loss → forecasting with intervals.
Domain pitfalls
  • Adversarial drift. Fraud patterns change because you deployed the model. A static model decays fast, and monitoring input drift is not optional.
  • The rejected-applicant problem: you only observe outcomes for those you approved, so your training data is censored by your own past decisions. Ignoring it is the most consequential mistake in credit modelling.
  • Alert volume is a hard constraint. A model producing 4,000 alerts for a team of six has produced nothing.
  • Fairness and explainability are legal, not aspirational — and proxies for protected attributes are heavily scrutinised.
  • Extreme imbalance makes ROC-AUC look wonderful; PR-AUC and precision at the alert budget are the honest metrics.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: losses prevented minus the cost of false positives (including customer friction), at the alert volume the team can actually review.

Mini-case. "Catch more fraud." Translated: at 200 reviewable alerts a day, maximise prevented loss. Real-time scoring with a hard latency budget, a champion/challenger setup because drift is continuous, and reporting in currency prevented rather than in AUC.

Domain 15

Supply chain and operations

How the business thinks · the data

Service level, fill rate, stockouts, holding cost, lead time, OTIF, working capital. Every decision is a trade between having too much and not having enough, and the costs of those two are rarely equal.

Data: orders, shipments, inventory positions, supplier lead times, promotions, and calendars. Lead-time data is usually the weakest and the most important.

Canonical problems → type
  • Demand forecasting → forecasting per SKU per location, with hierarchy.
  • Inventory policy → optimisation, taking the forecast as an input. The forecast is not the decision.
  • Lead-time prediction → regression, and its variance matters more than its mean.
  • Late-delivery risk → classification, for expediting decisions.
  • Network and routing → optimisation.
Domain pitfalls
  • Forecasting demand from sales. Sales are censored by availability — a stockout looks like zero demand, and training on it teaches the model nobody wants the product. This is the single most common error in the domain.
  • Optimising forecast accuracy instead of the decision. A 2% MAPE improvement that does not change any order quantity is worth nothing; safety stock depends on forecast error variance, not on the point estimate.
  • Promotions and price changes dominate demand and are often missing from the model's inputs.
  • The long tail: most SKUs are intermittent, and standard time-series methods handle them badly.
  • Aggregation: forecasting at the wrong grain — weekly national when the decision is daily per warehouse — makes an accurate model useless.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: service level at a given inventory investment — the two together, never one alone. Plus forecast error by horizon at the decision grain.

Mini-case. "Our forecasts are bad." Translated: reduce stockouts without raising inventory. First finding, usually: the data does not distinguish zero demand from zero availability. Fix that, forecast at the grain of the ordering decision, then feed error variance into a safety-stock calculation — and report in service level and working capital, because that is what the business is measured on.

Domain 16

Healthcare

How the field thinks · the data

Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, number needed to treat, clinical workflow. Clinicians think in terms of harm from action and harm from inaction, and they are trained to distrust a number without a confidence statement.

Data: electronic records, labs, vitals, imaging, notes. Highly sensitive, irregularly sampled, and full of clinically meaningful missingness.

Canonical problems → type
  • Deterioration / early warning → classification with a time horizon, e.g. sepsis risk in the next six hours.
  • Readmission risk → classification, for discharge planning.
  • Diagnosis support from imaging → deep learning, advisory only.
  • Resource and capacity forecasting → forecasting.
  • Treatment effect → causal, and it needs a trial-grade design.
Domain pitfalls
  • Missingness is informative and often leaky. A lab test was ordered because the clinician was already worried — so "test ordered" predicts the outcome and is unavailable in the way you need it.
  • Alert fatigue is a patient-safety issue. A model with poor precision at the alert volume actively harms care by training clinicians to dismiss warnings.
  • Distribution shift between sites is severe. A model trained at one hospital frequently fails at another — different populations, protocols and instruments.
  • Label timing: a diagnosis recorded at discharge is not known when you must predict.
  • Regulatory status: a tool that influences clinical decisions may be a regulated medical device.
Success, and a mini-case

Measured by: clinical outcomes, not model metrics — and by whether the alert changes what anyone does. Calibration matters more here than almost anywhere.

Mini-case. "Predict deterioration." Translated: flag patients at a volume the ward can actually respond to, with enough lead time to act. Careful point-in-time feature construction, external validation on another site, calibration checked explicitly, and presentation as a prompt with the contributing factors shown — never as a verdict.

Domain 17 · this project's own domain

HR and people analytics

How the business thinks · the data

Headcount, attrition (regretted versus not), time to hire, offer acceptance, internal mobility, engagement, cost per hire, tenure distribution. HR leaders think in terms of capability and risk, and they are acutely aware that these decisions concern identifiable people.

Data: HRIS records, performance reviews, surveys, compensation, absence, recruitment funnel. Small — thousands of rows, not millions — highly sensitive, and with subjective labels.

Canonical problems → type
  • Attrition risk → ranking for manager attention, not classification of individuals. This project's core problem.
  • Hiring funnel efficiency → analysis and forecasting; candidate screening is a decision about employment and carries a different bar entirely.
  • Engagement driver analysis → explanation, not prediction. It wants careful analysis and honest language about association.
  • Internal mobility matching → recommendation, advisory.
  • Headcount and capacity planning → forecasting.
  • Pay equity → statistical analysis, with legal involvement from the start.
Domain pitfalls — the heaviest list in this document
  • Post-outcome features: exit interview completed, handover uploaded, access revoked. Perfect predictors, all known after the decision. The classic leak here.
  • Small samples and thin subgroups. Slice a few thousand employees by department, level and tenure and you have cells of nine — where every dramatic percentage is noise.
  • Subjective labels. "Performance rating" measures a manager's judgement, and manager judgement varies systematically. "Regretted attrition" may be recorded inconsistently.
  • Proxies for protected attributes — postcode, school, working pattern, career gaps, part-time status. Removing the obvious column is not sufficient.
  • Historical bias. A model that faithfully reproduces past promotion decisions is a machine for perpetuating them at scale, with an appearance of objectivity — and accuracy makes that worse, not better.
  • Privacy and minimum cell size. "Engagement in the Lisbon team" where the team is three people identifies three people. Aggregation is not anonymisation.
  • The feedback loop: flagged employees get attention, which changes their outcomes, which becomes training data.
  • The base rate. At 5% attrition, a good model's flags are majority false-positive — so a flag must be framed as "worth a conversation", never as "will leave".
Success, architecture, and the mini-case

Measured by: regretted attrition among flagged-and-contacted employees versus a comparison group — and by the override rate, which is the best trust signal available.

Reference architecture (this is HR Insight AI): HRIS → nightly batch scoring with gradient boosting → risk stored per employee → surfaced to the employee's own manager only, with per-prediction attributions → an LLM writes the narrative around a number it did not compute → every view audited.

Mini-case. "We're losing good people." Translated: give each manager five names a month worth a conversation. Baseline: a rule on satisfaction and time since promotion — which is a strong baseline and must be beaten explicitly. Excluded: age, gender, nationality and their proxies, plus every post-outcome field. Evaluated per subgroup. Presented with the contributing factors and an override button that records a reason. And a documented answer to the question that governs the whole thing: would we be comfortable explaining this system, in detail, to the employees it scores?

"Will X happen?"ClassificationThe base rate, and which error costs more.
