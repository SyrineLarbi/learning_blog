Part I

The shape of a loop

Loop 01

The five stages, and what each is really scored on

StageLooks like it testsActually tests
Recruiter screenYour backgroundWhether you can explain what you built in two minutes, and whether your expectations are compatible. Prepare the two-minute version.
Technical screenWhether you can codeWhether you clarify before solving, and whether you narrate. Silence is unscoreable.
Take-home or pairingThe finished productYour judgement about scope, and your README. What you chose not to do is read as carefully as what you did.
System designWhether you know architecturesWhether you ask about scale before drawing, and whether you name what each choice costs.
Deep dive / behaviouralYour past workWhether your stories contain a decision, a cost and a change afterwards — and whether "we" ever becomes "I".
The one thing that runs through all five

They are hiring the person they will have to work with on a bad Tuesday. Every stage is partly a test of how you behave when you do not know something — which is why "I don't know, here's how I'd find out" is a strong answer, and bluffing is fatal in a way that being wrong is not.

Loop 02

The cheat sheet

The whole toolkit, on one screen
BEFORE YOU ANSWER ANYTHING
  ①  restate the question in your own words
  ②  ask the clarifying question you already suspect
  ③  say your approach in one sentence before executing it
  ⭐ THREE MOVES, NINETY SECONDS, AND THEY CHANGE THE SCORE OF
    EVERY ROUND.

IN A CODING ROUND
  · write two concrete input/output examples first
  · name the constraint: how big, how often, called in a loop?
  · say the edge cases you will handle: zero · one · many ·
    null · twice · order · race · hostile · halfway
  · ⭐ NARRATE. An interviewer cannot score thinking they cannot
    hear.
  · if you are stuck, say what you have ruled out

IN A DESIGN ROUND
  · requirements first, written where they can see them
  · ⭐ ESTIMATE BEFORE YOU DRAW. The number chooses the design.
  · start with three boxes and add under pressure
  · the data model is the highest-signal part
  · protect time for FAILURE — where seniority is most visible
  · close with the trade-off and what you left out

IN A BEHAVIOURAL ROUND
  · situation · task · action · result · ⭐ AND WHAT YOU
    CHANGED AFTERWARDS
  · "I", not "we" · numbers, not adjectives · two minutes

WHEN CHALLENGED
  ⭐ HEAR IT, CHECK IT, ADJUST. Defending a wrong answer is
    fatal; changing your mind on evidence is a positive signal.

WHEN YOU DO NOT KNOW
  "I haven't used that. Here's what I'd expect it to do, and
  here's how I'd verify it." ⇒ complete, professional answer.
Part II

The rounds

Loop 03

The technical screen

45 minutes, and how the time should be spent
0–5    INTRODUCTIONS AND YOUR TWO-MINUTE STORY
       ⭐ PREPARE THIS VERBATIM. What you built, the hard part,
         the outcome with a number. Two minutes, not five.

5–10   CLARIFYING THE PROBLEM
       ⚠ THE MOST OFTEN SKIPPED AND HIGHEST-VALUE FIVE MINUTES
         IN THE ENTIRE LOOP. Restate it, get one concrete
         example, name the shape of the data, name the
         constraint.

10–35  SOLVING IT, OUT LOUD
       · approach in one sentence first
       · write the examples down where they can see them
       · handle the happy path, then the edges you named
       · say what you are doing as you do it

35–42  QUESTIONS THEY ASK YOU
       edge cases, complexity, what you would change at scale.
       ⭐ "What would break if this got a hundred times bigger?"
         is asked in almost every screen — have the answer.

42–45  YOUR QUESTIONS
       ⚠ "No questions" closes on a flat note. Have two.

⭐ THE THREE THINGS THAT LOSE A SCREEN
  ① SILENCE. Even a wrong direction narrated beats a correct
    one produced in silence, because only one of them is
    scoreable.
  ② SOLVING THE WRONG PROBLEM CONFIDENTLY. Prevented entirely
    by the 5–10 minute block.
  ③ NOT FINISHING AND NOT SAYING SO. "I've handled the main
    path; the two things missing are X and Y" beats a silent
    unfinished screen every time.
Loop 04

Coding challenges

The challenge typeWhat they are checkingThe move that wins it
Transform a collection
group, aggregate, reshape		Whether you reach for a map instead of nested loopsBuild a lookup once. And say the complexity out loud — "this is O(n + m) rather than O(n × m)".
Parse or validate inputEdge-case disciplineEnumerate the malformed cases before writing: empty, wrong type, too long, duplicate keys, unexpected nesting.
Implement pagination or filteringWhether you know the contract mattersAsk whether the underlying data changes between pages. That question is the whole point of the exercise.
Fix a bug in given codeReading and hypothesis disciplineReproduce it first, state what you expected versus what happened, change one thing. Do not start editing.
Async orchestrationWhether you understand concurrencySpot the sequential awaits that could be parallel, and the loop with an await inside it.
A small API endpointWhether security is a habitValidate the input, check the id against the caller, and shape the response. Unprompted.
SQL from a requirementWhether you know what joins do to countsSay "this join will fan out and inflate the sum, so I'll aggregate first" before it happens.
On live coding with an audience

You will be slower than usual and that is expected. What is not expected is a silent screen. Two habits fix most of it: write the examples down first so you cannot drift onto the wrong problem, and say "I'm going to do X because Y" before each step so that even a wrong turn is legible.

And if you blank: say what you have ruled out. That is genuine information and it usually unblocks you.

Loop 05

The take-home

What is actually being read
⭐ THEY ARE NOT GRADING FEATURE COUNT. They are grading
  JUDGEMENT ABOUT SCOPE, and the README is where that is
  visible.

THE README THAT WINS IT
  □  HOW TO RUN IT — one command, and it must actually work on
     a clean machine. ⚠ A take-home that does not start is
     scored before it is read.
  □  WHAT YOU BUILT, in three lines
  □  ⭐ WHAT YOU DELIBERATELY DID NOT BUILD, AND WHY. This is
     the single strongest paragraph available to you: "no
     pagination because the dataset is fixed at 200 rows; I
     would add cursor pagination before this saw real data."
  □  THE TRADE-OFFS you took, with their costs
  □  WHAT YOU WOULD DO WITH ANOTHER DAY
  □  how long it took you, honestly

WHAT TO SPEND THE TIME ON, IN ORDER
  ①  IT RUNS. Cleanly, from the instructions, first try.
  ②  THE CORE PATH WORKS and is correct at the edges.
  ③  ONE OR TWO REAL TESTS — the permission boundary and one
     edge case beat twenty trivial assertions.
  ④  CLEAR STRUCTURE AND NAMES.
  ⑤  the README above.
  ⚠ NOT: extra features, a styling system, or an abstraction
    for requirements that do not exist.

⚠ THE FOUR THINGS THAT SINK A TAKE-HOME
  · it does not run
  · commented-out code and leftover console output
  · no error handling anywhere
  · a secret or a real credential committed
⭐ AND ONE THAT IS INCREASINGLY CHECKED: code you cannot
  explain. Expect a follow-up call walking through your own
  submission line by line.
Loop 06

The system design round

THE 45-MINUTE ALLOCATION — and the discipline is the score 0–5 REQUIREMENTS, written where they can see them functional: three to five things. non-functional: how many users, how fast, how fresh, how available, how private. ⭐ AND WHAT IS OUT OF SCOPE. Cutting scope in the first five minutes is the most senior move you will make in the round. 5–8 ESTIMATE users → requests per second → data per day → per year. Round violently and say you are rounding. ⭐ THE NUMBER CHOOSES THE DESIGN. Fifty requests a second is one boring database — and knowing that is what stops you over-building. 8–13 THE BOX DIAGRAM client → api → store, plus only what is needed. ⚠ eleven boxes in minute four, none of which you can justify, reads as inexperience. 13–20 THE DATA MODEL ⭐ the highest-signal part entities, relationships, and the access patterns. "we read this a thousand times per write" changes the design more than any box. 20–27 ONE REQUEST, END TO END, IN WORDS what is written, read, cached, queued — and what the user sees at each moment. 27–33 FAILURE ⭐ where seniority shows most what happens when each box is down or slow? which parts must be correct and which may be stale? ⚠ THIS IS ALSO WHERE PEOPLE RUN OUT OF TIME. Protect it deliberately. 33–39 SCALE THE BOTTLENECK the one thing that breaks first. Not everything — scaling everything is a list of technologies, not a design. 39–45 TRADE-OFFS AND WHAT'S NEXT what you would build first, what you left out, what breaks at 10× and what you would do then. ⭐ THE STRUCTURE IS THE SCORE. Two candidates with identical knowledge get different results because one followed this order and one free-associated.
Loop 07

The deep dive on your own work

The hardest round to fake, and the easiest to prepare
THEY PICK ONE THING FROM YOUR CV AND GO THREE LEVELS DOWN.

  level 1  "Tell me about the attrition feature."
  level 2  "Why XGBoost rather than logistic regression?"
  level 3  ⭐ "What was the base rate, and what did that do to
            your precision?"

  ⚠ MOST CANDIDATES ARE FINE AT LEVEL 1, ADEQUATE AT LEVEL 2,
    AND STOP AT LEVEL 3. Level 3 is where the round is decided.

⭐ SO PREPARE BY GOING THREE LEVELS DOWN ON EVERY CV CLAIM
  YOURSELF, BEFORE THE INTERVIEW. For each one:
  □  what did I choose, and what was the alternative?
  □  what did that choice COST?
  □  what number can I attach to the outcome?
  □  what would I do differently now?
  □  ⭐ WHAT WENT WRONG, and what did I change afterwards?

THE CLAIM MOST LIKELY TO BE PROBED
  any performance number. "50 seconds to 6" invites: what was
  the actual bottleneck, how did you find it, what did you try
  that did not work, and how do you know it stayed fixed?
  ⇒ IF YOU CANNOT ANSWER THOSE FOUR, DO NOT PUT THE NUMBER ON
    THE CV — and if you can, that claim becomes your strongest
    asset in the loop.

⚠ AND THE TRAP: CLAIMING TEAM WORK AS YOURS. Interviewers probe
  for it, and it collapses badly. "I owned the query
  optimisation; a colleague did the caching layer" is stronger
  than an unspecific "we".
Loop 08

Behavioural answers

The questionWhat they are listening for
"Tell me about a conflict with a colleague."That you separate the person from the position, and that it ended in a decision rather than a grudge. Never blame the other person.
"Tell me about a failure."A real one, owned without excuses, with the specific change it caused. The most commonly deflected question and the strongest available answer.
"When did you disagree with a decision?"Whether you can disagree and commit — argue with evidence, then execute properly anyway.
"Tell me about a time you missed a deadline."Whether you flagged it early. That is the whole answer.
"How do you handle an unclear requirement?"That you ask what the person will do with the result, rather than guessing or stalling.
"Tell me about mentoring someone."Specificity. What they could not do before, what you did, what they can do now.
"Why are you leaving?"Forward-looking, not grievance. Criticising your current employer reads as a preview.
Part III

Design scenarios

Four worked briefs. For each: the questions to ask first, the shape of the answer, and the trap.

Scenario 09

Design a live HR metrics dashboard

Ask first
  • How live is live? To the second, or refreshed every few minutes? The second is dramatically cheaper and almost always sufficient — nobody makes a hiring decision on a five-second-old number.
  • How many viewers, and when? This is a working-day spike, not smooth traffic.
  • Whose numbers? A regional manager's "total open roles" is not the company's.
The shape

Precompute the aggregates on a schedule, cache them, serve from the cache. The read-to-write ratio is thousands to one, so the "hard" problem largely evaporates once you stop treating it as real-time.

Show the timestamp. A dashboard that silently displays yesterday's figure as today's is worse than one that says it is behind.

The trap

Building for real-time because the word appeared in the brief. One question removes half the system. And the permission dimension is the one people miss entirely: if the aggregate is not computed per scope, the dashboard leaks headcount information sideways.

Strong close

"I'd build it batch-refreshed with a visible timestamp, per-scope aggregates, and a cache with jittered expiry so every viewer doesn't miss simultaneously. If they later need true real-time for one number, I'd add streaming for that number only."

Scenario 10

Design notifications

Ask first
  • Which events, and who decides someone receives them?
  • Which channels — in-app, email, push? And is there a category that ignores preferences, such as a security alert?
  • What is the worst-case volume? An approval flow with 300 items generates 300 notifications and one very angry user.
The shape

Two entities, not one: an event that happened, and a delivery per recipient per channel. Preferences per type and per channel. A queue, because delivery is slow and unreliable and must be retryable.

At-least-once delivery plus deduplication on the recipient's side — the alternative, at-most-once, silently loses the one that mattered.

The trap

Designing a bell icon rather than a system. And forgetting the leakage: preview text goes to a phone's lock screen and an email subject line. "Your termination letter is ready" on a lock screen is a design failure, not an edge case.

Strong close

"Batching and digests are core design here rather than an optimisation, and I'd send an id rather than the full record so the client fetches over HTTP where permissions are checked normally."

Scenario 11

Design an AI summary feature

Ask first
  • Is it advisory or decisive? The first question, before any box. If it filters, ranks or rejects, this is a decision about someone's employment with a completely different bar.
  • What goes into the prompt, and has that data left our boundary before?
  • How do we know it is any good — is there an eval set?
The shape

The deterministic system computes; the model narrates. Calculate the score in SQL or the model service where it is reproducible and auditable, and let the LLM write the sentence around it. Then the worst hallucination is a clumsy sentence, not a wrong figure in an HR review.

Out of the request path: precompute when the record changes, or stream. Constrained output shape. A fallback when the provider is down — the page renders, the panel says what is missing.

The trap

Letting the model produce a number, and putting a multi-second provider call inside a page load. Also: treating prompt injection as a prompting problem — a CV or document in the context means its author is speaking to your model, and only a trust boundary in code defends that.

Strong close

"I'd log the prompt version, token counts, stop reason and latency, because without those four a quality regression is unexplainable — and I'd build the twenty-case eval set before tuning anything."

Scenario 12

Design an employee record export

Ask first
  • What is it for? A manager preparing a review, a legal subject-access request, and HR sending a file to an external lawyer are three different features that differ on how much data leaves the system.
  • What is in "complete"? Name the categories and watch the request change: contact details, salary, reviews, disciplinary history, sickness, medical accommodations.
  • Is this requester entitled to all of it? Usually not — sickness and accommodations are frequently HR-only.
The shape

Assembled per requester, not once and filtered for display. A job rather than a request, because a twenty-year record with attachments is not a new joiner's. An audit row for every generation. A lifetime on the file, so it gets deleted.

The trap

Returning a link to a file in storage. A link is a credential: it carries no identity, works for anyone who has it, gets pasted into chat, and if the name is predictable the other records are reachable by editing the string. Say this unprompted and the round is effectively won.

Strong close

"Best is no file at all — render it in the app behind the session. If it must be a file, serve it through an authenticated endpoint; if it must be a direct link, a short-expiry pre-signed URL with an unguessable name, delivered to the verified account rather than returned in the response."

Mock 13

A full transcript, annotated

Technical screen · the first eight minutes, where it is decided
INTERVIEWER  "Write a function that returns each employee's
             average performance score."

⚠ THE WEAK OPENING
  CANDIDATE  "Sure —" [starts typing]
  ⇒ four undefined decisions are now being guessed at silently.

✅ THE STRONG OPENING
  CANDIDATE  "Before I write it — a couple of things I'd want to
             pin down. What should an employee with no reviews
             return? Zero, null, or should they be omitted?"
  INTERVIEWER "What would you suggest?"
  CANDIDATE  "Null, or omit them. Zero reads as a bad review
             rather than a missing one, and if this feeds a
             ranking it would push them to the bottom
             incorrectly."
  ⭐ SCORED: has judgement, not just an answer.

  CANDIDATE  "Second — a missing score versus a score of zero.
             If some reviews have no score recorded, averaging
             them as zero permanently drags that person down.
             I'd exclude nulls from the average and count them
             separately."
  ⭐ SCORED: knows the null/zero distinction is a real bug.

  CANDIDATE  "And the grain — average across all time, or per
             cycle? 'Their average' quietly compares a ten-year
             veteran with a new joiner."
  INTERVIEWER "Good. All time is fine."
  CANDIDATE  "Then my approach: I'll separate fetching the
             scores from computing the average, so the rule is
             testable with a plain list and no database. Let me
             write two examples first."
  ⭐ SCORED: names the seam, and pins the spec with examples.

⇒ EIGHT MINUTES IN, NO CODE WRITTEN, AND THE ROUND IS ALREADY
  GOING WELL. That is not a paradox — it is what the round is
  for.
Mock 14

Where interviews turn

The clarifying question

Asked in the first two minutes. It reframes you from "candidate solving a puzzle" to "colleague scoping work".

The unprompted edge case

Naming the empty list or the double-click before being asked. Both are certainties, not risks.

The cost sentence

"…and what that costs me is X." The most reliable marker of experience in any round.

The graceful correction

Being challenged, checking, and adjusting. Interviewers deliberately test this; digging in is fatal.

The honest gap

"I haven't used that — here's what I'd expect and how I'd verify it." Read as maturity, not weakness.

The structural close

Ending a bug story with what you changed so the class cannot recur. Turns "fixed a bug" into "owns a system".

Their side of the table

Loop 15

Questions worth asking them

AskBecause the answer tells you
"What does the deploy process look like, and how often do you deploy?"Engineering maturity, in one answer. Weekly with a pipeline is a different job from monthly by hand.
"What happened in your last incident, and what changed afterwards?"Whether post-mortems are blameless and whether anything actually changes.
"How does work get prioritised, and who decides?"Whether engineers have input, or receive a queue.
"What is the code review culture — who reviews whom?"Knowledge flow, and whether seniors are reviewable.
"What is the most frustrating part of working here?"The highest-signal question on this list. Everyone has one. A candid answer is a good sign; "nothing really" is not.
"How much of the codebase is generated, and how do you review it?"Whether they have thought about the thing every team is currently facing.
"What does success look like for me at three and twelve months?"Whether the role is actually defined.
"Why is this role open?"Growth, backfill, or churn — and how they answer matters as much as what they say.
Loop 16 · read this one twice

Why candidates actually get rejected

Rarely the reasons people assume
⭐ THE MOST COMMON REAL REASONS, IN ROUGH ORDER

  ①  SOLVED THE WRONG PROBLEM. Confidently, and without asking
     a single clarifying question.
  ②  COULD NOT EXPLAIN THEIR OWN PAST WORK past level one.
     Especially a number on the CV they could not stand behind.
  ③  NO TRADE-OFFS IN ANY ANSWER. Every choice presented as
     obviously correct, with no cost named.
  ④  DEFENDED A WRONG ANSWER when challenged. This one is
     close to fatal on its own.
  ⑤  SILENCE IN A CODING ROUND. Nothing scoreable happened.
  ⑥  "WE" WITH NO "I" in every story, so no individual
     contribution could be identified.
  ⑦  OVER-ENGINEERED EVERYTHING — microservices and Kubernetes
     for fifty requests a second, with no estimate behind it.
  ⑧  NO EDGE CASES, ever, unprompted.
  ⑨  BLUFFING. Almost always detected, and it retroactively
     devalues everything else you said.
  ⑩  DISPARAGED A PREVIOUS EMPLOYER. Read as a preview.

⚠ WHAT IS ALMOST NEVER THE REASON
  · not knowing one specific library or syntax
  · a suboptimal first solution that you improved
  · asking for a hint after genuinely trying
  · saying "I don't know" — ⭐ WHEN FOLLOWED BY HOW YOU WOULD
    FIND OUT
  · nerves

⭐ SO THE PREPARATION THAT ACTUALLY MOVES THE OUTCOME IS NOT
  MORE FACTS. It is: clarify before solving · narrate · name
  the cost · prepare your own CV three levels deep · and
  practise being wrong gracefully.
Loop 17

Negotiation, briefly

Three things worth knowing

Do not give the first number if you can avoid it. "What is the range budgeted for this role?" is a normal, professional question and it usually gets an answer.

Negotiate the whole package. Base, bonus, equity, start date, remote arrangement, learning budget. Some of those are easier for them to move than base.

Get it in writing before resigning. Pleasant verbal agreements are not offers, and the gap between them is where problems happen.
