Part I

Deciding

Senior 01

Decision frameworks

Match the process to the decision, not the reverse
⭐ THE FIRST MOVE: CLASSIFY THE DECISION.

  REVERSIBLE AND CHEAP     ⇒ just decide. A meeting costs more
                             than being wrong.
  REVERSIBLE AND EXPENSIVE ⇒ decide fast, but instrument it so
                             you find out quickly.
  IRREVERSIBLE             ⇒ ⭐ SLOW DOWN. Data models, public
                             APIs, a vendor holding your data,
                             anything that touches employment
                             decisions.

  ⚠ THE TWO FAILURES ARE SYMMETRIC AND BOTH COMMON: agonising
    over a reversible choice, and rushing an irreversible one.
    ⭐ MOST ENGINEERING DECISIONS ARE FAR MORE REVERSIBLE THAN
      THEY FEEL — which argues for deciding and moving.

THE QUESTIONS THAT ACTUALLY RESOLVE THINGS
  ①  WHAT ARE WE OPTIMISING FOR? ⭐ half of all technical
     disagreements are two people optimising different things
     without saying so. Surface it and the argument often
     dissolves.
  ②  WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND? ⇒ if nothing would, it is not
     a position, it is a preference.
  ③  WHAT IS THE COST OF BEING WRONG, AND WHO PAYS IT?
  ④  WHAT IS THE SIMPLEST THING THAT COULD WORK, and why is it
     insufficient?
  ⑤  WHAT DO WE DO IF THIS IS WRONG IN SIX MONTHS?

⭐ AND THE HABIT: DISAGREE AND COMMIT. Once a decision is made,
  execute it properly even if you argued the other way. ⚠ A
  team where the loser of an argument quietly undermines the
  outcome cannot make decisions at all — and re-litigating a
  settled choice is more expensive than either option was.

⇒ WRITE IT DOWN. An ADR (Volume II) for anything architectural:
  context, decision, alternatives, consequences.
Senior 02

Trade-offs, articulated

The sentence, one last time

"I chose X over Y because Z, and it costs me W."

If you cannot fill in W, you have not made a decision — you have accepted a default. This is the single most reliable marker of experience in any technical conversation, and it appears in every volume of this handbook because it is the same skill wearing different clothes.

The trade-offs you will actually face
SPEED NOW  ←→  SPEED LATER
  ⭐ THE MOST COMMON, AND THE MOST OFTEN DECIDED DISHONESTLY.
    Shipping fast is frequently correct — for a hypothesis you
    expect to discard. It is a disaster for a data model.
  ⇒ THE HONEST VERSION: "we are taking a shortcut here, it will
    cost roughly a week to undo, and we will undo it when X."
    Named and scheduled, that is a decision. Unnamed, it is
    just debt.

BUILD  ←→  BUY
  building costs engineering time forever, not once. ⭐ THE REAL
  QUESTION IS NOT COST — IT IS WHETHER THIS IS YOUR
  DIFFERENTIATOR. Nobody was ever promoted for writing an
  in-house authentication system.

CONSISTENCY  ←→  AVAILABILITY · LATENCY  ←→  THROUGHPUT ·
FLEXIBILITY  ←→  SIMPLICITY
  Volume II covers these. ⭐ AND THE ONE WORTH REPEATING:
  every abstraction added for future flexibility is complexity
  paid today for an option that usually expires unused.

GENERALITY  ←→  FITNESS
  a solution that handles every case handles none of them
  particularly well. ⇒ solve the case you have.

⭐ AND THE META-SKILL: MAKE THE TRADE-OFF VISIBLE TO THE PEOPLE
  WHO OWN THE CONSEQUENCE. "We can ship in two weeks with a
  manual step, or five weeks fully automated" is a business
  decision presented properly. Deciding it silently in a
  technical design is where engineers overstep — and where they
  get blamed for an outcome someone else would have chosen
  differently.
Senior 03

Technical debt

Four kinds, and only one is a problem
①  DELIBERATE AND DOCUMENTED — ⭐ THIS IS NOT A PROBLEM.
   "We hardcoded the tax rate because the rules land in
   Q3; here is the ticket." That is a loan with terms.

②  DELIBERATE AND UNDOCUMENTED — a shortcut nobody recorded.
   It becomes archaeology, and the next person assumes it was
   intentional design.

③  ACCIDENTAL — we did not know better at the time. Normal, and
   it is what a codebase looks like after learning.

④  ⚠ BIT ROT — the code did not change; the world did.
   Dependencies aged, the API moved, the assumption expired.
   ⭐ THE ONLY KIND THAT GROWS WHILE YOU DO NOTHING, which is
     why "we'll deal with upgrades later" compounds badly.

⭐ THE FRAMING THAT GETS DEBT PAID DOWN: TALK ABOUT INTEREST,
  NOT AESTHETICS.
  ⚠ "the code is messy" ⇒ nobody funds that.
  ✅ "every change to reporting takes three days instead of one
     because the logic is duplicated in four places, and we
     make about two changes a month" ⇒ that is a quantified
     cost, and it can be prioritised against features.

HOW IT ACTUALLY GETS PAID
  · ⭐ ALONGSIDE FEATURE WORK, IN THE AREA YOU ARE ALREADY
    TOUCHING. This is the only approach that reliably works.
  · a standing allocation — one day a fortnight — that is
    genuinely protected
  ⚠ NOT a "refactoring sprint" scheduled for later. It never
    arrives, and if it did it would be a large risky change
    with no feature to justify it.

⚠ AND THE HONEST WARNING: NOT ALL DEBT IS WORTH PAYING. Ugly
  code in a stable module nobody touches costs nothing. ⭐ PAY
  DEBT WHERE YOU WORK; TOLERATE IT WHERE YOU DO NOT.
Senior 04

Refactor or rewrite

Refactor whenRewrite when
The structure is wrong but the behaviour is right and understoodThe requirements have fundamentally changed — you are building a different thing
You can proceed incrementally, shipping throughoutThe technology is genuinely unsupportable — an abandoned framework, an unpatched runtime
Tests exist, or you can add them firstThe system is small enough that a rewrite is days, not quarters
Almost always. This is the default.You have a specification — meaning the old system's behaviour is documented or covered by tests
Why rewrites fail, specifically

The old system's ugliness is mostly accumulated knowledge. Every strange conditional is a bug someone hit, an edge case a customer reported, a regulation. A rewrite discards all of it and rediscovers it one production incident at a time.

And the second reason: you must maintain two systems while the rewrite catches up to a moving target. The feature freeze required to prevent that is usually politically impossible.

If you must replace something, use the strangler fig — route one path at a time from old to new, keeping both alive. Incremental, reversible per route, and it never requires a freeze.

Senior 05

Choosing technology

The questions in the order that matters
①  DOES THE BORING OPTION WORK?
   ⭐ THE FIRST AND MOST IMPORTANT QUESTION. Postgres, a
     monolith, server-rendered pages, a cron job. Boring
     technology has known failure modes, abundant answers to
     its problems, and people who can be hired to work on it.
   ⇒ CHOOSE INTERESTING TECHNOLOGY DELIBERATELY AND RARELY, and
     spend that budget where it is your actual differentiator.

②  WHO WILL MAINTAIN THIS IN TWO YEARS?
   a tool one person understands is a risk with a name.

③  WHAT IS THE ECOSYSTEM LIKE?
   is there a maintained client for our language? are the
   questions we will have already answered somewhere? ⭐ AND —
   is the documentation good? You will read it far more than
   you expect.

④  WHAT IS THE EXIT?
   if this is wrong in a year, how hard is it to leave? A tool
   that processes data is a smaller commitment than one that
   holds it or holds your business logic.

⑤  WHAT DOES IT COST, INCLUDING OPERATIONS?
   the licence is rarely the expensive part.

⚠ THE BAD REASONS, AND THEY ARE THE COMMON ONES
  · it is what a large company at enormous scale uses ⭐ (their
    constraints are not yours, and their solution is a response
    to problems you do not have)
  · a conference talk was persuasive
  · someone wants it on their CV
  · it is new

⭐ AND THE HONEST NOTE ABOUT THIS PROJECT'S STACK: NestJS,
  Next.js, Postgres, Prisma, FastAPI, XGBoost. Every one is a
  boring, well-documented, widely-used choice with a large
  hiring pool. That is a feature.
Part II

Decomposing

Senior 06

Why decomposition is the number-one skill

The claim, and the evidence for it

Nearly every engineering failure is a decomposition failure. The estimate was wrong because the work was never broken down. The design was wrong because the problem was never separated from the proposed solution. The code is unmaintainable because the boundaries were drawn in the wrong places.

And it is the skill AI helps least with: a model can implement a well-specified piece and cannot tell you where the pieces should be, because that depends on what varies, who owns what, and what you will need to change next quarter.

Senior 07

Top-down and bottom-up

Two directions, and you need both
TOP-DOWN — split the goal into parts
  "attrition reporting" → data pipeline · the model · the API ·
  the dashboard · the notifications
  ⇒ good for planning and for communicating.
  ⚠ THE RISK: PLAUSIBLE BOXES THAT DO NOT SURVIVE CONTACT. The
    split feels clean and the real work does not divide that
    way, so estimates are confidently wrong.

BOTTOM-UP — start from something concrete and generalise
  "make one employee's risk score appear on one screen"
  ⇒ ⭐ THE VERTICAL SLICE, AND IT IS THE MORE VALUABLE HABIT.
    One thin path through every layer reveals the real
    integration problems, the missing permission, the field
    nobody modelled — in days rather than at the end.

⭐ THE PRACTICAL METHOD: TOP-DOWN TO COMMUNICATE, BOTTOM-UP TO
  DE-RISK. Draw the boxes so people can see the shape, then
  build one thin slice all the way through before building any
  box completely.

THE THREE CUTS THAT ALWAYS WORK
  ①  ⭐ SEPARATE GETTING THE DATA FROM DECIDING ABOUT IT.
     fetch → decide → act, with the middle piece pure. The
     decision becomes testable with no setup, readable, and
     reusable from a job or a script.
     ⚠ THE SMELL THAT SAYS YOU HAVE NOT: to test the rule you
       must create a user, a row and a fake request. That
       difficulty is a design problem announcing itself.
  ②  SEPARATE THE PIPELINE STEPS — each with one input shape
     and one output shape, each nameable in three words.
  ③  SEPARATE WHAT CHANGES FROM WHAT DOESN'T — put the seam
     where the churn is, so the churn lands in one place.

⭐ HOW BIG SHOULD A PIECE BE? SMALL ENOUGH THAT ITS NAME IS
  ACCURATE. If the name needs "and", cut again. ⚠ And the
  opposite failure is real: five files of two lines each means
  following the logic requires opening all five.
Senior 08

Requirements

Finding the problem behind the request
⭐ HALF OF ALL REQUESTS ARE A PROPOSED SOLUTION WEARING THE
  COSTUME OF A REQUIREMENT.

    asked:  "add a CSV export button"
    actual: "finance re-types these numbers every Monday"
    ⇒ a scheduled email, or a filtered read-only view, may be
      the real answer — and less work.

THE QUESTIONS THAT FIND IT
  ①  WHAT WILL YOU DO WITH IT? ⭐ the single most useful
     question in requirements gathering. The answer reveals the
     real workflow.
  ②  WHAT DO YOU DO TODAY, WITHOUT IT? the current workaround
     is a specification, and it is usually more honest than the
     request.
  ③  WHAT HAPPENS IF WE DO NOTHING? sizes the problem, and
     occasionally ends the conversation.
  ④  WHO ELSE IS AFFECTED? ⇒ in an HR system, ⭐ THE EMPLOYEE
     IS A STAKEHOLDER, and often not in the room.
  ⑤  HOW WILL WE KNOW IT WORKED?

THE FOUR UNKNOWNS TO NAME EVERY TIME
  SCALE      how many, how often, how big at the extreme?
  OWNERSHIP  whose data, who may see it, who may change it?
  FAILURE    what happens when this fails halfway?
  DONE       how do we know it worked, and what happens next?

⭐ AND THE RULE: ASSUMING IS FINE. ASSUMING SILENTLY IS NOT.
  Write the assumption in the ticket. It costs a sentence and
  it converts a future argument into a correction now.

⚠ THE ANTI-PATTERN: TAKING THE REQUEST LITERALLY AND BUILDING
  IT PERFECTLY. Delivering exactly what was asked when it does
  not solve the problem is a failure that looks like
  compliance — and it is the one juniors are most often praised
  for.
Senior 09

Scope and MVP

The most senior move available to you
⭐ CUTTING SCOPE IS A SKILL, NOT A FAILURE. And it is the
  fastest way to deliver value, reduce risk and learn whether
  the thing is wanted at all.

WHAT AN MVP ACTUALLY IS
  the smallest version that DELIVERS REAL VALUE TO A REAL USER
  and TEACHES YOU SOMETHING.
  ⚠ NOT: a broken version of the full thing. ⚠ NOT: the full
    thing with no error handling. ⭐ THOSE ARE PROTOTYPES
    MASQUERADING AS PRODUCTS, and they damage trust.

THE CUTTING QUESTIONS
  ·  WHAT IS THE ONE THING THIS MUST DO?
  ·  which half could ship next month instead?
  ·  ⭐ WHICH PART CAN BE MANUAL AT FIRST? A human running a
     script weekly is a legitimate version one, and it teaches
     you what to automate.
  ·  which user group can we serve first?
  ·  what could be a report instead of a feature?

⭐ AND THE HONEST FRAMING TO USE WITH STAKEHOLDERS:
  "Here is what I would cut first if we are short on time, and
  here is what I would not cut." That sentence demonstrates
  judgement, invites the right conversation, and protects the
  part that matters. It is far stronger than either "yes to
  everything" or "that's not possible".

ITERATIVE DELIVERY — the discipline underneath
  ⭐ EVERY ITERATION SHIPS SOMETHING USABLE. Not "the backend
    this sprint, the UI next" — that delivers nothing until
    both are done and hides integration risk until the end.
  ⇒ A THIN VERTICAL SLICE, END TO END, EVERY TIME.
Senior 10

Estimation

Why estimates are wrong, and what to do about it
⭐ THE ROOT CAUSE IS ALMOST ALWAYS THE SAME: THE ESTIMATE
  COVERED THE CODING AND NOT THE WORK.

  what gets forgotten, every time:
    understanding the problem · the edge cases · code review ·
    the review feedback · testing · the migration · deployment ·
    the documentation · ⭐ THE INTEGRATION WITH THINGS YOU DO
    NOT CONTROL · and the two days of other people's
    interruptions

WHAT ACTUALLY HELPS
  ①  ⭐ DECOMPOSE UNTIL EACH PIECE IS A DAY OR LESS. Estimation
     error compounds with size — a "two week" task is a guess,
     and ten one-day tasks is an estimate. This one habit does
     most of the work.
  ②  ESTIMATE A RANGE, AND SAY WHAT DRIVES IT. "Three to eight
     days; eight if the legacy export format is undocumented."
     ⭐ THE CONDITION IS THE USEFUL PART — it tells everyone
       what to resolve to narrow the range.
  ③  SEPARATE THE UNKNOWN INTO A SPIKE. Timebox an
     investigation, then estimate. ⚠ Estimating something you
     do not understand is fiction with a number attached.
  ④  TRACK YOUR OWN RATIO. If you are consistently 2× under,
     that is not a character flaw — it is a calibration
     constant, and you can apply it.

⭐ THE HONEST CONVERSATION THAT BUILDS TRUST: SAY WHAT YOU DO
  NOT KNOW.
  "I can commit to the API by Friday. The AI-service
  integration depends on how their auth works, which I have not
  seen — I will know by Wednesday."
  ⇒ THAT IS MORE USEFUL THAN A CONFIDENT NUMBER, and it is what
    makes your later commitments believable.

⚠ AND THE ONE THAT DAMAGES CAREERS QUIETLY: SILENCE WHEN AN
  ESTIMATE SLIPS. ⭐ TELL PEOPLE EARLY. A revised date is a
  manageable problem; a missed date discovered on the day is a
  trust problem, and trust is the thing seniority is made of.
Part III

Communicating

Senior 11

Why it multiplies everything

The uncomfortable arithmetic

A good decision nobody understands does not get implemented. A risk nobody hears about is not mitigated. A design nobody can follow gets rebuilt.

Which means communication is not a soft skill sitting beside the technical ones — it is the mechanism by which technical work has an effect. Two engineers of equal ability, one of whom can explain their reasoning, do not have equal impact, and the difference grows with seniority because the work increasingly happens through other people.

Senior 12

Writing a document people read

Structure, and one rule about order
⭐ ANSWER FIRST. THE CONCLUSION GOES AT THE TOP.
  ⚠ engineers instinctively write chronologically — context,
    investigation, findings, conclusion. That is the order you
    discovered it in, and the wrong order to read it in. A busy
    reader stops after three paragraphs, and they stop before
    the point.

THE SHAPE
  ①  THE ANSWER, IN ONE OR TWO SENTENCES.
     "We should move report generation to a job queue. It will
      take about a week and removes the timeouts affecting
      long-tenure employees."
  ②  WHY IT MATTERS — the cost of the status quo, in numbers if
     you have them.
  ③  THE OPTIONS CONSIDERED, and why not.
  ④  THE DETAIL, for whoever needs it.
  ⑤  WHAT YOU NEED — ⭐ AND BE EXPLICIT. A decision? A review?
     Nothing? A document with no ask produces no action.

THE RULES OF THE CRAFT
  · SHORT SENTENCES. One idea each.
  · CONCRETE OVER ABSTRACT: ⭐ "queries take 4 seconds" beats
    "performance is suboptimal". A number is an argument.
  · ⭐ WRITE FOR THE PERSON WHO WILL READ IT IN SIX MONTHS with
    none of today's context. Usually that person is you.
  · say what you are NOT proposing — it prevents the most
    common misreading
  · state the risks yourself. ⭐ IT IS MORE CREDIBLE THAN HAVING
    THEM FOUND FOR YOU, and it is how you are trusted with the
    next decision.

⚠ AND THE TEST BEFORE SENDING: CAN SOMEONE READ ONLY THE FIRST
  PARAGRAPH AND ACT CORRECTLY? If not, restructure — the
  problem is not the writing, it is the order.
Senior 13

Explaining to non-engineers

Instead ofSay
"We need to refactor the reporting module""Every change to reports takes three days instead of one. A week of work now makes future changes routine."
"There's an N+1 query""The page asks the database 200 separate questions instead of one. That's why it takes four seconds."
"The model has 0.72 PR-AUC""Of the ten people it flags, about three would have left. That's far better than guessing — and it means seven conversations that turn out fine."
"We should add caching""We can remember the answer for a minute. Pages get much faster, and a number could be up to a minute out of date."
"There's technical debt""We took a shortcut in March to hit the deadline. It costs us about two days a month now."
The technique underneath the right column
⭐ TRANSLATE INTO CONSEQUENCE, NOT ANALOGY.
  the instinct is to reach for a metaphor. The better move is
  to state the EFFECT ON SOMETHING THEY ALREADY CARE ABOUT:
  time, money, risk, or a person's experience.

THE FOUR MOVES
  ①  LEAD WITH THE IMPACT, not the mechanism.
  ②  USE THEIR UNITS — days, money, people, customers.
  ③  ⭐ OFFER A CHOICE, NOT A LECTURE. "Two weeks with a manual
     step, or five fully automated" respects that the decision
     is theirs and makes the trade-off visible.
  ④  ⭐ SAY WHAT YOU RECOMMEND. Presenting options without a
     recommendation is abdication dressed as neutrality —
     you have the most information in the room.

⚠ AND TWO THINGS TO AVOID
  · CONDESCENSION. Simplifying the mechanism is right;
    simplifying the person is not. They make harder decisions
    than yours with less information.
  · FALSE CERTAINTY to end a conversation quickly. ⭐ "I don't
    know yet, I'll know by Wednesday" is a complete and
    professional answer.
Senior 14

RFCs and proposals

For a decision that affects other people's work
THE SECTIONS
  PROBLEM       what is wrong, with evidence. ⭐ IF THE PROBLEM
                SECTION IS WEAK, NOTHING BELOW IT MATTERS —
                and most rejected proposals are rejected here.
  GOALS         what success looks like
  NON-GOALS     ⭐ THE MOST UNDER-USED SECTION. It prevents the
                three tangents that would otherwise derail
                every review.
  PROPOSAL      what you want to do
  ALTERNATIVES  what else, and why not. ⭐ INCLUDING "DO
                NOTHING", seriously considered.
  RISKS         and the mitigation for each
  MIGRATION     how we get from here to there, incrementally
  OPEN QUESTIONS what you genuinely have not resolved

⭐ WHY THE PROCESS IS WORTH IT EVEN WHEN THE ANSWER IS OBVIOUS:
  WRITING FORCES YOU TO NOTICE WHAT YOU HAVE NOT THOUGHT
  THROUGH. A surprising number of proposals are abandoned by
  their own authors while writing the alternatives section —
  and that is the process working, not failing.

HOW TO RUN IT
  · share it early, while you are still willing to change it.
    ⚠ A proposal circulated as a formality invites either
      rubber-stamping or resentment.
  · ⭐ ASK SPECIFIC PEOPLE FOR SPECIFIC REVIEW. "Can you check
    the migration section?" gets a real response; "thoughts?"
    gets silence.
  · set a decision date, or it drifts indefinitely
  · record the outcome — ⭐ INCLUDING A REJECTION AND ITS
    REASON. A record of the road not taken is one of the most
    useful documents a team owns.
Senior 15

Diagrams

One zoom level per diagram

Context, containers, or components. Mixing all three produces something unreadable that nobody updates — and then it misleads.

Label the arrows

Protocol and direction. "HTTP/JSON, sync" versus "event, async" is the most information-dense thing on the page, and every sync arrow is a dependency that can take you down.

Include what fails

The queue, the third party, the cache, the trust boundary. Diagrams that show only the happy path hide exactly what a review should discuss.

Whiteboard beats a tool

For thinking and for interviews. Draw the boxes, talk through one request, add detail under pressure. Polish is not the point.

Senior 16

Saying no

The skill that makes seniority sustainable
⚠ "NO" ALONE IS RARELY THE RIGHT ANSWER, AND NEITHER IS "YES"
  TO EVERYTHING. The first makes you an obstacle; the second
  makes you unreliable, because you will miss something.

⭐ THE TRADE-OFF SENTENCE IS THE ANSWER:
  "I can do that. It means X slips by two weeks — is that the
  right priority?"
  ⇒ THIS DOES THREE THINGS AT ONCE: it accepts the work,
    surfaces the real cost, and puts the prioritisation where
    it belongs. It converts a refusal into a decision for the
    person entitled to make it.

THE OTHER USEFUL FORMS
  · "Not now, and here is when." A date beats a vague deferral.
  · "Not this, but here is what solves the problem." ⭐ THE
    STRONGEST FORM — it addresses the need while declining the
    proposed solution, and it requires having asked reflex 08's
    questions.
  · "Yes, and here is what we should stop doing." Makes
    capacity visible.
  · ⭐ "I don't think we should build this, and here is why."
    Sometimes correct, and it is the version that requires the
    most standing. Use it deliberately.

⚠ WHERE "NO" IS NOT NEGOTIABLE
  · shipping something that leaks personal data
  · removing a permission check to hit a date
  · an AI feature making employment decisions with no human
    review or explanation
  ⭐ THESE ARE NOT PRIORITISATION CONVERSATIONS. State the
    reason plainly, in writing, and escalate rather than
    comply. That is what professional judgement means, and it
    is occasionally the whole job.
Part IV

Learning

Senior 17

How learning actually works

Four findings worth acting on
①  ⭐ RETRIEVAL BEATS REVIEW.
   trying to recall something strengthens it far more than
   re-reading it. ⇒ SO: close the tutorial and rebuild the
   thing from memory. The struggle IS the learning; the
   comfortable feeling of re-reading is not.

②  ⭐ UNDERSTANDING IS ATTACHMENT TO WHAT YOU ALREADY KNOW.
   new information sticks when it connects to an existing
   model. ⇒ ASK "WHAT IS THIS LIKE?" — FastAPI's Depends is
   NestJS's constructor injection; a decorator is a decorator
   in both languages. That is why this handbook keeps drawing
   those parallels.

③  SPACING BEATS CRAMMING. Revisit after a day, a week, a
   month. Ideas deepen with experience between visits, which is
   why re-reading a volume of this handbook in six months is
   genuinely different.

④  ⭐ TEACHING EXPOSES EVERY GAP INSTANTLY.
   explain it to a colleague, write it down, answer a question
   in a channel. Every fuzzy area becomes audible the moment
   you try to say it out loud. Nothing else works as fast.

⚠ AND THE TRAP THAT IS SPECIFIC TO THIS ERA: WATCHING SOMETHING
  BE SOLVED IS NOT LEARNING TO SOLVE IT. A model producing a
  working answer to your problem creates the feeling of
  understanding without the retrieval that builds it.
  ⭐ WHICH IS WHY VOLUME I RECOMMENDED SOLVING SOMETHING
    WITHOUT ASSISTANCE, WEEKLY, DELIBERATELY. Not nostalgia —
    it is the only way the judgement in these eight volumes
    accumulates.
Senior 18

Learning a new technology fast

A sequence that works, and it is not "read the docs"
1  ⭐ WHAT PROBLEM DOES THIS EXIST TO SOLVE?
   read the project's own "why" page or its original
   announcement. ⇒ WITHOUT THIS, EVERY DESIGN DECISION IN THE
   TOOL LOOKS ARBITRARY. With it, most become obvious.

2  WHAT IS IT LIKE THAT I ALREADY KNOW?
   map the concepts onto something familiar, and note where the
   analogy breaks — ⭐ THE PLACE IT BREAKS IS THE THING WORTH
   LEARNING.

3  BUILD THE SMALLEST REAL THING.
   not a tutorial you follow — something you want, however
   trivial. ⇒ you will hit the actual friction immediately.

4  READ THE ERRORS PROPERLY.
   the first ten errors teach the model of the tool faster than
   any guide, because each one is a mismatch between your
   assumption and its reality.

5  THEN SKIM THE DOCUMENTATION STRUCTURE.
   ⭐ NOW you know which parts matter, and the map means you can
     find things later. Reading documentation cover to cover
     first is slow and it does not stick.

6  ⭐ FIND THE IDIOMATIC EXAMPLE.
   a well-regarded open-source project using it properly.
   Reading how experienced people use a tool teaches
   conventions no reference documents.

7  LEARN THE FAILURE MODES.
   what goes wrong, what the confusing errors mean, what the
   community complains about. ⇒ this is what separates "I have
   used it" from "I can be trusted with it in production".

⭐ AND THE PRINCIPLE UNDERNEATH: LEARN THE MODEL, NOT THE API.
  APIs change; the model of what a tool is doing survives. This
  is exactly why Volume V taught layer caching rather than
  Dockerfile syntax.
Senior 19

Deep work

The economics of interruption
⭐ COMPLEX WORK REQUIRES HOLDING A LOT OF STATE IN YOUR HEAD,
  AND AN INTERRUPTION DISCARDS IT. Rebuilding that state takes
  fifteen to thirty minutes.
  ⇒ SO FOUR ONE-HOUR BLOCKS ARE NOT EQUIVALENT TO ONE
    FOUR-HOUR BLOCK. They are roughly half as productive for
    anything requiring depth, and this is why a fragmented day
    feels busy and produces little.

WHAT ACTUALLY HELPS
  · BLOCK TIME AND DEFEND IT — a recurring calendar block for
    the work that needs depth
  · BATCH THE SHALLOW WORK — reviews, messages, small fixes in
    one window rather than continuously
  · ⭐ LEAVE A NOTE FOR YOURSELF BEFORE STOPPING. "Next: check
    whether the guard runs before the pipe." Three lines that
    save twenty minutes of state rebuilding tomorrow, and it is
    the highest-return habit on this list.
  · notifications off during the block. All of them.

⭐ AND THE HONEST CAVEAT ABOUT SENIORITY: THE MORE SENIOR YOU
  GET, THE LESS OF YOUR DAY IS DEEP WORK — and that is not a
  failure. Reviewing, unblocking, deciding and explaining IS
  the work at that level. The mistake is expecting to do both a
  full IC workload and a senior's coordination load in the same
  week, and then feeling inadequate about the arithmetic.
  ⇒ WHAT TO PROTECT IS ENOUGH DEPTH TO STAY TECHNICALLY
    CREDIBLE, not the volume you had as a junior.
Senior 20

Staying current without burning out

A filter, because the field emits more than anyone can absorb
⭐ THE THREE-TIER FILTER

  FUNDAMENTALS — learn deeply, they last decades
    ⇒ how systems fail · data modelling · concurrency ·
      networking · evaluation · security thinking · the
      trade-offs in Volume II.
    ⭐ EVERYTHING IN THIS TIER IS AS TRUE NOW AS IT WAS IN 2010,
      and it is what this handbook is mostly made of.

  TOOLS — learn as needed, they last a few years
    frameworks, cloud services, libraries. ⇒ learn on demand,
    from the real problem. ⚠ LEARNING A FRAMEWORK YOU HAVE NO
    USE FOR IS EXPENSIVE AND IT DOES NOT STICK.

  NEWS — skim, and mostly ignore
    releases, launches, opinions. ⭐ A WEEKLY SKIM OF ONE OR TWO
      SOURCES IS SUFFICIENT. The genuinely important
      developments are still important a month later; the rest
      were noise, and you can tell which is which with hindsight
      for free.

⚠ THE TRAP: FEELING BEHIND BECAUSE OF THE NEWS TIER. It is
  designed to produce that feeling. ⭐ NOBODY KNOWS ALL OF THIS.
  The people who appear to are specialists in one area who
  sound confident about the rest.

⭐ THE HEALTHIEST HABIT: LEARN FROM THE PROBLEM IN FRONT OF YOU.
  Depth acquired while solving something real sticks, connects
  to what you already know, and is immediately useful. Depth
  acquired from a tutorial for a problem you do not have
  evaporates.

AND ONE PRACTICE WORTH THE TIME
  ⭐ READ ONE SYSTEM'S DESIGN A MONTH. Pick something you use
    and work out why it is shaped that way. Architecture
    intuition is pattern exposure, and you can accumulate it by
    reading rather than by building.

Senior 21

Skill shapes

I-SHAPED deep in one thing, narrow ⚠ fragile — when that thing moves, so does your value ─ DASH-SHAPED broad and shallow ⚠ useful in a small team, and hard to be trusted with anything difficult ⊤ T-SHAPED ⭐ deep in one, competent across many the standard target, and for good reason: the depth earns trust, the breadth lets you see how the pieces meet. ⊤⊤ COMB-SHAPED deep in two or three, broad across the rest what senior generalists actually look like after a decade. ⭐ HOW TO CHOOSE YOUR DEPTH — and it is not "what is in demand": · what do people already come to you for? · what do you find yourself reading about anyway? · where does your unusual background give you an advantage nobody else has? ⇒ ⭐ THE LAST ONE IS THE MOST VALUABLE AND THE MOST OVERLOOKED. A supply-chain background plus data skills is a genuinely rare combination; so is HR domain knowledge plus ML engineering. THE INTERSECTION IS WHERE YOU ARE HARD TO REPLACE, and it is usually something you already have rather than something you must acquire. AND THE BREADTH THAT PAYS MOST RELIABLY ⭐ ONE LAYER EITHER SIDE OF YOUR DEPTH. A backend engineer who understands the frontend's constraints and the database's internals is far more effective than one who knows a second backend framework.

Culture and process

Agile in practice, and what actually matters
⭐ THE FOUR PRACTICES THAT PRODUCE MOST OF THE BENEFIT,
  WHATEVER THE FRAMEWORK IS CALLED
  ①  SHORT CYCLES with something usable at the end
  ②  TALKING TO THE PERSON WHO WANTED IT, directly
  ③  ADJUSTING THE PLAN when you learn something
  ④  LOOKING AT WHAT WENT WRONG, WITHOUT BLAME

⇒ SCRUM AND KANBAN ARE TWO PACKAGINGS OF THOSE.
  SCRUM   fixed-length sprints, a committed scope, ceremonies.
          Suits work that can be planned in two-week units.
  KANBAN  continuous flow, a work-in-progress limit, no
          sprints. ⭐ SUITS INTERRUPT-DRIVEN WORK — support,
          platform, data teams.
  ⚠ AND THE CEREMONY TRAP: a team can perform every ritual and
    still not do ①–④. Standing up daily to read a board aloud
    is not communication.

⭐ THE ONE PRACTICE WITH THE BEST RETURN: THE BLAMELESS
  POST-MORTEM.
  the question is "what made this possible?", never "who did
  it". ⇒ A CULTURE WHERE THE ANSWER IS A PERSON PRODUCES HIDDEN
  INCIDENTS, and hidden incidents are how small failures become
  large ones.
  three outputs or it did not happen: what stops the CLASS,
  what DETECTS it sooner, what makes it SMALLER.
  ⚠ "we'll be more careful" is a wish, not an action item.

AND THE TWO SIGNALS OF A HEALTHY TEAM
  · ⭐ PEOPLE SAY "I DON'T KNOW" AND "I WAS WRONG" WITHOUT
    COST. That is psychological safety, and it is the strongest
    predictor of a team that finds problems early.
  · WORK IN PROGRESS IS LIMITED. Six things at 80% is worth
    nothing; two things finished is worth two things.
Senior 24

Business acumen

Enough to argue for your work in the language decisions are made in
HOW THE BUSINESS MAKES MONEY
  subscription · transactional · licensed · services. ⭐ IT
  DETERMINES WHAT MATTERS TECHNICALLY: a subscription business
  lives or dies on retention, so reliability and onboarding are
  strategic; a transactional one cares about conversion and
  payment reliability.

THE NUMBERS WORTH UNDERSTANDING
  REVENUE − COST OF SALES = GROSS MARGIN
    ⭐ CLOUD SPEND USUALLY SITS IN COST OF SALES, which is why
      infrastructure efficiency is a margin conversation and
      not just an engineering one.
  CAC and LTV — what a customer costs to acquire and is worth
  PAYBACK PERIOD — how long until a customer is profitable
  BURN AND RUNWAY — ⭐ this is why "we need six months to
    rebuild it" is sometimes simply unavailable, regardless of
    whether you are right

WRITING A PROPOSAL THAT GETS FUNDED
  ①  the problem in BUSINESS terms — cost, risk, or a missed
     opportunity
  ②  the cost of doing nothing, quantified if you can
  ③  what you propose, and what it costs
  ④  the expected impact, with your confidence in it stated
  ⑤  ⭐ WHAT YOU WILL MEASURE AND WHEN YOU WILL REPORT BACK.
     This is what distinguishes a proposal from a wish, and it
     is what earns the next one.

⭐ AND THE TRANSLATION THAT MATTERS MOST: "THIS REDUCES RISK"
  IS WEAK. "This removes a failure mode that has caused two
  incidents this quarter, each costing about four hours of
  eleven people's time" is a number, and numbers get funded.

⚠ THE MISTAKE ENGINEERS MAKE: ASSUMING THE BUSINESS DOES NOT
  UNDERSTAND. Usually they understand their side perfectly and
  have never been told the cost of yours in units they use.

The AI-era career strategy

What is changing, what is not, and what to do
WHAT IS GENUINELY CHANGING
  · producing code is much cheaper ⇒ the bottleneck moved to
    deciding, judging and integrating
  · the floor rose: routine implementation is less of a
    differentiator than it was
  · ⭐ THE VOLUME OF CODE NOBODY FULLY READ IS GROWING, which
    makes review, evaluation and debugging more valuable, not
    less

WHAT IS NOT CHANGING
  · someone must decide what to build, and be accountable
  · someone must know whether it is correct
  · someone must explain it to the people affected
  · someone must be woken at 3 a.m. and own the outcome
  ⭐ ALL FOUR ARE JUDGEMENT AND ACCOUNTABILITY, and neither
    delegates.

THE ROLES THAT ARE GROWING
  anything with EVALUATION in it · anything at the boundary
  between a model and a consequence · domain specialists who
  can build · the people who make other engineers faster and
  safer

⭐ THE STRATEGY, IN FOUR LINES
  ①  GO DEEP ON SOMETHING THAT COMPOUNDS — a domain, a system
     class, evaluation, reliability.
  ②  GET FLUENT WITH THE TOOLS WITHOUT DEPENDING ON THEM.
     Volume I's habits: review everything, verify
     independently, solve something unaided weekly.
  ③  BUILD THE THINGS THAT MAKE OTHERS FASTER — the eval
     harness, the single path every query goes through, the
     constraint that removes a bug family permanently.
  ④  WRITE AND EXPLAIN. ⭐ The engineer who can say why is the
     one who gets to decide.

⚠ AND THE ANXIETY, ADDRESSED DIRECTLY: THE THING THAT MAKES YOU
  REPLACEABLE IS NOT AI — IT IS BEING ABLE TO DO ONLY WHAT IS
  ALREADY WRITTEN DOWN SOMEWHERE. Everything in these eight
  volumes that is worth learning is the part that is not.

Capstone 27

The mental models

Eight, and they recur across every volume of this handbook
①  ⭐ SOLVE THE PROBLEM, NOT THE REQUEST.
   half of all tasks are a proposed solution in disguise. Ask
   what it is for before building it.

②  ⭐ WHAT BREAKS, AND WHO NOTICES? (blast radius)
   a cosmetic glitch, a wrong number in a decision, money
   moving, or private data reaching the wrong person. THE
   ANSWER SETS HOW MUCH CARE THE TASK DESERVES — and pretending
   all code deserves equal rigour spends your care in the wrong
   place.

③  MAKE THE MISTAKE IMPOSSIBLE, NOT UNLIKELY.
   a comment · a convention · a review · a test · a lint rule ·
   a type · one path everything traverses · a database
   constraint. ⭐ AFTER EVERY BUG, ASK WHICH RUNG UP THIS COULD
   MOVE. Constraints outlive discipline.

④  THE DETERMINISTIC SYSTEM COMPUTES; THE MODEL NARRATES.
   compute the number where it is reproducible and auditable;
   let the model write the sentence around it.

⑤  ⭐ NAME WHAT THE CHOICE COSTS.
   "I chose X over Y because Z, and it costs me W."

⑥  IMAGINE THE FAILURE FIRST.
   zero · one · many · null · twice · order · race · lie ·
   halfway.

⑦  DUPLICATION IS CHEAPER THAN THE WRONG ABSTRACTION.
   two similar things are often two things. Wait for the third.

⑧  ⭐ BORING IS A FEATURE.
   the best compliment a codebase can receive is that it is
   unsurprising, and the best architecture is usually one
   database and one well-structured service.
Capstone 28

The ritual before code

⭐ NINETY SECONDS THAT CHANGE THE NEXT WEEK ① RESTATE THE TASK IN ONE SENTENCE. if you cannot, you do not understand it yet — and nothing you produce will be reviewable. ② NAME THE FOUR UNKNOWNS. SCALE · OWNERSHIP · FAILURE · DONE. ask about at least one; state assumptions for the rest. ③ STATE THE BLAST RADIUS. what breaks if this is wrong, and who notices. ④ SAY THE APPROACH IN ONE SENTENCE, BEFORE WRITING IT. a wrong approach costs one sentence to reject; a wrong implementation costs a review. ⑤ WRITE DOWN TWO CONCRETE EXAMPLES — input and expected output. ⭐ THEY PIN THE SPECIFICATION, CATCH THE MISUNDERSTANDING, AND BECOME YOUR TESTS FOR FREE. ⑥ NAME THE TRADE-OFF YOU ARE MAKING, AND ITS COST. ⑦ DECIDE WHAT YOU ARE *NOT* DOING, and say so in the ticket. ⭐ AND THE SAME LIST IS WHAT AN INTERVIEW SCORES, WHAT A DESIGN REVIEW ASKS, AND WHAT A GOOD PR DESCRIPTION CONTAINS. It is one habit with three payoffs — which is why it is the last thing in this handbook worth memorising.
Capstone 29

What "done" means to a senior

The definition that separates shipped from finished
A JUNIOR'S DONE          A SENIOR'S DONE
  it works                 □  it works, including at the edges
  tests pass               □  the failure paths are handled and
                              visible
  merged                   □  authorisation is enforced in code,
                              on every path
                           □  it is observable — logs, metrics,
                              a correlation id
                           □  ⭐ IT CAN BE TURNED OFF WITHOUT A
                              DEPLOY
                           □  ⭐ IT CAN BE UNDONE, or the reason
                              it cannot is stated
                           □  the migration is compatible with
                              the version still running
                           □  someone else can understand it —
                              names, and a why
                           □  the person who asked has seen it
                              and agrees it solves their problem
                           □  what you did NOT do is written
                              down

⭐ THE TWO ITEMS THAT MATTER MOST AND ARE MOST OFTEN MISSING:
  CAN IT BE TURNED OFF, AND CAN IT BE UNDONE. Everything else
  on the list is recoverable; those two are what determine how
  bad the worst day gets.

AND THE LAST ONE, WHICH IS THE WHOLE JOB
  ⭐ THE PERSON WHO ASKED HAS SEEN IT AND AGREES IT SOLVES THEIR
    PROBLEM. Code that satisfies every technical criterion and
    does not solve the problem is not done — it is finished,
    which is a different thing.

The production stack

What "production-grade" actually consists of
THE LAYERS OF A REAL SYSTEM — and this project's answer

  RUNTIME        containers on a managed platform
  CONFIG         environment variables, one artefact promoted
                 across environments
  SECRETS        a managed store, injected at runtime, never in
                 an image or a log
  DATA           managed Postgres (Neon), migrations in the
                 repository, tested restores
  CACHE / QUEUE  Redis where it earns its place; a queue for
                 anything unbounded
  ENTRY          a load balancer with managed TLS; a CDN for
                 static assets
  IDENTITY       short-lived tokens, checks in code, roles per
                 workload
  OBSERVABILITY  structured logs with a correlation id, four
                 metrics per service, traces, an external uptime
                 check
  DELIVERY       CI on every change, one artefact, rolling
                 deploys, verify-then-keep or roll back
  SAFETY         feature flags, budget alerts, runbooks for the
                 four known failures

⭐ THE POINT OF THE LIST IS NOT TO ADOPT ALL OF IT. It is to
  know which line you are missing, and to be able to say
  whether that is a deliberate choice or an oversight.
  ⇒ A SMALL TEAM WITH FIVE OF THESE DONE WELL AND FIVE
    KNOWINGLY DEFERRED IS IN BETTER SHAPE THAN ONE WITH TEN
    HALF-CONFIGURED.

⚠ AND THE ONE THAT IS NEVER OPTIONAL, WHATEVER THE SCALE:
  BACKUPS WITH A RESTORE YOU HAVE ACTUALLY TESTED. Everything
  else on this list degrades gracefully. That one does not.
Capstone 31

The five levels

Where this handbook has been taking you
1  IT WORKS
   the feature does what was asked, on the happy path.
   ⭐ MOVES YOU UP: the edge catalogue, applied to everything
     you write, out loud, for a month.

2  IT WORKS PROPERLY
   errors, edges, tests, debugging by bisection.
   ⭐ MOVES YOU UP: name the four unknowns before starting, and
     ask about at least one.

3  IT'S DESIGNED
   a feature end to end — schema, API, states, permissions,
   failure. Trade-offs with their costs named.
   ⭐ MOVES YOU UP: argue a requested feature DOWN in scope and
     be right. Ask "who can see this, per field?" where nobody
     else did.

4  IT'S THE RIGHT THING, AND IT WILL LAST
   find the problem behind the request · say no with an
   alternative · see the security consequence before the
   feature exists · design so mistakes are unrepresentable ·
   close bugs at the class level.
   ⭐ MOVES YOU UP: build the thing that makes everyone else
     faster and safer.

5  IT CHANGES HOW WE BUILD
   set the standard · own the measurement system · say which of
   the last ten proposals should not have been built · make the
   uncomfortable call in the room where it is made.
   ⚠ NOBODY REACHES THIS RUNG BY LEARNING A FIFTH FRAMEWORK.

⭐ THE HONEST SHAPE OF IT: LEVEL 1 → 2 IS KNOWLEDGE. EVERY
  LEVEL AFTER THAT IS A QUESTION YOU LEARNED TO ASK BEFORE YOU
  STARTED.
  ⇒ which is why this handbook has eight volumes of mechanism
    and one recurring habit — and the habit is the part that
    moves you.
