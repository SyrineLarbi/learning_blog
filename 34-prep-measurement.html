The premise

Why this comes first

What this curriculum is

The prep/ folder exists for one purpose: to make each technology you already claim survive forty-five minutes of pressure from someone who has shipped more than you. It was built after measuring the existing 95,000 lines of documentation, so it deliberately does not repeat what is already covered well — it adds the layer underneath.

This volume is the part that comes before any of the technology depth, because two things determine whether the rest lands: whether you can draw your own system without notes, and whether you can defend a number.

Part I

Reconstructing your own architecture

Source file 02 — the exercise that makes every later round easier.

Topic 01

Reconstructing it

The layer this adds

You built the system, so you know it works. What you probably cannot do from memory is state why each boundary is where it is — and that is the entire deep-dive round.

The exercise: rebuild the architecture diagram from scratch, on paper, without opening the repository. Then open the repository and find what you got wrong. What you got wrong is your study list.

The method
  1. List the deployable pieces and the protocol between each pair. Frontend, backend, AI service, database, cache, queue, storage.
  2. Mark every synchronous arrow. Each one is a dependency that can make you slow or down — and naming them is how you answer "what happens when the AI service is unavailable?"
  3. Mark the trust boundaries — where data crosses out of your control.
  4. Trace one request end to end in words. The report-generation path, because it touches every component.
  5. For each boundary, write one sentence on why it exists. If you cannot, that is a gap you will be asked about.
Where it goes wrong
  • Drawing the diagram you intended rather than the one that shipped. The intended version has no retry logic and no manual step in it.
  • Omitting the pieces that fail — the queue, the third party, the cache.
  • Not knowing which calls are synchronous, which makes every failure question unanswerable.
You must be able to answer

"Why three services rather than one?" — and the answer has to be a specific, stated reason (Python has the ML ecosystem), not a preference. "Microservices are modern" fails this question.

Topic 02

The eight questions to have answers to

The questionWhat a defensible answer contains
"Why did you split the AI service out?"One stated reason, plus the cost you accepted: a network call that can fail, no shared transaction, a second deployment.
"Where does truth live for each fact?"One store per fact, and what you do about anything duplicated for read performance.
"What is the slowest path, and why?"A measured number, not an impression — and which component owns the time.
"What happens if the database is unreachable?"Which features degrade and which stop. If the answer is "everything stops", say so; that is a legitimate design choice made explicitly.
"How do you know it is healthy right now?"The specific signals. If the honest answer is "we would hear from a user", say that — it is a better answer than an invented monitoring story.
"What would break at ten times the data?"Name one specific thing and one specific fix. Vague scaling answers read worse than a narrow, correct one.
"What would you change if you rebuilt it?"A real change with a reason. This is where they test whether you learned anything.
"What was the hardest decision?"A trade-off with both sides named, and what it cost you.

Topic 04

What a number must carry

The claim that gets probed hardest

"50 seconds to 6 seconds." It is the strongest thing on a CV and the most dangerous, because it invites exactly four questions — and if you cannot answer all four, the claim damages you more than its absence would.

The four questions, and what each requires you to have recorded
①  "WHAT WAS THE ACTUAL BOTTLENECK?"
   requires: a profile or trace from before the change, not a
   memory. ⭐ "The report endpoint made one query per employee —
   1,400 round trips" is an answer. "It was the database" is not.

②  "HOW DID YOU FIND IT?"
   requires: the tool and the method. `EXPLAIN ANALYZE`, a
   trace, query logging with a count. ⇒ THE METHOD IS SCORED
   MORE HEAVILY THAN THE RESULT, because the method generalises.

③  "WHAT DID YOU TRY THAT DIDN'T WORK?"
   requires: honesty, and it is a positive signal. "I added a
   cache first and it hid the problem until it missed" is a
   better answer than a clean narrative, because clean
   narratives sound rehearsed and nobody's debugging is clean.

④  "HOW DO YOU KNOW IT STAYED FIXED?"
   requires: something ongoing — a logged duration, an alert, a
   dashboard. ⚠ IF THE ANSWER IS "I MEASURED IT ONCE", SAY SO
   PLAINLY. That is a real answer with a real limitation, and it
   is far better than implying monitoring you do not have.

⭐ AND THE MEASUREMENT DISCIPLINE THAT MAKES ALL FOUR POSSIBLE:
  RECORD THE BEFORE NUMBER, WITH ITS CONDITIONS, BEFORE YOU
  CHANGE ANYTHING. Data volume, concurrency, cache state,
  environment. A before number with no conditions attached
  cannot be compared to anything.
Topic 05

Percentiles, properly

The layer this adds

Everyone knows "use p95 not the average". The layer underneath is why averages mislead in a specific, predictable way, and what a percentile does and does not tell you.

The mechanism
  • Latency distributions are right-skewed with a long tail. Most requests are fast; a few are very slow. The mean sits between them and describes neither.
  • p95 means "95% were at least this fast". It does not mean 5% of users are unhappy — a user making twenty requests has a good chance of hitting the tail at least once, so per-user experience is worse than per-request percentiles suggest.
  • Percentiles do not average. You cannot take the p95 of two servers and average them to get the fleet p95. That requires the underlying distribution, which is why monitoring tools store histograms.
  • The tail is where the causes are. A p99 that is ten times the p50 usually means a distinct mechanism — a cold cache, a lock, a retry, a specific customer with far more data.
Where it goes wrong
  • Quoting a single number with no percentile at all.
  • Measuring at low concurrency, where queueing does not yet exist, then claiming the number holds under load.
  • Averaging percentiles across instances or time windows.
You must be able to answer

"Your p50 is 200 ms and your p99 is 8 seconds — what is happening to that 1%?" A strong answer names a mechanism and how you would confirm it, rather than describing it as noise.

Topic 06

Benchmarking fundamentals

How to produce a number somebody else could reproduce
⭐ THE FIVE CONDITIONS A BENCHMARK MUST STATE, OR IT IS AN
  ANECDOTE:
  ①  THE DATA VOLUME. 200 rows and 2,000,000 rows are different
     systems, and most surprising results are a volume
     difference in disguise.
  ②  THE CONCURRENCY. One request at a time never reveals a
     pool limit, a lock, or queueing.
  ③  THE CACHE STATE. Cold or warm — a warm-cache number
     compared to a cold-cache number is not a comparison.
  ④  THE ENVIRONMENT. Your laptop, a container, production
     hardware. And whether anything else was running.
  ⑤  THE NUMBER OF RUNS AND THE SPREAD. ⭐ ONE RUN IS NOT A
     MEASUREMENT. Run it enough times to see the variance, and
     report the variance.

⚠ THE FOUR WAYS BENCHMARKS LIE
  · WARM-UP EFFECTS. JIT compilation, connection pools filling,
    OS page cache. ⭐ DISCARD THE FIRST RUNS DELIBERATELY, and
    say that you did.
  · MEASURING THE WRONG THING. Timing a function that returns a
    promise measures how long it took to create the promise.
  · COORDINATED OMISSION. If your load generator waits for a
    response before sending the next request, it stops sending
    during a stall — so the slow period is under-represented and
    your tail looks better than reality.
  · OPTIMISING THE BENCHMARK. A query fast on the test dataset
    because the test dataset fits in memory.

⭐ THE HABIT THAT MAKES YOUR CLAIMS DEFENSIBLE: WRITE THE
  BENCHMARK DOWN AS A SCRIPT, COMMITTED, WITH ITS CONDITIONS IN
  A COMMENT. Then "how do you know" has an answer you can point
  at rather than recall.
Topic 07

Before and after, honestly

Change one thing

Two changes and one improvement teaches you nothing about which caused it — and you will be asked which.

Same conditions both times

Same data volume, same concurrency, same cache state. Otherwise you have measured the conditions, not the change.

Report the distribution

"p50 from 1.2s to 180ms, p99 from 50s to 6s" is far stronger than one number, and it is what actually happened.

State what did not improve

Volunteering "the write path is unchanged" pre-empts the follow-up and reads as rigour.

Attribute honestly

If a colleague did the caching layer, say so. Interviewers probe for over-claiming and it collapses badly.

Keep the artefact

The query plan, the trace, the script. A claim with an artefact behind it is a different class of claim.

Topic 08

Database

In the order you should use them
①  QUERY LOGGING WITH A COUNT — ⭐ START HERE, ALWAYS.
   the ORM hides the SQL, so the single most valuable thing is
   seeing how many queries one request issues. An N+1 is
   obvious the moment the count is visible, and invisible
   otherwise.

②  `EXPLAIN ANALYZE` on the slow query.
   read: sequential scans on large tables · ⭐ ESTIMATED VS
   ACTUAL ROWS, the most informative line · the join strategy ·
   whether a sort spilled to disk.

③  THE SLOW QUERY LOG, to find candidates you did not suspect.

④  INDEX USAGE STATISTICS, to find indexes nobody uses — they
   are pure write cost.

⑤  CONNECTION POOL METRICS. ⭐ THE ONE PEOPLE FORGET: when
   everything is slow at once including trivial endpoints,
   suspect pool exhaustion rather than any individual query.

⭐ AND THE INTERPRETATION HABIT: A PLAN IS A TREE, READ INSIDE
  OUT, AND THE EXPENSIVE NODE IS USUALLY ONE NODE. Find it
  before proposing anything.
Topic 09

Backend

InstrumentAnswersThe detail that matters
A correlation id"Where did this request go?"Twenty lines of middleware, and it is the difference between debugging a three-service system and guessing at one.
Duration logging per handler"Which endpoint is slow?"Log the duration and the outcome. Aggregate later; you cannot reconstruct what you never recorded.
A trace / waterfall"Which call took the time?"Routinely reveals the actual bug — three calls that could have run in parallel running in sequence, or one call made forty times.
Event-loop lag"Is something blocking?"Node-specific and rarely watched. A rising lag means CPU-bound work is stalling every other request.
Heap snapshots"What is accumulating?"Take two, minutes apart, and diff. The growth is the leak — usually an unbounded cache or listeners added per request.
Saturation metrics"How full is it?"The leading indicator. Pool usage and queue depth rise before latency does.
Topic 10

Frontend

Three questions, three tools
"WHY IS THE FIRST LOAD SLOW?"
  ⇒ look at what is actually IN the bundle before guessing. It
    is normally one heavy dependency — a date library, a chart
    library, an icon set imported wholesale.
  ⭐ AND CHECK WHETHER IT NEEDED TO BE CLIENT-SIDE AT ALL. A
    `'use client'` high in the tree drags everything it imports
    into the browser.

"WHY DOES INTERACTION FEEL LAGGY?"
  ⇒ a profiler recording, looking at what re-renders on a
    keystroke. Almost always state lifted too high.
  ⚠ AND THE PATTERN THAT LOOKS INNOCENT: writing a style then
    reading a layout property in a loop — layout thrashing,
    which forces a synchronous recalculation each iteration.

"WHY DOES THE PAGE JUMP?"
  ⇒ layout shift: images and tables with no reserved space, and
    fonts swapping without a matched fallback.

⭐ AND THE MEASUREMENT POINT THAT APPLIES TO ALL THREE: MEASURE
  ON A THROTTLED CONNECTION AND A THROTTLED CPU. Your machine is
  not your users' machine, and a dashboard that is fine on a
  developer laptop can be unusable on a five-year-old office
  computer — which is exactly who uses internal HR tools.
Topic 11

Load and capacity

The layer this adds

A load test does not answer "is it fast". It answers "where is the breaking point, and which resource breaks" — and those are the two things a capacity claim needs.

The method
  • Ramp, do not spike. Increase gradually and watch for the knee — the point where latency stops rising linearly. That knee is your capacity number, and it is the one worth writing down.
  • Test a realistic mix. Hammering one cheap endpoint proves nothing; real traffic is mostly reads with occasional expensive writes, and the interaction is the point.
  • Watch the dependencies, not the CPU. Under load you usually find the connection pool limit, a downstream rate limit, or a metered API bill — rarely CPU.
  • Do the arithmetic first. A day is about 90,000 seconds, so a million actions a day is roughly 11 per second average and maybe 50 at peak — and that is a small number. Knowing this stops you over-building.
Where it goes wrong
  • Load-testing a shared or production service without telling anyone.
  • Coordinated omission in the load generator, which hides the tail.
  • Reporting throughput with no latency, or latency with no concurrency.
You must be able to answer

"How many concurrent users can it handle, and what breaks first?" Two numbers and one named resource. That is a capacity answer; anything vaguer is a guess.

Reference

Turning a claim into evidence

Run this on every technical claim you make in writing
FOR EACH CLAIM:
  □  what is the NUMBER, and what are its CONDITIONS?
  □  what ARTEFACT backs it — a plan, a trace, a script, a
     dashboard?
  □  what was the METHOD I used to find the cause?
  □  what did I TRY THAT FAILED?
  □  how do I know it STAYED fixed?
  □  ⭐ WHAT PART WAS SOMEONE ELSE'S WORK?
  □  what is the HONEST LIMITATION — measured once, one
     environment, one dataset?

⭐ AND THE DECISION THAT FOLLOWS: IF YOU CANNOT ANSWER THE FIRST
  FIVE, EITHER GO AND MEASURE IT PROPERLY OR TAKE THE NUMBER OFF
  THE PAGE. An undefended number is the single most reliable way
  to lose a deep-dive round — and a defended one is the single
  strongest asset you can bring to it.

⚠ AND THE LIMITATION THAT IS FINE TO STATE: "I measured this in
  staging with production-shaped data; I have not measured it
  under real concurrency." That is a professional answer. What
  is not fine is implying rigour you did not apply.
