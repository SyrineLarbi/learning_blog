Orientation

The TechCorp story

The setup

You have just joined TechCorp, a mid-size tech company of about 4,000 employees, as a data scientist. Each exercise is a real task from a different stakeholder — the IT director, the CHRO, your manager, a recruiting director, a VP of marketing, the board, an engineering lead, and finally the CEO.

The data is messy. The requirements are ambiguous. The stakeholders are non-technical. That is the point: the technical work in these exercises is not the hard part, and treating it as the hard part is the most common way to fail them.

Foundational

Exercises 01–02

Exercise 01 · IT Director · 4–5h

Data cleaning: corporate messy data

The brief

"Here are three exports — the HRIS, the payroll system and a survey tool. Nobody has ever joined them. Give me one clean dataset we can actually use."

They are non-technical, they believe this is a small task, and they will not answer a follow-up email for three days.

The traps
  • The join key does not exist. One source uses employee number, one uses email, one uses name. Matching on name produces false merges — and a wrong merge is worse than a missing row, because it silently attributes one person's data to another.
  • Missingness has a mechanism. The unhappiest employees skip the satisfaction survey, so those values are MNAR. Imputing the mean biases every downstream analysis toward contentment.
  • Outliers are not errors. A salary of 1 is an error; a salary of 400,000 is a real executive. Deleting the second deletes the finding.
  • Disguised nulls. 0, "N/A", "unknown", 1970-01-01 — the epoch means someone cast a zero. None of them read as missing to a null check.
  • Duplicates that are not identical. The same person twice with different spellings and a different id.
What to produce
  • One dataset, plus a decision log: every rule applied, the row count it affected, and the value it affected. Quantify twice — by rows and by value.
  • A was_missing flag for every imputed column. An imputed value that looks measured is worse than a null.
  • A reconciliation: your headcount against a number the IT director can check independently.
How to grade yourself

Could someone else rebuild your dataset from your log alone? Can you defend every dropped row? And did you keep the raw copies untouched — because a cleaning rule you cannot reverse is a decision you cannot revisit.

Exercise 02 · CHRO · 4–6h

EDA: investigation and storytelling

The brief

"Attrition is up. I have a board meeting in a week. Tell me what is going on."

That is the whole brief. No metric definition, no hypothesis, no time window.

The traps
  • Simpson's paradox is planted in this data. Attrition rises overall while falling in every department — because the department mix changed. Report the aggregate alone and you will be wrong in front of a board.
  • The cliff at the end. The final period looks catastrophic because it is incomplete. The most common false alarm in reporting.
  • Slicing until something looks dramatic. Three thousand employees sliced by department, level and tenure gives cells of nine, where every big percentage is noise.
  • Answering the question asked. "What is going on" is a symptom. The CHRO's decision is what to tell the board and what to do next quarter.
  • A deliverable nobody can use. Twenty charts and no conclusion.
What to produce
  • A one-page memo, answer first. The conclusion in two sentences, then why it matters, then the evidence, then what you are not claiming.
  • Three charts at most, each annotated — a line on the chart saying "reorganisation" where the step change is explains more than any styling.
  • The decomposition that resolves the paradox: rate change versus mix change, quantified.
How to grade yourself

Can the CHRO read your first paragraph and act correctly? Is every number in units they use — people and cost, not percentages of percentages? And did you state the effect size, not just that something is significant?

Exercises 03–05

Exercise 03 · Your manager · 4–5h

Feature engineering from domain knowledge

The brief

"Build the feature set for the attrition model. Talk to HR about what actually predicts leaving — do not just throw the columns in."

The traps
  • Post-outcome features. exit_interview_completed, handover_uploaded, access_revoked. Perfect predictors, all known after the decision. This is the leak the exercise is built around.
  • Aggregates that include the row itself. "Team attrition rate" computed over the whole period leaks the outcome of the very person you are predicting.
  • Encoding that invents an order. Cities as 1..50, so the model learns that city 40 is more than city 4.
  • High-cardinality one-hot. 400 job titles becomes 400 columns.
  • Proxies you did not think about. Postcode, school, working pattern and career gaps all correlate with protected attributes — so removing the obvious column is not sufficient.
What to produce
  • A feature table with, for each feature: what it means, how it is computed, and whether it would have been known at prediction time.
  • Ratios and differences rather than raw values — salary relative to the department median beats salary; months since last promotion beats hire date.
  • An explicit exclusion list, with the reason for each.
  • The feature logic in one module, callable from both training and serving.
How to grade yourself

Apply the one test to every row of your table: would this value have existed, with this value, at the moment I need the prediction? And can you explain each feature to an HR business partner in one sentence?

Exercise 04 · Recruiting Director · 5–7h

Supervised learning: model selection

The brief

"Which candidates should my team prioritise? And explain it to me — I am not technical, and I have to defend this to the people whose applications get deprioritised."

The traps
  • Advisory or decisive? If this ranks and the bottom of the ranking is never contacted, it is a decision about employment — a different bar for fairness, explainability and records.
  • No baseline. Without the current process measured, "83% accurate" means nothing.
  • Reaching for the complicated model. Logistic regression first; on tabular data of this size gradient boosting usually wins and the gap tells you whether complexity buys anything.
  • Tuning against the test set, then reporting that score.
  • An unexplainable recommendation. The stakeholder must defend it, so a model whose reasoning cannot be stated is unusable regardless of accuracy.
What to produce
  • A comparison table: baseline, logistic regression, boosting — same split, same metric named in advance, with the spread from cross-validation rather than a single number.
  • Per-subgroup evaluation. An overall 0.72 can be 0.75 for one group and 0.51 for another.
  • A non-technical explanation of one specific prediction, with contributing factors.
  • A recommendation with the trade-off stated: what this buys, what it costs, what you would not use it for.
How to grade yourself

Could the recruiting director repeat your explanation to a rejected candidate? Did you name a use you would refuse? And is your chosen model the simplest one that met the target — or the most accurate one you could build?

Exercise 05 · VP Marketing · 4–5h

Unsupervised learning: segmentation

The brief

"Segment our employees so we can tailor retention programmes. Give the segments names I can put in a deck."

The traps
  • Clustering always returns clusters. Ask for four and you get four, whether or not the data has any group structure. There is no accuracy score to hide behind.
  • Not scaling. Salary in tens of thousands and satisfaction between 0 and 1 are not comparable distances — salary will dominate every cluster purely because of its units.
  • The elbow method as an oracle. It suggests; it does not decide. And DBSCAN needs no k but does need density parameters.
  • Over-reading a t-SNE plot. Distances and cluster sizes in the picture are not meaningful. It is a visualisation, not evidence.
  • Segments that are not actionable. "Cluster 3" is not a name, and a segment nobody can do anything differently for is a chart rather than a finding.
What to produce
  • K-Means and DBSCAN compared, with your reason for choosing one — and an honest statement of whether the structure is real.
  • A profile per segment in business language: size, distinguishing characteristics, and the one intervention it suggests.
  • Names a VP can use, derived from the profile rather than invented.
  • A minimum size floor — a segment of three people identifies three people.
How to grade yourself

Would an HR business partner recognise these groups as real? That is the only validation available, and it is the correct one. And could you defend the number of clusters without appealing to a chart?

Exercises 06–07

Exercise 06 · The Board · 5–6h

Model evaluation and business decisions

The brief

"The board has approved a retention budget. Tell us where to spend it, and how confident you are."

The highest-stakes audience in the series, and the one least interested in your method.

The traps
  • Accuracy on imbalanced data. Predict "stays" for everyone and score 95%.
  • ROC-AUC on a rare class, which looks flatteringly high because true negatives are abundant. PR-AUC is the honest one.
  • The base rate. At 5% attrition a good model's flags are majority false-positive — about 32% precision for a model catching 90% of leavers with a 10% false-positive rate. Present that as "will leave" and you will be wrong two times in three, in front of a board.
  • Leaving the threshold at 0.5 and calling it neutral. It is an unexamined choice, and the cost matrix should set it.
  • Uncalibrated probabilities shown to humans as if they were probabilities.
  • No drift plan. A model approved once and never re-examined.
What to produce
  • A cost matrix agreed with the business: what a false positive costs (a manager's time, an awkward conversation) versus a false negative (a lost employee, replacement cost).
  • A threshold derived from that matrix, with the resulting precision and recall stated in people rather than percentages.
  • A calibration check, because a number is being shown to humans.
  • The value chain, with every assumption named and a range rather than a point.
  • A monitoring plan: input drift, prediction drift, and when the model is next reviewed.
How to grade yourself

Is every figure in the board's units? Did you state the false-positive reality before being asked? And is your confidence expressed as a range with its assumptions listed — because a precise number you cannot defend is worse than an honest interval.

Exercise 07 · Engineering Lead · 4–5h

ML pipeline: production readiness

The brief

"Your notebook is not deployable. Give me something my team can run, monitor and roll back."

The traps
  • Preprocessing reimplemented in the serving code — training/serving skew, which degrades production quality for no visible reason and is very hard to diagnose.
  • Saving the model rather than the pipeline. The preprocessing is part of the model.
  • No data version. Without a snapshot identifier the model is not reproducible at any price.
  • Inference in an async def. It is CPU-bound, so it blocks the event loop and the service stalls under any concurrency at all.
  • Loading the model per request rather than once at startup.
  • No rollback. Rolling back a model must be as fast as rolling back code.
What to produce
  • A single fitted pipeline artefact — imputation, encoding, scaling, model — saved with its library versions, feature order, training date, data version and metrics.
  • A FastAPI service that loads it once at startup, validates input with a Pydantic model, and runs inference in a plain def.
  • An integration test that posts a realistic payload and asserts the response shape — plus one that asserts an invalid payload is rejected with a 422.
  • The four logs that make a regression reproducible: model version, inputs, prediction, latency.
  • A monitoring plan and a documented rollback.
How to grade yourself

Could a backend engineer deploy this without asking you anything? Can you answer "which model produced this prediction" for any row? And is the preprocessing the same object in training and serving, not merely the same logic?

Exercise 08

Exercise 08 · The CEO · 10–14h

End-to-end corporate ML project

The one to do if you do only one

Ten phases from a stakeholder meeting to a monitored deployment. It contains a compressed version of every exercise above, and it is the piece worth showing an interviewer — because it demonstrates the thing almost no portfolio does: judgement about scope, measurement, and what you chose not to build.

The ten phases, and what each is graded on
①  THE STAKEHOLDER MEETING
   the CEO says a sentence. Turn it into a decision, a
   decision-maker, a cost asymmetry and a target.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: whether you argued the scope DOWN, and whether
     you asked what happens today without this.

②  PROBLEM FRAMING AND SUCCESS CRITERIA
   written down, agreed, before any data work.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: a target agreed in advance. Without it every
     result invites "can you make it better?"

③  DATA ASSESSMENT
   what exists, what is trustworthy, what is missing, what the
   label actually means.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: noticing that the label is subjective.

④  CLEANING AND FEATURES
   with a decision log and the point-in-time test applied.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: no post-outcome features. This is where the
     capstone is most often silently failed.

⑤  BASELINE
   the current process, or a rule, measured.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: having one at all.

⑥  MODELLING
   simplest first, complexity justified by the gap.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: restraint, and per-subgroup evaluation.

⑦  EVALUATION AND THRESHOLD
   the cost matrix, the threshold, calibration, and the value
   chain with its assumptions.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: honesty about the false-positive rate.

⑧  THE INTERFACE
   what the human sees, the evidence alongside the score, and an
   override that records a reason.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: framing it as a prompt, not a verdict.

⑨  DEPLOYMENT
   pipeline artefact, service, tests, logs, rollback.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: reproducibility from recorded inputs.

⑩  MONITORING AND THE LOOP
   input drift, prediction drift, an owner, a review date — and
   the feedback-loop problem: acting on predictions changes the
   data you will train on next.
   ⭐ GRADED ON: naming that problem unprompted.

⭐ AND THE FINAL DELIVERABLE THAT MATTERS MOST: A ONE-PAGE MEMO
  FOR THE CEO. Answer first, in money and people, with the
  assumptions listed and one honest paragraph on what could
  make this wrong. Everything above is the work; this is the
  only part they will read.
The question the capstone is really asking

Would you be comfortable explaining this system, in detail, to the employees it scores? If the answer is no, that discomfort is the finding — and saying so in the memo is a stronger result than any metric.

The traps, collected

Matching on names

A false merge attributes one person's data to another. Silent, and worse than a missing row.

Imputing MNAR data

The unhappiest people skip the survey. Filling the mean biases everything toward contentment.

Deleting real outliers

Sometimes the outlier is the finding.

The incomplete last period

The cliff at the end of every time series. The most common false alarm in reporting.

Simpson's paradox

The aggregate moves one way while every subgroup moves the other. Mix, not rate.

Post-outcome features

Exit interview, handover, access revoked. Great scores, useless model.

Self-referential aggregates

A team rate that includes the row you are predicting.

Accuracy on 5% base rate

95% by predicting nothing happens.

The default threshold

0.5 is a choice, not a neutral position.

Training/serving skew

The feature computed twice, slightly differently. Quality drops for no visible reason.

Clusters that mean nothing

Clustering always returns clusters. A domain expert is the only validation.

Cells of nine

Slicing until something looks dramatic. And below a floor, an aggregate identifies people.

How to grade yourself

The eight questions that apply to every exercise
□  ①  Can I state the DECISION this changes, and who makes it?
□  ②  Did I establish a BASELINE before claiming anything?
□  ③  For every feature: WOULD THIS HAVE BEEN KNOWN AT
      PREDICTION TIME?
□  ④  Is my metric right for the BASE RATE — and did I evaluate
      PER SUBGROUP?
□  ⑤  Is every number in the STAKEHOLDER'S UNITS: people,
      money, days?
□  ⑥  Did I write down what I DELIBERATELY DID NOT DO?
□  ⑦  Could someone else REPRODUCE this from what I recorded?
□  ⑧  Did I say what could make this WRONG?

⭐ SEVEN OF THOSE EIGHT ARE NOT TECHNICAL. That is the
  distribution the exercises are built to teach — and it is the
  distribution of real corporate data science work.

⚠ AND THE HONEST WARNING ABOUT SELF-STUDY: it is easy to read a
  brief, imagine the solution, and move on. ⭐ THESE ONLY WORK
  IF YOU PRODUCE THE DELIVERABLE — the memo, the decision log,
  the feature table, the running service. The deliverable is
  where the judgement actually gets exercised.
