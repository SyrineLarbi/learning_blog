The Supply Chain Analyst, A to Z. The whole job, explained from nothing.

This assumes you know nothing about supply chain and can read carefully. It is written in plain prose rather than in bullet points, because the things that matter here are relationships between quantities, and a list cannot show a relationship. There is no arithmetic in it. Everything a supply chain analyst calculates can be understood before it is computed, and understanding it first is what stops you producing confident nonsense later.

Thirty-two chapters · seven parts Assumes · nothing The spine · the triangle, and variability
Before you begin

How to read this

Read it in order the first time. The parts are built so that each one uses the one before it: you cannot understand safety stock without understanding variability, and you cannot understand why anyone cares about safety stock without understanding the triangle in Chapter 02. Skipping ahead produces the common failure of someone who knows the vocabulary and cannot use it.

There are two ideas that everything else hangs from, and it is worth naming them now so you can watch them recur.

The two ideas this whole course is built on

One — the triangle. Every supply chain decision trades service against cost against cash. You cannot improve one without paying in at least one of the others. Anyone who reports an improvement without naming what it cost has not finished the analysis.

Two — variability, not the average. Almost everything a supply chain does exists because demand and supply are uncertain. Averages delete uncertainty, which is precisely the information you needed. A supply chain that ran on averages would need no inventory, no safety stock and no planners at all.

Two practical notes. Where a calculation matters, this course explains what it is doing and what it assumes, and leaves the arithmetic to the companion handbook — because a formula memorised without its assumptions is how analysts produce numbers that are precisely wrong. And where the field disagrees with itself, or where a widely taught technique does not survive contact with a real business, this says so. There is a lot of confident teaching in supply chain that falls apart on a Tuesday afternoon.

Part I

The job

Chapter 01

What a supply chain analyst really does

A supply chain is the whole route a physical thing takes from where its materials come out of the ground to where somebody uses it, together with all the information and money that move alongside. It covers buying, making, storing, moving and returning. Anyone who touches any part of that is in supply chain; the analyst is the person who measures it and says what to do differently.

In practice the job has three recurring shapes, and most roles are some mixture of them.

The first is reporting: producing the regular numbers that tell the business what happened. Service levels, stock positions, freight spend, supplier performance. This is the least interesting part and it is where most junior analysts spend most of their time, which is worth knowing before you take the job. It is also where trust is built — if your weekly numbers are reliable, people believe you when you say something surprising.

The second is investigation: something has gone wrong and nobody knows why. Stock of one product has quietly tripled. A region's on-time delivery has fallen for three weeks. Freight cost per unit has risen while volumes were flat. Somebody has to find the cause, and finding causes in a supply chain is unusually hard because the cause is almost always several steps upstream of the symptom and several weeks earlier in time.

The third is decision support: a choice has to be made and the analysis decides it. Should we hold this item in the regional warehouse or ship it direct? Should we accept the supplier's discount for ordering twice as much? Should we move this route from air to sea? These are the questions where an analyst is worth a salary many times over, and they all have the same structure — an obvious saving in one place, and a less obvious cost somewhere else that you have to go and find.

What the job is not

It is not mainly forecasting, despite what the courses suggest. Most analysts touch the forecast rarely and inherit it constantly.

It is not mainly optimisation software. Large companies have such tools, most businesses do not, and the analyst's value in either case is in knowing what to ask the tool and whether to believe it.

And it is not a desk job in the way that a finance analyst's is. The numbers in your report correspond to pallets in a building somewhere, and the fastest way to become good is to go and look at the building.

Chapter 02

The triangle, and why you can never win all three

This is the single most important idea in the course, and once you have it you will see it in every meeting for the rest of your career.

Every supply chain is trying to do three things at once. It is trying to give customers what they want when they want it — call that service. It is trying to do that without spending too much on buying, making, storing and moving — call that cost. And it is trying not to have too much money tied up in goods sitting still, because stock is cash that has been converted into boxes — call that cash.

The three are in tension, permanently and structurally. It is not a failure of management that they conflict; it is the nature of the problem.

Suppose you want to improve service. The easiest way is to hold more stock, so that you never run out. That improves service immediately — and it consumes cash, and it adds cost in storage, handling, insurance and the goods that eventually expire or become obsolete. Suppose instead you want to cut cost. The easiest way is to order in larger quantities less often, because freight and handling are cheaper per unit that way. That does cut cost — and it raises average stock, which consumes cash. Suppose you want to release cash. Cut stock. That works — and your service falls the first time demand surprises you, which it will.

The sentence that makes you useful

Whenever anyone proposes a supply chain improvement, ask: "and what does that cost us in the other two?"

An analyst who reports we reduced inventory by twenty per cent has reported one third of a result. The complete version is we reduced inventory by twenty per cent, service fell from 97 to 94 per cent, and expedited freight rose by this much. Only then can anyone judge whether it was a good idea.

This single habit will distinguish you from most people in the room, and it costs nothing but discipline.

There is a fourth consideration that has become unavoidable and does not fit neatly into the triangle: risk. A supply chain optimised hard against all three corners tends to be extremely fragile, because every buffer that made it resilient was an inefficiency that somebody removed. The decade of disruptions that began in 2020 taught a great many companies this at enormous expense, and the fashionable response — holding more stock everywhere — is mostly just paying for resilience with cash, which is the triangle again wearing a new hat.

The honest formulation is that there is no optimum, only a chosen position. A business selling critical medical supplies should sit near the service corner and pay for it. A business selling commodity goods on price should sit near the cost corner and accept that it will occasionally run out. Much of the confusion in supply chain meetings comes from people arguing about a decision when what they actually disagree about is where on the triangle the company ought to sit — and nobody has said so out loud.

Chapter 03

Planner, analyst, buyer, engineer — and what separates them

The job titles in this field are used inconsistently between companies, which makes them a poor guide to what you would actually do. The useful distinction is not the title but the time horizon and the decision rights of the role.

RoleHorizonWhat they actually own
Demand plannerWeeks to 18 monthsThe forecast. Argues with sales about what will be sold, and is judged on error and bias rather than on being right.
Supply / inventory plannerDays to monthsWhat to order and when. Owns the stock position and, usually, the service level.
Supply chain analystAnythingNo operational decisions of their own. Measures, investigates, and recommends. Influence without authority is the defining feature of the role.
Buyer / procurementMonths to yearsSuppliers, contracts and prices. Often measured on savings, which as Chapter 21 explains is a dangerous measure on its own.

Two observations about this table matter for your career. The first is that the analyst role is the only one with no operational authority, which has a consequence people underestimate: your entire output is persuasion. A planner who is right acts. An analyst who is right has to convince somebody else to act, and a correct analysis that nobody acts on has accomplished nothing. Part VII is mostly about this.

The second is that the analyst role is unusually good preparation for the others, because it is the only one that sees the whole chain. A planner learns planning very deeply. An analyst learns a little about everything and, crucially, learns where the seams are — and almost all the money in a supply chain is lost at the seams between functions rather than inside them.

Chapter 04

Who you work for, and what they actually want

The people who ask you for numbers do not want numbers. They want a decision made easier, an argument settled, or a risk removed from their name. Understanding what each function is actually being measured on tells you what they will do with your analysis, and it explains conflicts that otherwise look like personality clashes.

FunctionMeasured onSo they will push you toward
SalesRevenue, sometimes volumeMore stock, faster delivery, and forecasts that are optimistic — because a forecast is also a target they will be held to.
FinanceMargin, working capitalLess stock, cheaper freight, and a suspicion of any number that cannot be reconciled to the ledger.
OperationsThroughput, cost per unit, safetyStable, predictable volumes — and a deep dislike of last-minute changes, which are expensive in ways that do not show up in anyone else's numbers.
ProcurementSavings against a baselineLarger order quantities and longer contracts, both of which look like savings and both of which consume cash and flexibility.
Customer serviceComplaints, response timeVisibility. They mostly want to know where the order is, and are the first to learn that something has gone wrong.
ExecutivesWhatever this year's strategy saysOne number and one recommendation. They will not read your appendix.

Read that table again with the triangle in mind and the pattern jumps out: sales sits at the service corner, finance sits at the cash corner, operations and procurement sit at the cost corner. They are not being difficult. Each is optimising the thing they are paid to optimise, and the supply chain is where those three objectives are forced to meet.

This is why the analyst's position is genuinely valuable, and why it is uncomfortable. You are the only person in the conversation whose measure is the whole triangle. When you tell sales that their service demand costs four million in stock, and tell finance that their stock target will cost eleven million in lost sales, you are not being unhelpful — you are doing the only part of the job that nobody else is positioned to do.

The trap for a new analyst

You will be asked for a number that supports a position somebody has already taken. It will be phrased helpfully — can you pull the figures showing that the new carrier is cheaper — and the phrasing has already decided the answer.

The move is not to refuse. It is to answer the question you were asked and the one you were not: here is the freight comparison, and here is what happened to transit time and damage rate over the same period. You have not blocked anyone. You have made it impossible to present a third of the picture without knowing that is what they are doing.

Part II

The chain itself

Chapter 05

The chain end to end, in one pass

Before any of the specialised parts make sense, you need the whole route in your head. Here it is, following a single physical item forwards.

It begins with raw materials — something extracted, grown or synthesised. Those go to a supplier who turns them into a component, and often to a second and third supplier beyond that, because most real chains are several layers deep. The layer that sells to you is called tier one; the layer that sells to them is tier two, and so on. Most companies have excellent visibility of tier one and almost none beyond it, which is why disruptions are so often a surprise: the factory that stopped was three layers away and nobody knew it existed.

Components arrive at a manufacturing or assembly site, where they become a finished product. That product moves to a warehouse — often several in sequence, a central one feeding regional ones — and then to the customer, either directly, or through a retailer, or through a distributor. And a small but expensive fraction of it comes back, which is reverse logistics, the part everyone forgets when designing the network.

Alongside the goods, two other things move, and they are the analyst's real subject.

Information moves in the opposite direction. A customer's order travels back up the chain as a signal to replenish, which becomes a supplier's order, which becomes a raw material order. The quality and speed of that signal determines almost everything about how well the chain performs — which is the subject of Chapter 08.

Money moves in the opposite direction too, and on a different clock. You pay your supplier on one schedule and get paid by your customer on another, and the gap between those two is working capital. A supply chain that is physically excellent can still bankrupt a company if the cash timing is wrong.

Two words you will hear constantly

Upstream means toward the suppliers and raw materials. Downstream means toward the customer. The convention is a river, and it holds: goods flow downstream, information and money flow upstream.

And push versus pull: a push chain makes things based on a forecast and hopes they sell; a pull chain waits for an actual order. Nearly every real chain is both, and the point where it switches — the decoupling point — is one of the most consequential design decisions a business makes. Push it closer to the customer and you get speed and more stock; pull it further back and you get less stock and longer waits.

Chapter 06

Lead time, and why it is the most important number in the building

Lead time is how long something takes from the moment you ask for it to the moment it is available to use. That sentence hides more than it reveals, and getting precise about it is one of the fastest ways to become useful.

The lead time that matters is almost never the one the supplier quotes. A supplier quotes manufacturing lead time — how long after they start until it is ready. What you actually wait is longer, and it includes the time your own purchase order sat in someone's queue before being sent, the supplier's queue before they started, the transit, the customs clearance, and the time the goods sat in your own receiving dock before anyone put them away and made them available to sell. A supplier quoting three weeks can easily represent nine weeks of real replenishment lead time, and the difference is entirely inside processes that nobody measured because each one felt small.

Lead time governs the chain for a reason that is worth stating carefully: you have to commit to a quantity before you know the demand, and the amount you have to guess about is exactly one lead time's worth. A chain with a two-day lead time barely has to forecast at all. A chain with a six-month lead time is making an enormous bet on a forecast that far out, and the forecast at that horizon is very poor. This is why the single most powerful thing you can do to a supply chain is not forecast better — it is make the lead time shorter, because a shorter lead time makes the forecasting problem easier rather than solving it harder.

The lever nobody pulls

Given a choice between a project that improves forecast accuracy by five per cent and a project that cuts replenishment lead time from eight weeks to five, take the lead time every time. Shorter lead time reduces safety stock, reduces the forecast horizon you have to be right about, increases flexibility, and improves service — it is the rare intervention that moves all three corners of the triangle in the same direction.

It is also unglamorous, because the weeks usually come from administrative delay rather than from anything technical. Finding them means walking the process and asking, at every handover, how long does it sit here?

The other thing to know about lead time is that its variability matters at least as much as its length, and often more. A supplier who reliably takes thirty days is much easier to plan around than one who averages twenty days but ranges from five to forty. You can plan for consistency. You cannot plan for a range, so you buffer against it — with stock, which costs cash. This is the first appearance of the idea that dominates the next chapter.

Chapter 07

Variability, and why the average is the enemy

This is the analytical spine of the whole subject. If you retain one chapter, retain this one.

Imagine a product that sells exactly one hundred units every single day, without deviation, and a supplier who delivers in exactly ten days, without deviation. How much safety stock do you need? None. You order a thousand units whenever you drop to a thousand, they arrive exactly as you run out, and you never hold a unit more than you need. Service is one hundred per cent, inventory is minimal, cost is minimal. The triangle dissolves.

That supply chain does not exist. Real demand is one hundred on average but eighty on Monday and one hundred and forty on Friday and three hundred the week of a promotion. Real lead time is ten days on average but occasionally eighteen because the vessel was late. Every buffer in every supply chain — every unit of safety stock, every extra day of lead time quoted, every spare truck — exists because of variability, and for no other reason.

This explains why average-based analysis is so dangerous in this field. If you plan against average demand over average lead time, you have designed a system that will be short roughly half the time. That is not a subtle error at the margins; it is a system that fails every other cycle.

Where averages will lie to you specifically

Average demand hides the peak. A warehouse sized for average volume cannot handle December. Capacity is always a question about the peak, never the mean.

Average lead time hides the late deliveries, and the late ones are the entire reason you hold safety stock.

Average service level hides the pattern of failure. Ninety-five per cent service achieved as a steady trickle of small misses is a completely different business problem from ninety-five per cent achieved by being perfect for eleven months and catastrophic in one.

Average inventory hides the shape. The same average stock can be one item hugely overstocked and everything else empty, which is the single most common real inventory problem and is invisible in the headline number.

The practical habit that follows is simple and you should adopt it now: whenever you are handed an average, ask for the spread. Not because the spread is always needed, but because the question itself will repeatedly reveal that the average was concealing the thing you were being asked about. In supply chain, the interesting information is almost always in the variation rather than in the central tendency — and the polished management report will almost always show you only the central tendency.

There is one more property of variability worth carrying. It compounds along a chain but not proportionally: combining several variable things produces something less variable than the sum of their variations, which is why pooling works. Holding one central stock for five regions needs less total inventory than five regional stocks for the same service, because the regions' surprises partly cancel each other. This is called the pooling or portfolio effect, it is one of the genuinely reliable results in the field, and it is the argument behind nearly every network centralisation project you will ever see. The counter-argument is that centralising adds lead time and freight to the customer — which is, once again, the triangle.

Chapter 08

The bullwhip effect, and why it is partly your fault

Here is an observation that has been reproduced everywhere it has been looked for. If you measure how much demand varies at each stage of a chain — at the shop, at the distributor, at the manufacturer, at the component supplier — the variation gets larger the further upstream you go. Consumer demand wobbles a few per cent; the orders the manufacturer receives swing wildly; the raw material supplier sees something close to chaos. The same underlying demand, amplified at every handover.

This is the bullwhip effect, and it is the most famous phenomenon in supply chain for a good reason: it is expensive, it is universal, and every cause of it is a rational decision taken by someone doing their job properly.

There are four causes, and they are worth knowing individually because each has a different remedy.

Demand signal processing. Each stage forecasts from the orders it receives rather than from real consumer demand. When orders rise, each stage adjusts its forecast up, then orders enough to cover the higher forecast and to rebuild its safety stock for the higher level. The adjustment is amplified at every step. Nobody has done anything wrong; the structure produces the amplification.

Order batching. Nobody orders daily, because ordering has a cost and freight is cheaper in full loads. So a steady trickle of consumption becomes a lumpy series of large orders, and what the supplier sees is a sequence of spikes and silences that looks nothing like the smooth demand underneath.

Price fluctuation. Promotions and quantity discounts cause customers to buy far more than they need, then buy nothing for a while. The supplier sees a huge order followed by a drought, and has no way of knowing that the underlying consumption never changed. Forward buying against a price rise does the same thing.

Shortage gaming. When supply is tight and orders are being rationed proportionally, the rational move is to order more than you need so that your share is bigger. Everyone does this, demand appears to explode, capacity is built to meet phantom demand, and when the shortage ends the orders evaporate — often leaving the supplier with capacity nobody wants. This is the mechanism behind several famous industry-wide boom and bust cycles.

What actually reduces it

Share real demand upstream. If the manufacturer can see actual consumer sales rather than only the orders they receive, the first cause largely disappears. This is the logic behind vendor-managed inventory and collaborative planning arrangements.

Order more often in smaller quantities, which requires making ordering and freight cheaper per event rather than just accepting the batch.

Stabilise pricing — every-day-low-price strategies exist partly for this reason, not only for marketing.

Allocate on past sales rather than on current orders during shortages, which removes the incentive to inflate.

For you as an analyst, the bullwhip matters in two concrete ways. First, it means that when you see wild demand variation on an item, your first question should be whether it is real consumer variation or an artefact of how orders are placed — because the remedies are completely different. Second, it means that your own company's ordering behaviour is somebody else's demand problem. The order pattern you generate upstream is as much a part of your supply chain performance as the forecast you receive, and almost nobody measures it.

Part III

Demand

Chapter 09

What a forecast is for, which is not to be right

Every course on this subject opens by teaching forecasting techniques, and almost every one of them skips the question that should come first. What is the forecast for?

It is not for being right. It will not be right. A forecast is a statement about a future that has not happened, and the only certainty in the field is the one everybody quotes and few act on: all forecasts are wrong. The useful half of that sentence is the part usually left off — they are wrong, so the question is not how to make them right but how to build a supply chain that works despite them being wrong.

A forecast exists to let you commit resources before you know what you need. That is all. Because lead times are longer than customers are willing to wait, somebody has to decide how much to buy, make or position before the demand arrives. The forecast is that decision's input. Which leads to the principle that ought to govern every forecasting discussion and rarely does:

The test for a forecasting improvement

A more accurate forecast is worth nothing unless a decision changes because of it.

If your replenishment happens weekly in fixed quantities, improving the daily forecast changes nothing. If your supplier's minimum order is three months of demand, forecasting accurately at one month changes nothing. Forecast at the granularity and horizon of the decisions that consume it, and no finer. An enormous amount of analytical effort in this field is spent producing precision that no decision can use.

There is a second consequence worth holding on to. Because forecasts are always wrong, every supply chain needs a mechanism for being wrong safely — buffers, flexibility, short lead times, the ability to change a plan late. A business that responds to forecast error by demanding better forecasts is attacking a problem it cannot win. A business that responds by becoming more responsive is attacking the one it can.

Chapter 10

How forecasts are made, without the mathematics

Almost every statistical forecast decomposes demand history into three things and projects them forward. Understanding the three is enough to have an intelligent conversation with anyone, including someone defending a very expensive piece of software.

The level is roughly where demand sits — the underlying magnitude, ignoring wobble. The trend is whether that level is drifting up or down over time. The seasonality is the repeating pattern that recurs at a fixed period — higher in December, lower in August, higher on Fridays. Whatever remains after those three is the residual: the part that was not predictable, which is the error you will be measuring in the next chapter.

The methods differ mostly in how they weigh the past. A simple average treats every historical period equally, which is fine for stable demand and terrible when anything changes. A moving average looks only at recent periods, which adapts faster but throws information away. Exponential smoothing — the workhorse of the field — weighs recent periods more heavily than old ones, on a smooth decay, and can be extended to handle trend and seasonality as well. Regression relates demand to something else that drives it, such as price or weather or a marketing spend, which is powerful when you genuinely know the driver and misleading when you have merely found a correlation. Machine learning methods do something similar at greater scale and with far less ability to explain themselves.

The honest state of forecasting technology

Extensive competitions comparing methods on thousands of real series have repeatedly produced an uncomfortable result: simple methods are surprisingly hard to beat, complex methods win by modest margins when they win at all, and combining several simple methods often beats any single sophisticated one.

This should make you sceptical of any vendor promising transformational accuracy gains, and it should also redirect your attention. The gains available from fixing the process — cleaning the history, removing the effect of past stockouts, agreeing whose number it is — are almost always larger than the gains available from changing the algorithm.

One technical point deserves a plain-language explanation because it causes real errors. Demand history is not sales history. What your system records is what you sold, and what you sold was capped by what you had. If you were out of stock for a week, the history shows low sales that week, the forecast learns from it, and you order less next time — which makes you run out again. This is a genuine self-reinforcing failure loop, it is extremely common, and correcting for it is called demand unconstraining. Any forecast trained on unconstrained sales data will systematically under-forecast exactly the items that sell best.

Chapter 11

Measuring forecast error, and the trap in every metric

You cannot improve what you do not measure, and forecast measurement is unusually full of traps. Two ideas do most of the work.

Error is how far off you were, regardless of direction. Bias is whether you were consistently off in the same direction. These are completely different problems with completely different causes, and reporting only one of them is the most common measurement failure in demand planning.

A forecast can have large error and no bias: you are wildly wrong every month but equally often high and low, so the errors cancel. That is a precision problem — you need a better model or you have genuinely unpredictable demand. A forecast can have small error and persistent bias: you are only slightly wrong, but always in the same direction. That is an organisational problem, and it is almost never fixed by statistics. Persistent over-forecasting usually means sales targets are being entered as forecasts. Persistent under-forecasting usually means somebody is being punished for excess stock and has learned to protect themselves.

Why bias matters more than accuracy in practice

An unbiased forecast with high error costs you safety stock. A biased forecast costs you the same safety stock and then adds a systematic surplus or shortage on top of it.

And bias is usually cheaper to fix, because it is nearly always a human incentive rather than a mathematical limitation. Look at bias first. Always. If the forecast has been high for eleven of the last twelve months, that is not bad luck and no model change will fix it.

Two further measurement points that will save you arguments. First, error measured as a percentage behaves badly on small numbers — being two units wrong on a forecast of one is a two hundred per cent error, which will dominate any average and tell you nothing useful. Any percentage-based accuracy measure will make your intermittent, low-volume items look catastrophic and your high-volume items look excellent, regardless of how well anyone is actually forecasting. Weight by volume or by value if you want a number that reflects the business.

Second, and more important: always compare the forecast against a naive one. The naive forecast is the simplest possible guess — next period will be the same as this one, or the same as this period last year. If your sophisticated forecast is not beating that, you have an expensive process producing no value, and this happens far more often than anyone admits. The formal version of this comparison is called forecast value added, and running it across each step of your forecasting process — the statistical output, then the planner's adjustment, then the sales override — frequently reveals that one of the human steps is making the forecast worse. That finding is uncomfortable to deliver and extremely valuable.

Chapter 12

The hard cases

Standard forecasting assumes a reasonable history of reasonably regular demand. Much of a real catalogue does not look like that, and knowing which hard case you are in prevents you applying a method that cannot work.

New products have no history at all. Nothing statistical can help; the forecast comes from analogy to a similar product's launch curve, from the sales plan, or from a structured judgement process. The important discipline is to plan the correction in advance: agree before launch that the forecast will be revised at four and eight weeks against actuals, because the launch forecast will be wrong and the only question is how fast you notice.

Promotions break every model, because the pattern they create is caused by a decision rather than by the market. The uplift is usually large, partly borrowed from future periods — customers stock up rather than consume more — and specific to the mechanic. The practical approach is to forecast the baseline and the uplift separately, and to keep a record of past promotions with their mechanics so that the next one has an analogue. Failing to strip promotional periods out of history before fitting a model is one of the most common technical errors in the field, because the model then expects a spike that is not coming.

Intermittent demand — items that sell occasionally, with long gaps — defeats ordinary methods entirely, because an average of a series that is mostly zero is a number that never occurs. Spare parts are the classic case. Specialised methods exist which forecast the size of a demand and the interval between demands separately, and they are better than nothing, but the honest position is that these items are genuinely hard and are usually better managed by a service-level policy than by a forecast.

Long lead time with short selling season is the worst combination and it is the fashion industry's permanent condition. You must commit before you know anything and cannot replenish once you learn. The answer is not better forecasting but structural: postpone the commitment as late as possible, keep some capacity in reserve, or design the product so the final differentiation happens late.

The classification that should come first

Before forecasting anything, sort the catalogue by how predictable it is, not by how much it sells. High-volume regular items deserve a statistical forecast and automation. Intermittent items deserve a policy rather than a forecast. New and promotional items deserve human attention and a planned revision.

Trying to run one process across all of them is why demand planning teams are permanently overloaded while their accuracy does not improve.

Part IV

Inventory

Chapter 13

Why inventory exists at all

Inventory is cash converted into objects. It is expensive to hold — storage, handling, insurance, financing, obsolescence, damage, and the return you could have earned on the money — and the total of those is usually estimated at somewhere between fifteen and thirty per cent of the value of the stock per year, which is a much larger number than most people expect.

Given that, the question is why any rational business holds any. There are exactly five reasons, and every unit of stock in every warehouse is there for one of them. Being able to say which is the fastest diagnostic in inventory analysis.

Cycle stock exists because you order in batches rather than continuously. If you use a hundred a day and order a thousand at a time, you carry an average of five hundred simply as a consequence of the order size. It is the direct price of ordering infrequently.

Safety stock exists because demand and lead time are variable. This is the buffer against the uncertainty from Chapter 07, and it is the subject of Chapter 15.

Pipeline or transit stock is what is in motion — on a vessel, in a truck, between sites. You own it, it is on your balance sheet, and it is doing nothing but travelling. It is proportional to lead time, which is another reason shorter lead times release cash.

Anticipation stock is built deliberately ahead of a known future event: a seasonal peak, a planned shutdown, an announced price rise, a strike.

Dead stock is the residue — obsolete, damaged, superseded, or simply bought in error years ago and quietly carried ever since. It is not there for a reason; it is there because nobody has been made to deal with it.

The analysis that will make your reputation

Take the total inventory value and split it into those five categories. Almost no company can do this on demand, and the result is almost always surprising.

The usual finding is that the amount everyone argues about — safety stock — is a modest fraction, while cycle stock driven by supplier minimum order quantities and dead stock nobody has written off together account for far more. Which means the stock reduction project aimed at safety stock is attacking the wrong pile, and will damage service to release money that was sitting untouched somewhere else.

Chapter 14

How much to order, and the truth about EOQ

The order quantity question is a genuine trade-off with a clean shape. Order in large quantities and you place fewer orders — less administrative effort, better freight rates, possibly a volume discount — but you carry more average stock. Order in small quantities and you carry less stock but pay the ordering and freight cost more often. Somewhere between is a total cost minimum.

The classical answer to this is the economic order quantity, which balances the cost of placing an order against the cost of holding stock and identifies the quantity where their sum is lowest. It is over a century old, it is taught in every course, and it is worth understanding for two reasons: the shape of the answer is genuinely useful, and the assumptions it makes are a checklist of everything that is wrong with tidy supply chain theory.

Those assumptions are that demand is known and constant, that lead time is fixed, that the unit price does not change with quantity, that there is no minimum order, that capacity is unlimited, and that ordering cost is a known fixed number. In a real business essentially none of these hold. Demand varies, suppliers impose minimums and offer discounts, warehouse space is finite, and almost nobody can tell you what it actually costs to place a purchase order.

What to take from it anyway

The most useful property of the classical answer is that the total cost curve is very flat near its minimum. Being thirty or forty per cent away from the theoretically optimal quantity costs you only a few per cent in total cost.

That is genuinely liberating, and it is the practical lesson: do not agonise over the exact order quantity. Get into the right region, then round to something operationally sensible — a full pallet, a full container, a week's demand. The operational convenience is worth more than the theoretical optimum, and the theory says you are allowed.

Where order quantity decisions genuinely go wrong is not in the arithmetic but in the incentives. Volume discounts and supplier minimum order quantities routinely push order sizes far above anything justifiable, because the discount is visible and immediate and lands in procurement's savings number, while the resulting inventory is invisible, gradual, and lands on someone else's balance sheet. When you find a product with wildly excessive stock, look at the purchase order history before you look at the forecast: the cause is very often a single decision to take a discount.

Chapter 15

Safety stock, service level, and what "95 per cent" actually promises

Safety stock is the buffer you hold against being wrong. Its size depends on three things and nothing else: how variable demand is, how variable and how long the lead time is, and how certain you want to be of not running out.

The first two come from the business. The third is a choice — and it is the choice people make carelessly, because the number involved sounds far more reassuring than it is.

The most misunderstood number in supply chain

Setting a ninety-five per cent service level in a replenishment system does not mean you will satisfy ninety-five per cent of demand. In the standard formulation it means that in ninety-five per cent of replenishment cycles you will not run out before the delivery arrives. That is a statement about cycles, not about units.

The distinction matters enormously, because a stockout that lasts two days near the end of a cycle may cost you a very small share of demand. The two measures — cycle service level and the proportion of demand actually met, which is called fill rate — can differ by a great deal, and fill rate is nearly always the one the business thinks you promised.

When somebody says "we run at 95 per cent service", your first question is which of those two do you mean, and which does the system actually target? They are frequently not the same and nobody has noticed.

The second thing to understand about safety stock is its shape as you push service upward, because it governs every conversation about targets. Moving from eighty to ninety per cent costs a certain amount of stock. Moving from ninety to ninety-five costs more than that. Moving from ninety-five to ninety-nine costs dramatically more, and the last fraction of a per cent costs more than everything before it combined. The cost of service rises steeply and without limit as you approach certainty, because you are buying protection against rarer and rarer events.

This has a direct and useful consequence. A blanket service target across a whole catalogue is always wrong. It over-serves the cheap, fast-moving, easily-replaced items where high service is nearly free, and under-serves the critical items where it matters. Service levels should be set by item, according to how much the stockout costs and how much the stock costs, which is what Chapter 17 is about.

The third thing is what actually drives the number, and it surprises people: lead time variability usually matters more than demand variability. Because you are exposed for the whole lead time, a supplier whose delivery date swings by a week forces far more safety stock than a modest wobble in daily demand does. Which means the cheapest route to less inventory is frequently not a better forecast at all — it is a conversation with the supplier about consistency, or a change of supplier. That conversation costs nothing and releases cash, and it is very rarely the project anyone proposes.

Chapter 16

A replenishment policy answers two questions: when do we order and how much. There are only a few structures in common use, and the choice between them is more consequential than most teams realise.

Re-parameterising an existing policy is unglamorous, requires no new technology, and routinely releases more cash than any project anyone is currently proposing.

Chapter 17

Segmentation, and where to spend your attention

A real catalogue has thousands of items and you cannot think carefully about all of them. Segmentation is how you decide where thought is worth spending, and the crude version of it is genuinely powerful.

The classic is ABC analysis: rank items by annual value — unit cost times annual volume — and split them into groups. The top group, typically around a fifth of the items, usually accounts for something like three-quarters or more of the value. The bottom group is a very large number of items worth very little in total. The observation that a small minority of causes accounts for the majority of effects is one of the most reliable patterns in business, and inventory obeys it consistently.

The action that follows is straightforward. The A items get attention: individual review, tight control, careful forecasting, frequent counting. The C items get automation and generous buffers, because it is cheaper to hold six months of a cheap item than to spend a planner's time on it every week. The most common inventory management failure is the opposite of this — a team spending equal effort per item, which means the valuable items get too little attention and the trivial ones get far too much.

Value alone is not enough, though, and the improvement is worth knowing. A two-dimensional segmentation is far more useful: value on one axis, predictability on the other. An item can be high-value and steady, high-value and erratic, low-value and steady, or low-value and erratic, and those four quadrants call for genuinely different treatment.

Predictable demandErratic demand
High valueAutomate and keep buffers thin. Forecast statistically. The best case — most of your value, easy to manage.Where your planners should live. High value, hard to predict, expensive to get wrong. Individual attention, and consider whether responsiveness beats forecasting here.

One further dimension is worth adding when it applies: criticality. A cheap, erratic item can still stop a production line or an operating theatre, and its stockout cost has no relationship to its purchase price. Any segmentation driven purely by value will systematically under-protect exactly those items, which is why spare parts inventories managed on an ABC basis fail in a characteristic and expensive way.

Part V

Moving and buying

Chapter 18

Inside the warehouse

A warehouse does five things in sequence, and the proportions of effort between them are not what outsiders expect. Goods are received and checked. They are put away into a location. They are picked when an order needs them. They are packed. They are shipped. Some also do value-adding work — kitting, labelling, light assembly — and all of them handle returns, which is the least designed-for and most expensive activity in the building.

The number worth carrying: picking is typically the majority of warehouse labour cost, and most of picking is walking. Not lifting, not scanning, not packing — walking between locations. Which means that most warehouse productivity improvement is, at bottom, an exercise in reducing distance travelled, and that is a much more tractable problem than it first appears.

The main lever on distance is slotting: deciding where each item is stored. Put the fastest-moving items closest to where pickers start and end, put slow items at the back and high up, and keep items that are frequently ordered together near each other. Slotting decays — what moved fast last year does not this year — and a slotting review is one of the highest-return, lowest-cost analyses a supply chain analyst can run. All you need is order line history and a location map.

The other levers are about how picking is organised. Batch picking collects items for several orders in one trip. Zone picking gives each picker a region and passes orders between them. Wave picking releases work in scheduled groups to match dispatch times. Each trades throughput against complexity and each suits a different order profile — many small orders favour batching, few large orders do not.

The measures that matter, and the one that misleads

Useful: lines picked per hour · order accuracy · dock-to-stock time, which is how long goods sit between arriving and being available to sell — often surprisingly long, and it is pure hidden lead time · and inventory record accuracy, which is the percentage of locations where the system and reality agree.

That last one underpins everything. If record accuracy is poor, every downstream calculation is fiction — your replenishment triggers on numbers that are wrong, your service failures have no explanation, and no amount of planning sophistication will help. Check it before you analyse anything else.

Misleading: storage utilisation. A warehouse filled to ninety-five per cent of capacity is not efficient, it is congested — there is nowhere to put arrivals, everything requires double handling, and productivity collapses. High utilisation is a warning, not an achievement.

Chapter 19

Transport, and what freight actually costs

The four modes trade speed against cost in a predictable order, and the ranking is stable enough to memorise. Sea is the cheapest per unit and the slowest, measured in weeks, and carries the overwhelming majority of world trade by volume. Rail is cheap, moderately slow, and constrained by where the tracks are. Road is the flexible middle and the only mode that reaches almost every door. Air is by far the fastest and by far the most expensive per unit, typically by an order of magnitude or more, which is why it is reserved for high-value, urgent, or perishable goods — and for covering up planning failures, which is a use worth measuring separately.

Freight pricing has a logic that analysts often miss. You pay for whichever is greater: the weight or the space. A container of feathers and a container of bricks cost differently to move, and carriers resolve this with a volumetric or dimensional weight — a formula converting size into a chargeable weight. The practical consequence is that for bulky, light goods, packaging design is a freight cost decision, and reducing box size can cut transport spend more than renegotiating a rate.

The second structural feature is the step change between a full load and a part load. A full truck or container has a simple price. A part load is priced on a scale that is substantially more expensive per unit, because the carrier has to consolidate it with other people's freight. This produces a threshold effect that drives real behaviour: it is frequently cheaper to ship a full container of slightly more than you need than a part container of exactly what you need — which is the order quantity trade-off from Chapter 14 arriving from a different direction.

The cost that hides from the freight report

Expedited freight is a symptom, not a cost line. When air freight appears on a route that normally goes by sea, something failed upstream — a forecast, a supplier, a decision made too late.

Most companies report freight spend in total and never separate the planned from the emergency. Splitting that out, and attributing each instance to its root cause, is one of the most revealing analyses available to you. The finding is usually that a small number of recurring upstream problems generate most of the premium spend, and that fixing them is cheap compared to the freight.

Chapter 20

Incoterms, customs, and the paperwork that stops the truck

When goods cross a border, two things decide who is responsible for what: the commercial terms agreed in the contract, and the customs rules of the countries involved. Analysts frequently ignore both and then cannot explain their landed costs.

Incoterms are a standard set of three-letter terms defining, for any shipment, exactly where the seller's responsibility ends and the buyer's begins. They settle three separate questions — who pays for transport, who bears the risk if it is damaged, and who handles the export and import formalities — and those three do not all switch at the same point, which is where confusion comes from.

You do not need to memorise all of them. You need to know the shape: at one extreme, the seller does nothing but make the goods available at their own premises and everything else is yours. At the other, the seller delivers to your door with duties paid. Everything else is a point along that line, and the common ones cluster around the port — the seller gets the goods to the vessel, and you take over from there. The analytical point is simple: a quoted unit price means nothing until you know the Incoterm, because two suppliers quoting the same price on different terms are quoting substantially different real costs.

Customs adds duty, which depends on what the goods are and where they were made. What they are is determined by a classification code from an internationally standardised system, and the code drives the duty rate. Where they were made is the country of origin, which is not necessarily where they were shipped from and has specific legal rules about how much processing confers origin. Getting either wrong is expensive — retrospectively, with penalties.

The number that should be on your report is landed cost: the unit price plus freight plus insurance plus duty plus handling plus any brokerage, divided by the units. It is the only comparable figure between suppliers in different countries, and it is remarkable how often sourcing decisions are made on unit price alone. A cheaper supplier further away with a higher duty rate is a routine way to increase total cost while reporting a saving.

Chapter 21

Procurement, and total cost of ownership

Procurement exists to secure supply at an acceptable total cost and an acceptable risk. Note that purchase price appears nowhere in that sentence as a goal in itself, which is the source of most of the friction between procurement and everyone else.

The concept that repairs this is total cost of ownership: the full cost of using a supplier over the life of the relationship, not the price on the invoice. It includes the landed cost from the previous chapter, and then everything that follows from the choice — the inventory you must hold because their lead time is long or unreliable, the quality failures and the cost of handling them, the administrative effort, the payment terms and what they do to your working capital, the switching cost if it goes wrong, and the risk exposure if they are a single source.

The analysis procurement most needs from you

A supplier offering a five per cent discount for double the order quantity is offering a visible, immediate, easily-reported saving. Your job is to price the invisible side: the extra inventory that will sit for months, the cash it consumes, the obsolescence risk, and the warehouse space.

Frequently the discount is genuinely worth taking. Frequently it is not, and nobody has ever calculated it — because the saving lands in procurement's number and the cost lands in finance's, and no single person was accountable for the sum. Being the person who produces that sum is one of the clearest ways an analyst demonstrates value.

This is also why measuring procurement on savings alone is structurally dangerous. Savings are measured against a baseline that the person being measured often helps define; they reward one-off price reductions over relationship quality; and they create an incentive to buy more than needed, to sign longer than sensible, and to select on price where reliability was what mattered. None of this means savings should not be measured — it means savings should be reported next to inventory, service and quality, which is the triangle again.

Chapter 22

Suppliers: performance and risk

Supplier performance measurement is usually done badly in a specific and correctable way. The common measure is on-time delivery against the date the supplier confirmed — and suppliers manage that number by confirming dates they know they can hit, which is to say by quoting longer lead times. A supplier can achieve excellent on-time performance while being slow and unhelpful, and the metric will applaud them.

The better set is three measures reported together: on-time against the date you originally requested, not the date they later confirmed; in-full, because a delivery missing a third of the quantity is not a delivery; and quality at receipt. Combining the first two gives the measure most businesses use, on time in full — and it is a strict measure, because an order is a failure if it misses on either count. Newcomers are often shocked by how low real OTIF figures are, which is a sign that the measure is working rather than that it is unfair.

Supply risk deserves its own treatment because the last few years made the case expensively. The exposures worth mapping are single-source dependency, where only one supplier can provide something; geographic concentration, where several suppliers are different companies in the same region and therefore fail together; financial fragility, where a supplier may simply not survive; and hidden tier-two concentration, where your carefully diversified suppliers all buy from the same place upstream and you do not know it.

How to think about resilience without romance

Resilience is bought, and the triangle sets the price. Dual sourcing costs volume leverage and management effort. Holding strategic stock costs cash. Regional supply usually costs unit price. Excess capacity costs utilisation.

So the useful question is never "are we resilient" but "which specific failures are we buying protection against, and what is each one costing us per year?" That converts an anxious conversation into a decision a business can actually take.

Chapter 23

The network, and why it is usually the biggest lever

Network design is the question of how many facilities you have, where they are, and which customers and products each one serves. It gets less attention than forecasting or inventory because it changes rarely, and it deserves more, because the network sets the ceiling on everything else. No amount of planning excellence will overcome a warehouse in the wrong place.

The fundamental tension is between centralisation and proximity, and both sides have real arithmetic behind them. Fewer, larger facilities mean less total inventory, because of the pooling effect from Chapter 07, and lower fixed and overhead cost. More, smaller facilities mean shorter delivery distances to customers, faster service, and lower outbound freight. Inbound freight generally favours centralisation, outbound freight generally favours dispersion, and inventory strongly favours centralisation.

Which means the network answer depends almost entirely on the ratio between the value density of your goods and the cost of moving them. Small, valuable items — pharmaceuticals, electronics — centralise well, because the inventory saving dominates and shipping them is cheap relative to their value. Bulky, cheap items — bottled water, building materials — must be produced and stored near their market, because freight dominates everything.

Two structures worth knowing by name. Cross-docking means goods arrive and are transferred straight to outbound vehicles without being stored — it removes inventory and handling entirely and demands very tight coordination, so it suits predictable, high-volume flows and fails badly on erratic ones. And postponement means deliberately holding product in a generic form and doing the final differentiation — packaging, labelling, configuration, language — as late as possible, ideally after the order is known. This is one of the most elegant ideas in the field, because it lets you pool inventory across variants you would otherwise have to forecast separately.

Part VI

Doing the analysis

Chapter 24

Where the data comes from, and why it is wrong

Supply chain data is worse than data in most other functions, and it is worth knowing why before you are surprised by it. The reason is that much of it is generated by people doing physical work under time pressure, and the system record is a by-product of that work rather than its purpose.

The sources you will meet are the ERP, which holds orders, stock and master data and is the system of record; the warehouse management system, which holds locations, movements and picking activity at a much finer grain; the transport system or the carriers' own portals, which hold shipments and events; and a large, undocumented population of spreadsheets holding the things the systems could not do. That last category is not a failure of discipline — it is where the business does its actual thinking, and ignoring it will make you wrong.

The characteristic errors are specific enough to check for directly.

The five that will catch you

Master data drift. Weights, dimensions, lead times and costs were entered once at item creation and never revised. Lead times in particular are frequently the number someone typed in years ago, not the one the supplier currently achieves. Check a sample against reality before you trust any calculation that uses them.

Timestamps that mean different things. "Delivered" may mean the carrier scanned it, the goods physically arrived, someone signed for it, or someone booked it into the system the following morning. Lead time calculations built on mismatched definitions are wrong by exactly the size of the administrative delay.

Sales recorded as demand. Chapter 10's problem. What you did not have, you did not sell, and the record shows low demand rather than a stockout.

Units that change. Eaches, cases, pallets, layers — the same item measured four ways in four systems, with conversion factors that are themselves master data and themselves wrong. This produces errors by factors of twelve and twenty-four, which is at least easy to spot.

Cancellations and returns handled inconsistently. Whether an order that was placed and cancelled counts as demand determines your accuracy figures, and different reports in the same company will answer it differently.

The habit that follows is the same one a data analyst learns: before analysing anything, establish what one row of your table represents. One order line, one order, one shipment, one delivery, one item per day. Joining an order-level table to a line-level table and then summing a freight cost will multiply that cost by the number of lines, and the resulting number will be confidently, silently wrong. In supply chain this is the most common source of incorrect analysis, and it is entirely preventable by counting rows before and after every join.

Chapter 25

The five analyses you will run again and again

Almost everything asked of a supply chain analyst is one of five shapes. Recognising which one you have been handed tells you what the answer will look like before you start.

The comparison. Is this better or worse than that — this month against last, this region against another, this supplier against the alternative. The trap is that almost nothing is comparable without normalising for something: volume, mix, seasonality, working days. A region with worse cost per unit may simply ship smaller orders. Before comparing, ask what differs between the two things other than the thing you are measuring.

The decomposition. A total moved, and you must say what moved it. Freight cost rose — was it volume, rate, mix of modes, distance, or fuel? This is the most common investigative request and the most satisfying to answer well, because the honest version quantifies each contribution rather than naming the first plausible cause. The discipline is to make the parts sum back to the whole.

The trade-off. Two options, each better on a different axis. This is where the triangle does its work, and the output is not a recommendation but a priced comparison: option A costs this much more and delivers this much better service, at this cash position. Give the decision to the decision-maker with the price attached, rather than hiding your own weighting inside a single recommended answer.

The exception hunt. Find the items, suppliers, routes or customers that are behaving abnormally, so that scarce attention goes where it is needed. Almost all useful supply chain reporting is of this kind rather than the summary kind, and the difficulty is defining "abnormal" in a way that produces a short enough list to act on.

The sizing. How big is this problem, roughly, and is it worth pursuing? Done in an hour, with generous assumptions stated out loud. This is the most undervalued analysis of the five: a rough number that stops a three-month project aimed at a small prize is worth far more than the precise answer nobody needed.

The habit that makes all five better

Estimate the answer before you calculate it. Write down what you expect, with a rough magnitude, before touching the data.

Two things follow. When the result matches, your confidence is earned rather than assumed. When it does not, you have either found something genuinely interesting or made an error — and you will investigate, which is exactly what you would not have done if you had no expectation. Analysts who skip this step ship wrong numbers, because a wrong number with no prior expectation looks exactly like a right one.

Chapter 27

Cost to serve, and the customers who lose you money

Companies know their gross margin by product with reasonable accuracy. Very few know what it actually costs to serve a given customer, and the gap between those two is where a lot of quietly destroyed profit lives.

Two customers buying identical products at identical prices can cost wildly different amounts to serve. One orders monthly in full pallets to a single location, on standard lead time, and pays on time. The other orders three times a week in broken cases to eleven locations, changes the order after it is picked, requires a specific delivery window, returns a tenth of what arrives, and disputes invoices. The second customer consumes many times more supply chain resource, and in a conventional margin report they look the same.

Cost to serve analysis attributes the real activity cost to each customer, channel or order type: picking effort by the number of lines and the pick type, delivery by distance and drop size, returns by their actual rate, and the administrative overhead of exceptions. It is not a precise science and it does not need to be — the finding is usually so stark that precision is irrelevant.

What this analysis almost always finds

A distribution in which a minority of customers or order types generate a disproportionate share of profit, a large middle contributes modestly, and a tail of customers is served at a genuine loss — often including some of the largest by revenue, because size buys price concessions and size brings complexity.

The response is rarely to fire them. It is to change the terms: a minimum order quantity, a delivery schedule instead of on-demand, a charge for small drops, a different channel. Most unprofitable customers become profitable with a structural change neither party had thought to discuss.

The same logic applies to products and to order types, and running it on the long tail of a catalogue is frequently uncomfortable. A slow-moving item with acceptable margin can still be destroying value once you count the space it occupies, the counting effort, the obsolescence and the picking inefficiency of a location that is visited twice a year. This is the analysis behind most rational range rationalisation, and it is one of the few places where an analyst can identify money that nobody else in the business can see.

Chapter 28

How you will fool yourself

The errors below are not hypothetical. Each is common, each survives review, and each produces a number that looks entirely reasonable.

Averaging an average. Taking the mean of twelve monthly service percentages gives a different and wrong answer compared to computing service across the whole year, whenever the months have different volumes. The same applies to averaging accuracy across items of different sizes. Whenever you average a ratio, ask whether it should be weighted — the answer is almost always yes.

Comparing periods with different working days. A month with twenty-two working days against one with nineteen will show a volume rise that is entirely calendar. Easter and Ramadan move between periods from year to year. Normalise per working day before concluding anything about a trend.

Reading a mix change as a performance change. Cost per unit fell — because the mix shifted toward larger, cheaper-to-handle items, not because anyone improved. This is the single most common false conclusion in logistics reporting, and the defence is to recompute the current period using the previous period's mix and see how much of the movement survives.

Trusting the system lead time. Master data, which as Chapter 24 explains is often stale. Calculate lead time from actual receipt dates against actual order dates before you use it in any inventory model.

Assuming the cause is upstream of where you noticed it. Service failures get blamed on the forecast because the forecast is the visible upstream thing. Frequently the cause is a purchase order raised late, a supplier who quietly changed their schedule, or stock that exists in the system and not in the building. Trace the actual instances before theorising about the system.

Solving the measured problem rather than the real one. Excess inventory is the symptom people report. The cause may be a minimum order quantity, a discount taken two years ago, a forecast bias, a dead range nobody has written off, or a service target set too high for that item. Those have five different remedies and the inventory number cannot distinguish between them.

The professional habit worth building now

Before you send any number, write one sentence saying what would have to be true for it to be wrong.

Not a hedge in the email — a note to yourself. It forces you to name the assumption you have made about grain, about definitions, about which demand rate you divided by. About half the time you will go back and check something, and a meaningful fraction of those checks will find an error before somebody else does. That habit alone separates analysts people trust from analysts people verify.

Part VII

Working, and growing

Chapter 29

S&OP, and the meeting where it comes together

Sales and operations planning is the monthly cycle in which a company reconciles what it intends to sell with what it is able to supply, and commits to one plan. Where it works, it is the most important process in the business. Where it does not, it is an expensive meeting that produces slides.

The standard cycle has five stages and they run in order. Data gathering assembles actuals and refreshes the statistical forecast. Demand review settles on one unconstrained demand plan — what we believe we would sell if supply were unlimited — with sales, marketing and planning arguing it out. Supply review tests that plan against capacity, materials and labour, and identifies where it cannot be met. Pre-meeting reconciliation resolves what can be resolved and identifies the gaps and options that genuinely need a decision. Executive meeting takes those decisions and commits.

Three things distinguish an S&OP that works. It produces one number that everybody then plans against, rather than sales, finance and operations each keeping their own. It looks forward — a cycle spent explaining last month is a review meeting, not a planning one. And decisions are actually taken and recorded, which requires someone senior enough to take them in the room.

Your role in it, as an analyst

You will very likely own some of the preparation, which is a better position than it sounds. The person who prepares the pack shapes what gets discussed.

Use that carefully: bring the gaps and the options, not the history. A pack that says here is what happened produces a meeting about blame. A pack that says here are the three places where demand and supply do not meet, and here is what each resolution would cost in service, cost and cash produces a meeting about decisions — and it is the triangle doing the work again.

Chapter 30

Writing the answer, and defending it

Your analysis is worth exactly as much as the action it causes, which makes the communication half of the job rather than an afterthought to it.

The structure that works is answer first. State the conclusion in the first sentence, then the two or three reasons, then the detail for those who want it. This feels wrong when you have spent three days on the analysis and want to show the journey, and it is what every senior reader needs, because they are deciding whether to keep reading.

Four things belong in any recommendation and one of them is usually missing. The conclusion, stated as a decision rather than an observation. The size — how much money, how much service, how many units, because "significant" is not a quantity. The confidence — what you assumed, what you could not check, how wrong this could be. And the trade-off, which is the one that gets left out: what this costs in the other corners of the triangle.

On defending it, expect four kinds of challenge and prepare for each. The number is wrong — answerable only by knowing your data lineage, so know where every figure came from. That is not what causes it — answerable by having tested the alternative explanations before you arrived. We tried that before — answerable by finding out, in advance, whether they did and what happened. And it is not feasible — which is frequently correct and is the reason to have talked to the operation before recommending anything about it.

The sentence that keeps you credible

"I do not know, and here is how I would find out."

Analysts damage themselves by guessing under pressure in meetings. The guess is remembered, it is often wrong, and the next analysis is doubted. Saying you do not know costs nothing in a culture worth working in, and it makes your confident statements mean something.

Chapter 31

Your first ninety days

In the first month, do not build anything. Go and look at the physical operation — the warehouse, the dock, the picking face — and spend a day with whoever places the orders. A supply chain analyst who has not seen the goods handled will misinterpret the data for years, and the credit you earn by having been in the building is disproportionate and permanent.

Alongside that, learn three things. What the company sells and to whom, in enough detail to know which products matter. Where the data lives and which report is the one people actually believe, which is rarely the official one. And who decides what — the real map, not the organisation chart.

In the second month, reproduce an existing report from source data and make it match. This is unglamorous and it is the best possible investment: you will discover the definitions, the exceptions, the filters everyone forgot, and the two places where the official number is quietly wrong. You will also earn the right to be believed later, because you will be able to say precisely how your figure relates to the one they already trust.

In the third month, deliver one small, complete, useful thing. Not the transformation project — something a person asked for, delivered fully, on time, with the trade-off named. Reputation in this role is built from a sequence of small reliable deliveries, and the analyst who spends six months building something ambitious before showing anything has usually built the wrong thing.

Chapter 32

Getting hired, and what they actually test

Supply chain analyst interviews test four things, and candidates usually prepare for only the first.

Technical competence. Can you handle data — spreadsheets certainly, SQL increasingly, a dashboard tool often. Expect an exercise with a messy dataset, and expect it to contain a deliberate trap: duplicated rows, a mixed unit of measure, a date format problem, or a join that changes the grain. Noticing the trap matters more than finishing the exercise.

Domain reasoning. Can you think about a supply chain problem out loud? "Inventory has risen twenty per cent while sales are flat — what would you look at?" There is no correct answer; they are watching whether you structure the problem, whether you ask about grain and definitions before theorising, and whether you name the triangle without being prompted.

Estimation. Rough arithmetic about magnitudes, out loud, without a calculator. How many pallets in a truck, roughly. Whether a saving is worth pursuing. This tests whether you have a feel for the physical reality behind the numbers, and it is the part that most distinguishes people who have been in a warehouse from people who have not.

Communication. Can you explain a finding to someone who does not want the detail? This is often assessed informally through how you answer everything else.

If you are coming from adjacent experience

Operational supply chain experience is an asset that candidates routinely undersell, and the fix is mechanical: put numbers on it.

"Managed supplier relationships" says nothing. "Managed forty suppliers covering sixty per cent of inbound volume; cut supplier lead time variability from a fourteen-day range to five, releasing roughly three weeks of safety stock" says everything — and it demonstrates, in one sentence, that you think in the terms this handbook has been teaching.

Every role you have held has quantities attached: how many items, how many suppliers, what value, what improvement, over what period. Go and excavate them. A CV describing years of real supply chain work without a single number in it is being read as junior.

Glossary

ABC analysis
Ranking items by annual value to decide where management attention goes. Best combined with a predictability axis.
Backorder
Demand accepted but not yet fulfilled because stock was unavailable.
Bullwhip effect
The amplification of demand variability as you move upstream. Four causes, all rational.
Cash-to-cash cycle
Days between paying for materials and being paid by customers.
Cost to serve
The real activity cost of serving a customer, channel or order type.
Cross-docking
Transferring goods from inbound to outbound without storing them.
Cycle stock
Inventory that exists purely because you order in batches.
Decoupling point
Where a chain switches from forecast-driven to order-driven.
Dependent demand
Demand calculated from a plan rather than forecast. Components of a scheduled build.
EOQ
Economic order quantity. The order size minimising ordering plus holding cost, under assumptions that rarely hold. Useful mainly because its cost curve is flat.
Fill rate
The proportion of demand satisfied from stock. Not the same as cycle service level.
Incoterms
Standard terms defining where the seller's responsibility for cost, risk and formalities ends.
Landed cost
Unit price plus freight, insurance, duty and handling. The only comparable figure across sources.
Lead time
Time from requesting something to having it available. Almost always longer than the supplier's quote.
MOQ
Minimum order quantity. A frequent hidden cause of excess inventory.
MRP
Material requirements planning. Calculating component needs from a production schedule.
OTIF
On time in full. Strict: an order fails if it misses on either count.
Pooling effect
Combining variable demands needs less total buffer than holding them separately. The argument for centralisation.
Postponement
Holding generic product and differentiating it as late as possible.
Reorder point
The stock level that triggers replenishment. Covers demand over lead time plus safety stock.
Safety stock
Buffer held against variability in demand and lead time. Driven more by lead time variability than most people expect.
S&OP
Sales and operations planning. The monthly cycle reconciling demand and supply into one committed plan.
SKU
Stock keeping unit. One distinct item as the business counts it.
TCO
Total cost of ownership. Everything a supplier choice costs, not just the invoice.
Unconstraining
Correcting sales history for periods when you were out of stock, so demand is not underestimated.
VMI
Vendor-managed inventory. The supplier sees your consumption and replenishes you. Reduces bullwhip.
