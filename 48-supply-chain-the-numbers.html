Handbook 48 · the companion to 47 · still no code

The numbers, and what each one hides. Every calculation a supply chain analyst runs, explained before it is computed.

Handbook 47 taught the concepts. This one takes each calculation those concepts lead to and explains what it is doing, what it assumes, and where it breaks. There is still no algebra here, because a formula memorised without its assumptions is exactly how analysts produce numbers that are precisely wrong — and in this field, the assumptions are almost always the interesting part.

Before you begin

How to use this

This is a reference rather than a course. Read it once end to end so you know what is here, then come back to individual entries when you need them. Each one follows the same shape: what the number is trying to say, how it is arrived at in words, what it assumes, and the specific way it will mislead you.

Two principles run through every entry and are worth stating once rather than eighteen times.

The two rules for every number in this handbook

Every calculation has a grain and a period. Per what, and over what window. Almost every dispute about a supply chain number turns out to be two people computing it at different grains or over different periods, and neither has said so. Write both down before you compute anything.

Every improvement has a counterparty. If a number moved favourably, something moved unfavourably somewhere — the triangle from Handbook 47, Chapter 02. A calculation reported without its counterparty is an incomplete piece of work, however correct the arithmetic.

Part I

Demand

Calculation 01

Average demand, and which average you want

What it does. Summarises how much is consumed per period, which is the input to nearly everything downstream — reorder points, safety stock, days of supply, capacity.

How it is arrived at. Total demand over a window, divided by the number of periods in that window. The difficulty is entirely in choosing the window and deciding what counts as demand.

What it assumes. That the window is representative of the period you are planning for. On a seasonal item this is straightforwardly false: an annual average is wrong in every single month of the year, being too high in the trough and too low in the peak. On a growing or declining item, an average of the last twelve months is systematically behind reality.

Where it will mislead you

The window. Too short and you are reacting to noise; too long and you are planning for a business that no longer exists. There is no correct length, only a choice you should state.

Demand versus sales. If you were out of stock, your sales understate demand — and every number built on this average inherits the understatement. Correct the history for stockout periods, or accept that you are planning to repeat them.

Working days. A monthly average across months with nineteen and twenty-three working days is comparing unlike things. For anything operational, compute per working day and multiply back up.

Returns and cancellations. Decide explicitly whether they are subtracted, and be consistent, because different reports in the same company routinely differ on this and it is a common source of two people having "the same number" that does not match.

Calculation 02

Variability, expressed as one number

What it does. Puts a size on how much demand bounces around its average — which, as Handbook 47 Chapter 07 argues, is the quantity that actually drives your buffers.

How it is arrived at. The standard measure is the standard deviation: roughly, the typical distance between an individual period's demand and the average. A small standard deviation means demand clusters tightly around its mean; a large one means it scatters widely.

On its own that number is hard to interpret, because whether a deviation of forty units is large depends entirely on whether average demand is fifty or five thousand. The fix is the coefficient of variation — the standard deviation expressed as a proportion of the average — which makes variability comparable across items of completely different sizes. This single derived number is the most useful segmentation variable most analysts never compute.

How to read it in practice

As a rough working guide: a coefficient of variation well below a half indicates demand that is regular enough to forecast statistically with confidence. Around one, demand is erratic and forecasting effort delivers little. Well above one, you are usually looking at intermittent demand, where the average is a number that never actually occurs and a policy beats a forecast.

Plotting value on one axis and coefficient of variation on the other produces the segmentation from Handbook 47 Chapter 17 directly, and takes about twenty minutes from order history.

What it assumes and where it breaks. The standard deviation describes a spread around a stable centre. If demand has a trend or a season, much of what it measures is not randomness at all but structure you could have predicted — and using it to set safety stock then buffers you against your own laziness rather than against genuine uncertainty. Remove trend and seasonality first, then measure the variability of what is left. That residual is the real uncertainty, and it is usually considerably smaller than the raw figure, which means the naive calculation systematically over-buffers seasonal items.

It also assumes the period you measure over matches the period you are exposed for. Variability of weekly demand is not four times the variability of daily demand — variation grows more slowly than that when periods are combined, which is the pooling effect again. Getting this wrong is one of the commonest errors in safety stock calculation, and it always errs toward too much stock.

Calculation 03

Forecast error

What it does. Measures how far the forecast was from what happened, so you can tell whether a change to the process helped.

How it is arrived at. Take the difference between forecast and actual for each period, remove the sign so that overs and unders do not cancel, and summarise. The two common summaries are the mean absolute error, which reports the typical miss in units, and the mean absolute percentage error, which reports it as a proportion of actual demand.

What each hides. The unit-based measure is honest but not comparable across items of different sizes — a miss of fifty units means something different on a product selling sixty and one selling sixty thousand. The percentage measure is comparable and badly behaved at the bottom end: if actual demand was one and you forecast three, that is a two hundred per cent error, and a handful of such items will dominate any average you compute across a catalogue. It is also mathematically asymmetric — it punishes over-forecasting more heavily than under-forecasting, which creates a quiet incentive to forecast low.

The three decisions that change the answer more than the metric does

At what level do you measure? Accuracy always looks better the more you aggregate. National monthly accuracy can be excellent while the store-week accuracy that actually drives replenishment is hopeless. Measure at the level the decision is taken.

At what horizon? A forecast made one week ahead and one made six months ahead are different products. Fix the horizon to the one your lead time forces you to commit at, and report it.

Weighted by what? An unweighted average across a catalogue gives a trivial item the same influence as your biggest seller. Weight by volume or by value if you want a number that reflects the business rather than the length of the item list.

Because of those problems, many practitioners report weighted absolute percentage error — total absolute miss across all items divided by total actual demand — which behaves sensibly, cannot explode on small numbers, and automatically weights by size. If you are choosing one measure for a business, that is usually the least bad choice.

Calculation 04

Bias, and forecast value added

What bias does. Detects whether you are wrong in a consistent direction, which is a completely different problem from being wrong by a lot.

How it is arrived at. Identical to error, except that you keep the sign. Over-forecasts and under-forecasts cancel. If the result over many periods is near zero, the forecast is unbiased — it misses, but symmetrically. If it is persistently positive or negative, you have a systematic problem.

The only subtlety worth knowing is that a single period tells you nothing; bias is a statement about a run. A practical check is simply to count how many of the last twelve periods were over-forecasts. Six or seven is unremarkable. Ten or eleven is not luck, and no statistical model change will fix it — persistent bias is nearly always an incentive, not an algorithm.

Forecast value added — the most uncomfortable and most useful calculation here

What it does. Tests whether each step in your forecasting process makes the forecast better or worse.

How. Compute the error of a naive forecast — next period equals this period, or equals the same period last year. Then the error of the statistical forecast. Then the error after the planner's adjustment. Then after the sales override. Each stage either reduced error or increased it.

What it finds. Very frequently, that the statistical forecast beats naive by a modest margin and that one of the human adjustment steps makes it worse. This is well-documented across many companies, and it is the single highest-value thing a new analyst can compute — partly because of the finding, and partly because it converts an argument about whose forecast is better into a measurement.

Deliver it carefully. Someone's judgement is the subject.

Part II

Inventory

Calculation 05

Safety stock

What it does. Sizes the buffer that protects you against being wrong during the period you are exposed.

How it is arrived at, in words. Three ingredients multiply together. The first is how variable things are — demand variability and lead time variability combined. The second is how long you are exposed, which is the lead time, because that is the window during which you cannot react. The third is how certain you want to be, expressed as a service level, which converts into a multiplier: higher confidence means a bigger multiplier, and the multiplier rises steeply as you approach certainty.

That is the whole logic. More variability, longer exposure, or higher required confidence all increase the buffer, and the arithmetic in the textbook is just those three ideas made precise.

The five things that go wrong

Only demand variability is included. The commonest implementation error. Lead time variability usually contributes more than demand variability, so a calculation that omits it under-buffers exactly the items with unreliable suppliers.

The lead time is master data. Stale, as Handbook 47 Chapter 24 warns. Compute it from actual receipts against actual order dates.

Seasonality is counted as randomness. If you did not remove the season first, you are buffering against a pattern you could have predicted. This inflates safety stock on exactly the items where stock is most expensive.

The service level is uniform across the catalogue. A single target applied to everything over-serves cheap items and under-serves critical ones. Differentiate it.

Nobody recomputes it. Demand patterns, lead times and suppliers change; the parameter was set at implementation. Re-parameterising an existing system is the least glamorous and most reliably profitable inventory project there is.

And the assumption underneath all of it, which the textbook states quietly and practitioners forget: the standard calculation assumes demand variation follows a normal distribution — symmetric, with rare extremes. Real demand is frequently not like that. It is often skewed, with occasional very large orders, and for intermittent items it is nothing like normal at all. On those items the formula produces a number with a reassuring air of precision and no particular relationship to the risk. For genuinely lumpy demand, simulate or use a policy; do not trust the formula because it returned a value.

Calculation 06

The reorder point

What it does. Says at what stock level you should place the next order.

How it is arrived at. Two parts added together. The demand you expect to consume during the lead time — average demand per day multiplied by lead time in days — plus the safety stock from the previous entry. The first part covers the normal case; the second covers the surprise.

That is genuinely all there is to it, and the simplicity is worth noticing: the reorder point is not a sophisticated quantity, it is a question of whether you will run out before the replacement arrives.

The four adjustments real systems need

Use the right stock figure. The trigger should compare against available stock, which is what you physically have plus what is already on order minus what is already committed to customers. Triggering on physical stock alone causes duplicate ordering; triggering on physical plus on-order without subtracting commitments causes stockouts.

Match the units of time. Daily demand with lead time in weeks is a factor-of-seven error, and it happens regularly.

Include the review period if you do not have continuous visibility. If you only check on Mondays, your exposure is the lead time plus up to a week, and the buffer must cover both.

Recalculate seasonally. A reorder point computed on annual average demand will trigger far too late entering a peak and far too early leaving it.

Calculation 07

Order quantity

What it does. Says how much to order once the trigger fires, balancing the cost of ordering frequently against the cost of holding the resulting stock.

How it is arrived at. The classical economic order quantity finds the point where the annual cost of placing orders and the annual cost of holding the average resulting inventory are balanced. Order more and you hold more; order less and you order more often. The answer rises with annual demand and with ordering cost, and falls as holding cost rises.

What it assumes. Constant known demand, fixed lead time, no quantity discounts, no minimum order, unlimited space, and a known cost per order. In a real business, essentially none of these hold, and the last one is the quiet problem: almost nobody can tell you what it actually costs to place a purchase order, so the input is usually a guess and the output inherits it.

Why it is still worth computing

Because the total cost curve is flat near its minimum. Being thirty or forty per cent away from the theoretical answer costs only a few per cent in total cost.

So compute it, then ignore the precise figure and use it as a sanity range. If your current order quantity is four times the theoretical answer, something is driving that — a discount, a minimum, a container — and that driver is the thing to analyse. If it is within a factor of two, round it to a pallet or a container and move on. The flatness of the curve is permission to be practical.

Calculation 08

Turns, and days of supply

What they do. Both express how much inventory you hold relative to how fast you use it — one as a rate per year, the other as a duration. They are the same information in two units, and people argue about which to use for no good reason.

How they are arrived at. Inventory turns is annual cost of goods sold divided by average inventory value: how many times the stock is replaced in a year. Days of supply is current inventory divided by average daily demand: how many days the stock would last. Divide roughly three hundred and sixty-five by the turns and you have days of supply.

The four traps

Cost, not price. Turns must use cost of goods sold against inventory valued at cost. Mixing a revenue figure with a cost-valued inventory inflates turns by your entire gross margin and produces a number that looks excellent and means nothing.

Which average inventory. Year-end alone is the worst choice, because year-end is usually a deliberate low point. Use a monthly average at minimum.

Which demand rate for days of supply. Historic, forecast, or recent — on a seasonal item these give wildly different answers, and entering a peak the historic rate makes you look far better stocked than you are.

The aggregate hides the shape. This is the important one. A healthy overall turns figure routinely conceals a portfolio in which a few fast items turn beautifully and a long tail never moves at all. Always look at the distribution of days of supply across items, not the average — the picture is usually startling, and it is where the recoverable cash is.

Calculation 09

Inventory carrying cost

What it does. Puts a price on holding stock for a year, so that inventory decisions can be compared against other costs rather than argued about in the abstract.

How it is arrived at. Add up the annual cost of everything that follows from holding goods, and express it as a percentage of the inventory's value. The components are the cost of capital — the return that money could have earned elsewhere, which is usually the largest single element — plus storage, handling, insurance and tax, shrinkage and damage, and obsolescence.

The total commonly lands somewhere between fifteen and thirty per cent of inventory value per year. Most businesses use a single assumed figure, and most people outside supply chain are startled by how large it is.

Why the single company-wide rate is usually wrong

Obsolescence risk is the component that varies most, and it varies enormously by item. Holding a year of a stable industrial fastener carries a modest cost. Holding a year of a fashion garment or a consumer electronic carries a risk of the stock becoming nearly worthless, which dwarfs every other component.

Applying one blended rate across a catalogue therefore systematically under-prices holding the risky items and over-prices holding the stable ones — which pushes every downstream calculation, including order quantity and safety stock, in exactly the wrong direction on both. Differentiating the rate by product category is a small piece of work with a large effect.

Part III

Service

Calculation 10

The four service measures, and why they disagree

What they do. All four attempt to say how well you met demand. They give different answers on the same data, and almost every argument about service level in a business is two people using different ones without realising.

MeasureWhat it countsWhen it flatters you
Cycle service levelThe proportion of replenishment cycles in which you did not run out.Always. A brief stockout just before a delivery counts as a whole failed cycle even if almost all demand was met — but equally, it says nothing about how much demand was missed.
Fill rate, by unitThe proportion of units demanded that were supplied.When shortages hit small orders. Missing ten units out of ten thousand looks excellent, even if it meant a customer's order failed.
Fill rate, by lineThe proportion of order lines supplied complete.Less than the unit measure. A stricter and generally more honest view.
Order fill rateThe proportion of whole orders supplied complete.Never. This is the harshest measure and the closest to the customer's actual experience — one missing line fails the order.

On the same week's data, these four can differ by many percentage points, in a predictable order: the unit measure highest, the order measure lowest. A business reporting ninety-eight per cent on units may be delivering eighty-something per cent of complete orders, and the customers are describing the second number while the dashboard shows the first.

What to do about it

Report the one that matches how the customer experiences failure, which for most businesses is by line or by order rather than by unit. Then report the cycle service level separately if that is what your replenishment system targets — because those are two different things and the system is optimising one while the business is judged on the other.

And when you inherit a service number, your first question is always: which of these four, and over what population? Excluding backorders, or excluding items flagged as discontinued, or counting only stocked lines, changes the answer more than most improvement projects do.

Calculation 11

OTIF, computed honestly

What it does. Combines two conditions — was it on time, and was it complete — into a single pass-or-fail per order.

How it is arrived at. Count the orders that met both conditions; divide by all orders. Because it is an and, it is strictly lower than either component, and the arithmetic is unforgiving: if nine in ten are on time and nine in ten are complete, and the two failures are independent, only about eight in ten are both.

That multiplication is why newcomers find real OTIF figures shockingly low, and why the measure works — it prevents the two components being traded against each other quietly.

The four definition choices that decide your answer

On time against whose date? The date the customer requested, or the date you later confirmed. Measuring against the confirmed date lets anyone achieve excellent OTIF by quoting longer lead times, which is the single most common way this measure is gamed — usually without dishonest intent, simply because the confirmed date is the one in the system.

What is the tolerance? Is a delivery one day early on time? Most customers think so; some, with limited dock space, emphatically do not. Is one hour late a failure? The window you allow changes the number substantially.

What counts as in full? Exactly the quantity ordered, or within an agreed tolerance? For anything sold by weight, an exact-match rule produces a meaningless figure.

Measured at dispatch or at delivery? Dispatch is under your control and is what most companies actually measure. Delivery is what the customer experiences. The gap between them is the carrier's performance, and measuring at dispatch means you never see it.

None of those four has a universally correct answer. What matters is that the choices are written down, that they do not change quietly, and that the customer-facing measure uses the customer's definitions rather than the ones that are convenient to compute.

Part IV

Money

Calculation 12

Landed cost

What it does. Produces the real cost of getting one unit into your warehouse, so that suppliers in different countries on different terms can be compared at all.

How it is arrived at. Start with the unit price. Add the freight to your facility, insurance, customs duty, brokerage and clearance fees, any port or handling charges, and any inland transport. Divide the total by the number of units. Every one of those components is skipped somewhere.

The three things that make it wrong

The Incoterm was not checked. Two suppliers quoting the same unit price on different terms are quoting materially different costs, because the term decides who pays for which parts of the journey. A quote is not a number until you know its term.

Freight was allocated by unit count rather than by what drives it. A shipment carrying both dense and bulky items should allocate freight by volume or by chargeable weight, not by dividing equally per unit. Allocating per unit makes bulky items look cheap and dense items look expensive, which then distorts every sourcing and pricing decision downstream.

The exchange rate is a point in time. A landed cost computed at one rate and compared to one computed at another is not a comparison. State the rate and the date.

One genuine subtlety. Landed cost is per unit, so it depends on the shipment size — a part container carries much higher freight per unit than a full one. Which means the landed cost of a supplier depends on how you buy from them, not only on who they are. Comparing a supplier you buy from in full containers against one you buy from in part loads, without normalising, will tell you the wrong answer about the suppliers and the right answer about your own ordering.

Calculation 13

Total cost of ownership

What it does. Extends landed cost to everything that follows from choosing a supplier, over the life of the relationship — which is what you are actually deciding between.

How it is arrived at. Landed cost, plus the consequences. The inventory their lead time and minimum order force you to hold, priced at your carrying cost. The quality cost — defect rate multiplied by what a defect costs you to handle, which includes inspection, returns, rework and the service failures caused downstream. The administrative effort of dealing with them. The payment terms, which are genuinely worth money: thirty extra days of credit on a large spend is a real cash benefit and belongs in the comparison. And the risk, which resists precise pricing but can at least be named — single source, distant geography, financial fragility.

The comparison worth building once and reusing

A supplier five per cent cheaper per unit, with a lead time four weeks longer and a minimum order of three months' demand, is frequently more expensive in total — and the extra inventory alone often exceeds the discount, before touching risk or quality.

Build this comparison as a reusable template rather than a one-off. It is the single analysis that most reliably changes a sourcing decision, and its persuasive power comes from the fact that nobody disputes the components — they simply had never been added up by one person.

Calculation 14

Cost to serve

What it does. Attributes real supply chain activity cost to individual customers, channels, order types or products, so you can see who is actually profitable.

How it is arrived at. Identify the activities that consume cost — receiving, storing, picking, packing, delivering, handling returns, processing exceptions. For each, find the thing that drives its cost: picking is driven by lines and by pick type, delivery by drops and distance, storage by space occupied and time, returns by their actual rate. Then allocate each activity's cost to customers according to how much of that driver they consume.

What it assumes. That you can find a driver for each activity and get a rough cost per unit of driver. Both are approximations, and that is fine — the findings are usually so large that precision is beside the point. A customer costing three times the average to serve does not become profitable because your pick cost estimate was twenty per cent out.

The two ways this goes wrong

Allocating by revenue. This is the default in most finance systems and it destroys the entire purpose — it assumes cost is proportional to sales, which is precisely the assumption the analysis exists to test. Allocate by activity drivers or do not bother.

Chasing precision. Teams spend months refining driver rates and never publish. The useful version is built in two weeks with stated assumptions, and it is better to be roughly right and in the conversation than exactly right afterwards.

Calculation 15

Cash-to-cash cycle

What it does. Measures how many days your money is tied up between paying your suppliers and being paid by your customers. It is the cash corner of the triangle expressed as a single number, and it is the measure finance cares about most.

How it is arrived at. Three components. Days of inventory — how long stock sits before being sold. Plus days of receivables — how long customers take to pay after being invoiced. Minus days of payables — how long you take to pay suppliers. The first two tie money up; the third is money you are holding onto.

A shorter cycle is better, and a negative one — where you are paid before you pay — is the position that lets a business grow without financing, which is why certain retailers and platform businesses are so cash-generative.

The honest reading

Of the three levers, extending supplier payment terms is by far the easiest and by far the least real. It improves your number by moving the burden to your suppliers, who finance it and eventually price it back into your unit cost, or become financially fragile — which is a supply risk you have created.

Reducing days of inventory is the genuine improvement, and it is the hard one, because it means everything in Part II of this handbook. When cash-to-cash improves, always ask which of the three components moved. If it was payables, nothing has actually improved.

Part V

Operations

Calculation 16

Warehouse productivity

What it does. Measures how much work the building gets done per unit of labour, so that cost can be managed and capacity planned.

How it is arrived at. The standard measure is lines picked per labour hour, counting hours actually worked on picking rather than all hours paid. Variants count units or orders instead of lines, and the choice matters — units per hour flatters an operation picking full cases and punishes one picking singles, even though the second is harder work.

Why raw productivity comparisons are almost always invalid

Order profile dominates everything. An operation picking ten-line orders of full cases will show several times the lines-per-hour of one picking single-line orders of individual items, with identical management and identical effort. Comparing two sites, two periods, or two shifts without normalising for profile is the commonest mistake in warehouse analysis.

Normalise on mix — pick type, lines per order, units per line — before concluding anything. And when a site's productivity shifts, check the profile before you check the people; it has usually moved.

Two companion measures belong alongside it. Order accuracy, because productivity achieved by picking wrong is not productivity — the error returns as a return, a credit and a service failure. And inventory record accuracy, the proportion of locations where system and reality agree, which underpins every calculation in this handbook. If record accuracy is poor, your reorder points trigger on fiction and no amount of planning sophistication will help.

Calculation 17

Freight cost, per what

What it does. Expresses transport spend in a form that can be tracked and compared.

The difficulty is entirely in the denominator. Cost per unit shipped, per order, per kilogram, per cubic metre, per pallet, per drop, per kilometre — each tells a different story and each can move for reasons that have nothing to do with transport performance.

Per whatUseful forMoves for the wrong reason when
Per unitComparing against product margin.Product mix shifts toward larger or denser items.
Per kilogram or cubic metreComparing carrier rates.Rarely — this is the cleanest for rate comparison.
Per order or per dropUnderstanding cost to serve.Order size changes. A move to fewer, larger orders improves it without any efficiency gain.
As a percentage of salesExecutive reporting.Prices change, mix changes, or promotions run. This is the most quoted and least reliable freight measure there is.

The separation that matters more than the denominator: split planned freight from expedited freight, always. They are different phenomena. Planned freight is a rate and a volume question. Expedited freight is a failure indicator — something upstream went wrong and someone paid an order of magnitude more to recover it. Reporting them together hides the most actionable number in logistics, because expedited spend can be traced back to specific causes and those causes are usually cheap to fix relative to the premium.

Calculation 18

Supplier performance

What it does. Scores suppliers so that conversations with them are about evidence rather than impressions.

How it is arrived at. Three components, reported separately before being combined. On time — measured against your originally requested date, for the reason in Calculation 11. In full — the quantity delivered against the quantity ordered. Quality — the proportion accepted at receipt without defect.

Two further measures earn their place. Lead time variability, which as Handbook 47 Chapter 15 explains usually drives your safety stock more than demand does, and which no conventional scorecard includes. And responsiveness — how quickly they answer, how they handle a problem — which cannot be computed and should be recorded anyway, because it predicts what happens in a crisis better than any of the numbers.

The scorecard mistake worth avoiding

Combining everything into a single weighted score is how most supplier scorecards are built, and it destroys the information that makes the scorecard useful. A supplier with excellent quality and terrible reliability and one with the reverse can produce the same composite, and they require completely different conversations.

Report the components. Use the composite only for ranking a long list, never for deciding what to say to a supplier.

Reference

Sanity checks before you send anything

Six questions, in order. They take about five minutes and they will catch most of what goes wrong.

What is one row? Order, line, shipment, item-day. Then: did any join change it? Count rows before and after. This single check prevents the commonest serious error in supply chain analysis.

Does it reconcile? Compare your total against something the business already trusts — the finance figure, the official report. If it differs, understand why before presenting, because somebody will ask and "I don't know" at that moment costs you the whole analysis.

Did I use cost or price? And consistently on both sides of every ratio.

Are the periods comparable? Working days, seasonality, promotions, calendar shifts. Normalise per working day before claiming a trend.

Is this a mix effect? Recompute the current period using the previous period's mix. If most of the movement disappears, nothing improved — the composition changed, and that is a different and usually less interesting story.

What did it cost in the other two corners? Service, cost, cash. If you cannot answer, the analysis is not finished — and an unfinished analysis presented as a recommendation is how good analysts damage good supply chains.
